### Fundamentals corner

One concept, and I am building it off Episode 208's published title only — "Ownership Changes A Bloodline" — not off anything I heard him say.

When you buy a share of stock, the thing you own is a claim on what is left over. That phrase is the whole idea, and it is worth sitting with.

A company's cash gets paid out in a fixed order. Suppliers, employees, and lenders get paid first, and their claims are contractual — the company owes them a specific number on a specific date whether business is good or terrible. Shareholders are dead last in that line. There is no promised payment, no due date, no obligation. You get whatever remains after everyone with a contract has been satisfied. That is why equity is called the "residual claim."

Which sounds like the worst seat in the house, and in a bad year it is — in bankruptcy, shareholders routinely get nothing while lenders recover most of their money. But the trade runs both ways. A lender's upside is capped: lend at 7% and you make 7%, no matter how well the company does. The leftover claim has no ceiling. When a company earns far more than it owes, every dollar of that excess belongs to the owners. That asymmetry — last in line, but unlimited on the upside — is the entire reason equity has historically outrun bonds over long stretches. You are paid for standing at the back of the line.

Here is the part that connects to "bloodline," and the part most new investors miss: **your slice can get thinner without you selling anything.** Owning 100 shares means nothing on its own. What matters is 100 divided by the total shares that exist. When a company issues new shares — to pay employees, to fund an acquisition, to raise cash — the denominator grows and your ownership percentage shrinks. That is dilution. Conversely, when a company buys back its own stock, the denominator shrinks and your slice grows while you sit still.

So when you are researching any name on a future pick list, find the share count. In a company's quarterly or annual filing it is "shares outstanding," and the figure worth watching is the diluted count, which includes shares not yet issued but already promised. Pull it for this year and for five years ago. A company whose share count keeps climbing is quietly handing your ownership to someone else, and strong-looking total profits can hide it — the pie grew, but your slice of it did not. A company steadily shrinking its share count is doing the reverse. Same business, opposite direction for you as an owner.

That is the difference between owning a stock and owning a piece of a company. The number of shares you hold is what your brokerage shows you. The fraction of the business those shares represent is the thing that actually compounds — or does not.
