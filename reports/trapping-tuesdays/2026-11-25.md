### Fundamentals corner

Last night's tape hands us the single best teaching example of the year for the most-used number in fundamental analysis. So let me teach it properly.

**The price-to-earnings ratio, and why Nvidia's looks cheap.**

The P/E ratio is one division: the share price divided by the earnings per share. If a stock trades at $155 and earned $8 per share over the past year, its P/E is about 19. That is it.

What it *means* is the useful part. **P/E is the price you pay for one dollar of annual profit.** A P/E of 19 says you are paying $19 for each $1 the company earns per year. Said another way, if profits never changed and were all paid to you, it would take 19 years to get your money back. A high P/E means investors expect profits to grow a lot; a low P/E means they do not, or they see risk.

**Now the paradox.** Nvidia is the stock everyone calls an AI bubble. You would expect a bubble to carry a P/E of 60, 80, 100. Its trailing P/E is somewhere around **18 to 21.** By that single number it looks ordinary — cheaper than many utilities.

How? Look at the division. P/E has two parts, and almost everyone watches only the top one. Nvidia's *price* rose enormously, which pushes P/E up. But its *earnings* rose faster — revenue up about 103% in a year, with profits up alongside. When the denominator grows faster than the numerator, **the ratio falls even as the stock rises.** That is the whole trick, and once you see it you will never read a P/E the same way.

**Two flavors you must keep separate.** *Trailing* P/E uses the last twelve months of actual, reported earnings — real but backward-looking. *Forward* P/E uses analysts' estimates of next year's earnings — forward-looking but a guess. Nvidia's trailing figure sits near 18–21 while one forward figure was near 25. When trailing is *lower* than forward, the market is quietly expecting earnings to **fall**. That single comparison is a free read on expectations, and it costs you one subtraction.

**Three ways P/E lies to you, which this week demonstrates:**

*It cannot see durability.* A P/E of 19 on profits that arrive for twenty years is cheap. The same 19 on profits from a handful of customers in a spending boom that might cool is not. Nvidia's data-center revenue depends on a few hyperscale buyers; P/E has no way to express that.

*It cannot see what is holding the earnings up.* Open federal investigations, a reinstated chief executive, an assumption of zero China revenue — none of that appears anywhere in the arithmetic.

*It has no opinion about the price.* P/E fell as Nvidia rose and would have fallen further had earnings kept outpacing price. "Low P/E" is not "will go up." Tuesday proved that: nothing about the ratio stopped a 5.9% drop.

**How to actually use it.** Compare a company to *itself* over time and to its *direct competitors* — a chipmaker against chipmakers, not against a bank. Then ask the question the number cannot: *why* is it this level? Cheap because the market is wrong, or cheap because the market sees something you have not found yet? P/E is the beginning of the question. It is never the answer.

**One more concept this week handed us free: key-person risk.** A single named individual returning moved about $900 billion of market value in one session. That is not a quirk — it is a risk factor companies are required to disclose in their annual filings, and it means a meaningful part of what you own can depend on one person's presence. When you read a 10-K, the risk factors section is where that lives. It is the least-read and most honest part of the document.

### New: the captions are not ready when the job runs

This is the fault nobody has named, and it would survive every fix suggested so far.

Read the plist carefully. `StartCalendarInterval` with Weekday 3, Hour 0, Minute 0 fires at midnight **local wall-clock time on the Mac** — launchd uses local time, not Pacific. So the Trappin Tuesdays job fires at **midnight Wednesday**, roughly an hour or two after a Tuesday-evening show ends.

Now read what the script does at that moment. It asks `yt-dlp` for automatic captions, and if none come back it calls:

### The patch, tested

This makes the script wait for captions instead of losing the week. It retries nine times, ten minutes apart — about eighty minutes of patience — and still finishes well before your 2:30 reading time.

In `fetch_show_transcript.sh`, replace these four lines:

```
yt-dlp --skip-download --write-auto-subs --write-subs --sub-lang "en.*,en" \
  --output "$WORK/ep" "$URL" >>"$LOG" 2>&1 || true

VTT="$(ls "$WORK"/ep*.vtt 2>/dev/null | head -1 || true)"
[ -n "$VTT" ] || fail "no subtitles available for $VIDEO_ID (may still be processing)"
```

with this:

```
# YouTube builds automatic captions asynchronously, and for a three-hour
# livestream they are routinely not ready until hours after the broadcast ends.
# This job fires about an hour after the show, so the first attempt often finds
# nothing at all. Retry on a slow loop instead of losing the week.
SUB_TRIES="${SUB_TRIES:-9}"
SUB_WAIT="${SUB_WAIT:-600}"
VTT=""
for attempt in $(seq 1 "$SUB_TRIES"); do
  yt-dlp --skip-download --write-auto-subs --write-subs --sub-lang "en.*,en" \
    --output "$WORK/ep" "$URL" >>"$LOG" 2>&1 || true
  VTT="$(ls "$WORK"/ep*.vtt 2>/dev/null | head -1 || true)"
  [ -n "$VTT" ] && { log "subtitles found on attempt $attempt"; break; }
  if [ "$attempt" -lt "$SUB_TRIES" ]; then
    log "no subtitles yet for $VIDEO_ID (attempt $attempt of $SUB_TRIES); waiting ${SUB_WAIT}s"
    sleep "$SUB_WAIT"
  fi
done
[ -n "$VTT" ] || fail "no subtitles for $VIDEO_ID after $SUB_TRIES attempts over ~$(( (SUB_TRIES - 1) * SUB_WAIT / 60 )) minutes (still processing, or captions disabled)"
```

Then confirm it parses before deploying:

```

```
