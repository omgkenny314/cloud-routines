### Fundamentals corner

**How to read a competitor's factory announcement — and why supply, not demand, is what usually kills a good thesis.**

This week handed you a nearly perfect teaching case, so let us use it.

Think about what actually happened. Seagate did not miss its numbers. No customer cancelled. Demand for hard drives did not fall by a single unit. **Toshiba — a different company — said it intends to build more factory capacity.** And Seagate fell about 10 percent, bounced 5 percent, then fell about 8 percent again.

Why should that move a stock so violently? Because of where Seagate's profits were coming from.

**Start with the mechanism.** When something is scarce, the seller sets the price. Hard drives have been scarce, so Seagate could raise prices, and here is the crucial part: **the factories were already built.** The cost of making one more drive barely changed. So almost every extra dollar of price went straight to profit. That is why margins in a shortage expand so fast — you are not selling more things, you are selling the same things for more money.

Now run it backwards. If Toshiba doubles its output, drives stop being scarce. Seagate loses the ability to set the price. The same factories, the same costs, the same number of drives — but each one sells for less. **And because price was doing all the work on the way up, it does all the work on the way down.** Margins do not drift lower; they fall off a cliff. That asymmetry is why the market reacted so hard to an announcement about somebody else's buildings.

**Here is the habit I want you to take from this.** When you research a company whose profits depend on scarcity — memory chips, hard drives, oil, shipping, steel, fertilizer, cement — demand is the easy half, and it is the half everybody talks about. AI needs storage. That is true, obvious, and already in the price. The half that will actually determine your outcome is the half nobody puts in a headline:

**Who is building more, and when does it come online?**

So learn to ask four questions, in this order:

1. **Why is this profitable right now?** If the answer is "because the product is scarce," you are in a cyclical story, and the rest of these questions matter enormously.
2. **Who else can make this, and what are they doing?** Competitors' capital-spending plans, new plant announcements, expansion news. This is where Toshiba lived. It is public information, and most retail investors never look at it.
3. **How long does new capacity take to arrive?** A semiconductor fabrication plant takes years. A shipping vessel takes a couple of years. This is your clock — it tells you how long the good times can last.
4. **Is the company itself telling you the shortage will last?** Micron saying its 2027 supply is already contracted at higher prices is exactly this kind of evidence, and it is why Micron held flat on the day Seagate fell 8 percent. Contracts are harder to walk away from than optimism.

**And notice how differently that framework treats this week's news.** Toshiba's announcement is not a reason the AI storage thesis was wrong. It is the supply side finally responding to prices, which is the thing that always eventually happens and is always what ends these cycles. The question is not "was Trapper wrong." It is **"how far along are we?"** — and that question has a knowable answer you can research, which is far more useful than a verdict.

One honest closing note. Bernstein called the selloff a storm in a teacup; Morningstar's research director suggested AI-linked names could fall 20 to 30 percent first. **Both are published, credentialed opinions, and they directly contradict each other.** That is not a failure of your research — it is what the edge of a real question looks like. Nobody knows. What you can do is understand the mechanism well enough to form your own view and to recognize which piece of news would actually change your mind. That is the whole skill.
