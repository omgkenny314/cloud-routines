## The deeper dive

This section is education and context so you can do your own research. It is not advice to buy or sell anything.

### Nvidia (NVDA) — the $189 LEAP idea

What it does: designs the GPUs and networking that AI data centers are built on, plus the CUDA software layer that keeps customers locked in.

Where it trades: about $217 as of late August 2026, with the 200-day moving average sitting somewhere around $194–202 depending on the source. Up roughly 17% year to date, which matches what he said.

The problem with the trade as stated: his trigger is "if Nvidia goes to 189 at the 200-day." But $189 is now *below* the 200-day. For price and that average to meet at $189, the 200-day has to keep falling toward the price — which happens after months of weakness, not in a week. So this isn't a setup you can act on today; it's a conditional order that may never trigger, and if it does, the reason it triggered will matter as much as the level. He also didn't specify a strike, so "go out 12 months" is a direction, not a plan.

The catalyst you should know about: Nvidia reports fiscal Q2 results after the close on **August 26, 2026**, against guidance of roughly $91 billion in revenue. That's four days from now and it will move the stock hard in either direction. Any technical level drawn before that print is provisional.

On the Bank of America "50% discount" claim — that's one bank's model of fair value, not a fact about the stock. Treat it as a data point, not a measurement.

On his Nvidia stake numbers, he was accurate, which is worth saying. Nvidia's August 14 13F disclosed about $30 billion in Intel — genuinely about 47% of its disclosed equity portfolio, built from a $5 billion investment — and roughly $21 billion in SpaceX, about a third of the portfolio. CoreWeave around $4.7 billion, Nebius around $4.69 billion, Coherent around $3.07 billion, Nokia around $2.21 billion, Synopsys around $2.15 billion. He read those off correctly.

His "he never wants to be the monopoly" framing is his interpretation, not a stated Nvidia strategy. A simpler reading: Nvidia is investing in its own customers, which boosts their ability to buy Nvidia chips. That's a real dynamic worth understanding — and a real risk, because circular revenue relationships can flatter growth on the way up and unwind fast on the way down.

### Micron (MU) and SanDisk (SNDK) — the memory supercycle

What they do: Micron makes DRAM and NAND memory, including the high-bandwidth memory (HBM) that AI accelerators need. SanDisk, spun out of Western Digital, makes NAND flash storage.

Where they trade: Micron is around $940, down from a June high near $1,255 — it has technically entered a bear market off that peak, even though it's still up enormously. SanDisk closed around $1,600 on August 20, against a 52-week range of roughly $46 to $2,354 and an all-time high of $2,335 set on June 25, 2026.

Read that 52-week range again. SanDisk went from about $46 to about $2,354 inside a year and has since given back roughly a third from the high. That is an extraordinarily volatile security. Trapper's read that SanDisk "stayed strong" on a red day is accurate for that one day, and tells you very little about the next one.

The fundamentals genuinely are strong: SanDisk reported roughly $20.2 billion in fiscal 2026 revenue with profit margins reported around 77% on the back of a NAND shortage. Micron posted record revenue of $41.46 billion in its June quarter with guidance around $50 billion for the following quarter, and raised capex to roughly $20 billion to expand HBM capacity. UBS has a $1,625 target on Micron citing tight HBM supply and rising pricing.

The risk to understand, and Trapper didn't raise it: memory is the most cyclical business in semiconductors. Shortages produce enormous margins, enormous margins attract capacity, capacity produces gluts, and gluts destroy earnings. Every prior memory cycle has ended this way. The bull case is that AI demand makes this cycle different and longer. That may be right — but "this time the cycle is different" is the single most expensive sentence in investing, and the June-to-August drawdowns in both names are the market already testing it.

His thesis holds up on the facts as they stand. What he understated is how much of that is already in the price.

### Seagate (STX)

What it does: hard disk drives, increasingly the high-capacity "nearline" drives that cloud data centers use for bulk storage, now shipping with HAMR (heat-assisted magnetic recording) under the Mozaic brand, which raises capacity per drive.

Where it trades: around $847 on August 20, 2026. FY26 revenue grew 34% to $12.2 billion with EBIT margin around 24%, record free cash flow of $3.1 billion, $1.4 billion of debt paid down, and $810 million returned through dividends and buybacks. Management guided the next quarter to $7.10–$7.50 EPS on $4.0–$4.2 billion revenue, well ahead of estimates, and multiple banks raised targets above $1,000 after the August 14 print.

So the business is doing exactly what he thinks it's doing, and his conviction is supported. What the transcript shows is a different lesson: he was right about the company and still lost roughly $109,000 across two positions in one overnight session, because he was expressing that view through options with a short time horizon. Being right about a business and being right about a trade are separate problems. His own segment on stop losses being useless against gap-downs is the honest version of this.

### Nike (NKE) — his call was right, and it's worth understanding why

Where it trades: about $40, having just hit a 12-year low. He said "$100 then, $39 now," and he's essentially correct. That's a genuinely good call, publicly made in August 2023, and he's entitled to the victory lap.

The thesis held because it was about distribution and product, not sentiment. Under CEO Elliott Hill, who took over in late 2024, Nike is running a "Win Now" turnaround aimed at repairing the wholesale relationships the previous direct-to-consumer strategy damaged. Meanwhile Nike's share of the US performance running market fell to roughly 25%, with Hoka around 10% and On around 9% — together roughly 19% of the US premium running market. Deckers, which owns Hoka, trades around $92 and has held up far better than Nike, though Hoka's growth is slowing and Deckers is well off its own 52-week high of $125.

One part of his thesis is now out of date and you should know it: he said Nike "lost clothing to Lululemon." Lululemon is down about 43% in 2026, trading around $120 against a 52-week high of $226, with weak guidance and its own operational problems. Athleisure did take share from Nike — but the winner of that fight is not currently winning either. This is a good reminder that a thesis can be right about who lost without being right about who won.

His forward call — "I don't see it coming back" — is an opinion about a company mid-turnaround with a new CEO, not a fact. Twelve-year lows are where both value opportunities and value traps live, and nothing in the current data settles which one this is.

### Coca-Cola (KO)

Around $90.50, near an all-time high, against a 52-week range of roughly $65 to $92. P/E around 25x, dividend yield about 2.4%, and 64 consecutive years of dividend increases. His chart read — all-time high near $90–91, all three moving averages rising — was accurate.

### Eli Lilly (LLY)

Around $1,269, near the top of a 52-week range of $685 to $1,293. ### The neocloud complex — CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN, Applied Digital

CoreWeave reported Q2 revenue of $2.58 billion, up 112% year over year, with revenue backlog at $104 billion — nearly triple a year ago, up 246% — and raised full-year guidance to $12.4–13.2 billion. His $104 billion backlog figure was correct.

Here is the number he did not mention: CoreWeave carries roughly $51 billion in liabilities against about $2.2 billion in cash. That is the entire debate about this company. The backlog is real and the growth is real, and the balance sheet is built on debt that has to be serviced whether or not those contracts convert on schedule.

Nebius took a different route: Q2 revenue of $582.3 million, up 454%, with adjusted EBITDA positive at $236.2 million, and roughly $9 billion in customer prepayments in 2026 funding its buildout. Getting paid up front instead of borrowing is a materially different risk profile, and it's a real reason to distinguish between two companies Trapper mostly grouped together.

IREN signed a $3.4 billion five-year cloud deal with Nvidia, which also pledged up to $2.1 billion in investment. Applied Digital rose alongside the group on CoreWeave's and Nebius's results. Worth noting: Trapper said he "wasn't really a fan" of IREN — that call looks weak against the Nvidia deal, though he was early and public on CoreWeave and APLD, which he's entitled to.

The structural risk across all four: they are capital-intensive businesses renting depreciating hardware to a small number of very large customers, funded by debt or prepayments, in a market where the chips they're renting get superseded every 18 months. When AI capex spending decelerates — not stops, just decelerates — this is the layer that feels it first and hardest.

### The ETFs — NCLD, DRAM, PSI

**NCLD (Roundhill Neocloud ETF)** launched on **August 6, 2026** — twelve days before this episode. Expense ratio 0.65%. It's actively managed, investing in AI cloud and data center infrastructure including GPU-as-a-service, colocation, cooling, power and networking.

His own framing that this is "safer" than owning individual names is only partly true: it diversifies single-company blowup risk, but not sector risk, and this sector moves together.

**DRAM (Roundhill Memory ETF)** — he called it "drum," the ticker is DRAM. It's the first memory-focused ETF. **PSI (Invesco Semiconductors ETF)** is the most conventional of the three: about $2.34 billion in assets, tracking a 30-stock index that screens on price momentum, earnings momentum, quality, management action and value. **The 10-year yield.** He's right and this is the most important thing in the episode. The 10-year is around 4.7% and touched a 20-month high of 4.75% earlier in the month. The 30-year hit a 19-year high of 5.34%. Two-thirds of respondents in a Bloomberg survey expect the 10-year above 5% before year-end. His "5% is where it gets dangerous" line matches where the market itself is looking. His metaphor — the stock market as makeup the bond market wears — is a genuinely good way to hold this.

**Oil and the war.** WTI was around $81 on August 14 and $86–87 by August 20–21, so his "toward $85" was right and it has kept going. His SPR figures check out directionally — reserves are low enough that the drawdown reportedly threatens the salt caverns storing the oil. His recession call is a forecast, not a fact, and it's a common one right now; treat it as one informed opinion.

**Nasdaq 23-hour trading.** He got the substance right and one detail wrong. The launch date is **December 6, 2026**, running 9pm Sunday to 8pm Friday with a one-hour daily pause. But the SEC already granted accelerated approval on April 10, 2026 — his slide saying it "still requires SEC approval" is out of date. There's an SEC roundtable on September 17, 2026 on operational readiness, and the December date depends on DTCC and clearing infrastructure being ready, not on further approval. Practical detail he didn't mention: market orders and various opening and closing order types will not be available overnight, which matters a lot for anyone thinking about how their stops behave.

**Active managers.** His numbers are exact. Morningstar data shows only 27% of actively managed US large-cap funds beat their benchmarks over the twelve months to June 30, 2026, and just 13% over the full decade. The top ten S&P 500 companies are more than 40% of the index, the highest concentration since the 1960s — which is precisely why managers who won't concentrate that hard fall behind.

Where his conclusion overreaches: the data says professional stock pickers underperform a passive index. It does not say individuals outperform professionals. The cleanest inference from that statistic is an argument for low-cost index funds, not an argument for self-directed stock picking. He is drawing a different conclusion than the data supports, and the fee point — that you don't pay yourself a management fee — is true and worth having.

**Hedge fund positioning.** He showed two slides that point opposite ways: record short positioning in Nasdaq 100 futures, and hedge funds buying equities every session last week. He never reconciled them. They're not necessarily contradictory — you can hedge index exposure with futures shorts while buying single stocks, which is roughly what the single-stock-versus-macro-products split in his own slide describes — but presenting both as bullish confirmation without addressing the tension is where the analysis got thin.

### Fundamentals corner

**Beta — what it actually measures**

This episode surfaced beta naturally, and Trapper's on-air explanation was wrong in a way that's worth correcting cleanly, because the error is common.

Beta measures how a stock moves *relative to the market*, usually the S&P 500. The market's beta is 1.0 by definition.

A beta of 1.0 means the stock has historically moved roughly in step with the index. If the S&P rises 1%, the stock tends to rise about 1%.

A beta above 1.0 means the stock amplifies the market. A beta of 1.8 means that when the S&P rises 1%, the stock has tended to rise about 1.8% — and fall about 1.8% when the market falls 1%. Micron and SanDisk live here. That amplification is why Trapper's account swings six figures in a session.

A beta below 1.0 but still positive — say 0.6 — means the stock moves in the same direction as the market but with less force. Coca-Cola lives here. This is what most people mean when they say "less volatile."

A **negative** beta means something different in kind, not degree. It means the stock has historically moved in the *opposite* direction of the market. When the S&P falls, the stock tends to rise. Gold miners sometimes show this; so do certain hedged or defensive assets.

Trapper said negative beta means a stock "doesn't move as much as the market... it's not volatile." That's the definition of *low* beta, not negative beta. A negative-beta stock can be extremely volatile — it just tends to be volatile in the opposite direction.

Why the distinction matters for what he was actually saying: the slide he read said a record number of S&P constituents now have negative beta, and he concluded "negative beta stocks going to be where the money at." If those stocks genuinely have negative beta, that's not a claim that they're safe — it's a claim that the index has split into two groups moving against each other, which is exactly the "two tribes" framing the analyst he quoted used. In a market where AI stocks are the index, being negatively correlated to the index mostly means being outside the AI trade. That could be an opportunity or it could just be being left behind. His practical suggestion — screen for beta of 1.0 or lower if you want less violence in your account — is sound. The reasoning he attached to it wasn't.

Two limits worth knowing. Beta is backward-looking; it's calculated from past returns and it changes over time. And beta says nothing about whether a company is good — a wonderful business and a terrible business can have the same beta. It's a measure of how a stock moves, not of what it's worth.
