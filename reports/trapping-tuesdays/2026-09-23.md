## What to fix

Two theories are already on record and both are still live. Last week's report said check whether the launchd jobs are loaded at all. Yesterday's Market Mondays report found something sharper: the README's reinstall recipe copies the plist but never copies the script, and the repository file is named with underscores while the plist asks launchd for a hyphenated name. Either one would explain total silence.

**One paste tests all of it at once.** Run this in Terminal:

```
ls -l ~/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh; launchctl list | grep show-transcript; tail -5 ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/fetch.log
```

Three lines of output, and they separate the cases cleanly. No such file means the script was never deployed under the name launchd wants. Nothing from the launchctl line means the jobs are not loaded. Log entries you did not trigger mean the job is firing and failing downstream, which is a different problem entirely.

**The new thing I found this week: your alarm probably cannot fire, for a reason nobody has checked yet.**

When the script fails, it alerts you by running `/usr/bin/python3` against the deployed copy of `push_notify.py`. That path is macOS's own system Python. It is deliberately isolated and carries no packages installed with pip. If `push_notify.py` imports anything third-party — `requests` is the usual one for something that sends a push — then that call dies with a module-not-found error every single time, no matter how healthy the rest of the relay is.

And the script routes that error into the log and exits anyway, so the alarm failing is itself silent. One command tells you:

```
/usr/bin/python3 ~/.local/lib/double-o/push_notify.py send "relay test" "testing the alarm path"
```

If your phone does not buzz, your watchdog has been dead the whole time, which is the piece that has never added up — five weeks of failures and not one complaint from a script whose every failure path is supposed to complain. The fix is to point that line at the Python that actually has the packages, usually `/opt/homebrew/bin/python3`.

**And the structural problem underneath all of this.** Your `.gitignore` excludes `fetch.log` and `launchd-*.log`. The relay's working clone is a checkout of this same repository, so those patterns apply there too, which means the logs never leave your Mac. From here I cannot tell "the job never fired" from "the job fired and failed" — every diagnosis in five weeks of these reports, mine included, has been inference from commit timestamps.

It costs one tiny commit a week, and it converts next week's report from guesswork into fact. ### Fundamentals corner: why cheap cyclicals are most dangerous at the top

Last week I taught you to write the date next to every fundamental number. This builds directly on it, and it is the concept the memory trade above forces you to understand.

Most companies you will look at are reasonably steady. A company like Coca-Cola sells roughly the same amount of soda next year as last year. Earnings drift up gradually, and a price-to-earnings ratio behaves the way you would intuitively expect: lower is cheaper.

**Cyclical companies do not work like that.** Memory chips, oil, steel, shipping, homebuilding — these are commodity businesses where everyone sells essentially the same product, so the only thing that matters is the price of that product. And that price swings violently, because supply takes years to build and demand can turn in months.

Here is the trap, and it is exactly inverted from what feels correct.

When memory prices are at record highs, as they are right now, memory companies earn enormous profits. Earnings per share explodes. The P/E ratio — price divided by earnings — collapses, because the bottom of the fraction got huge. Micron can look *cheap* on a P/E basis at the very moment its business is running hotter than it will ever run again. Those are called **peak earnings**, and a low P/E built on peak earnings is the most expensive kind of cheap there is.

The reverse is just as counterintuitive. At the bottom of a cycle, when memory prices have crashed and these companies are losing money, the P/E is enormous or does not exist at all, because earnings are near zero or negative. The stock looks wildly overpriced at precisely the moment the cycle is about to turn.

So for cyclicals the rule flips: **a low P/E is a warning and a high P/E can be an opportunity.** Professional investors look at price-to-book value instead, or at the price of the underlying commodity, because those do not lie to you the way earnings-based ratios do.

Two habits to carry out of this.

First, before you use a P/E on any company, ask what the product sells for and whether that price is near a record. If memory chips cost five times what they did a year ago — and they roughly do — then today's earnings are not a normal year, and no ratio built on them describes a normal year.

Second, learn to separate the *company* question from the *cycle* question. Seagate having its nearline capacity fully booked through 2027 is a real fact about a real business with real pricing power. That is different from asking whether memory prices stay at record highs, which is a question about supply and demand across an entire industry. Trapper's August call got the cycle right. Whether the cycle continues from here is a completely separate question, and nothing in a five-week win tells you the answer.

Micron reports on September 30. Whatever it says, watch what happens to its P/E ratio on the sites you read — and remember that the number moving does not mean the business changed. It means the denominator finally updated.
