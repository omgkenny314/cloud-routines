**Fault one: the Trappin Tuesdays job fires before YouTube has made the captions. There is no retry. The relay has never worked on schedule.**

Here is the chain, and you can verify every link:

- The schedule file `launchd/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-tt.plist` sets `Weekday 3`, `Hour 0`, `Minute 0`. Weekday 3 is **Wednesday**. So the job fires **Wednesday at midnight**, local time.

**And here is the proof it has always been this way.** The one successful fetch in the repository is stamped `2026-08-20T23:39:10Z`. In your timezone that is **Thursday August 20 at 6:39pm Central**. **Both shows stopping on the same day also rules out anything about the show itself** — not a channel change, not a captions policy, not Trapper doing anything differently. It is the relay.

So the picture is: the relay only ever succeeded by running late. When it runs on time, it runs too early and dies for the week.

**The fix is to move both jobs to the morning after.** This is on your Mac. One paste:

```
for S in tt mm; do
  P=~/Library/LaunchAgents/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-$S.plist
  /usr/libexec/PlistBuddy -c "Set :StartCalendarInterval:Hour 5" "$P"
  launchctl unload "$P" 2>/dev/null; launchctl load "$P"
  echo "$S -> $(/usr/libexec/PlistBuddy -c 'Print :StartCalendarInterval' "$P" | tr -d '\n')"
done
```

That moves both jobs to 5am, giving YouTube five or six hours after the stream ends to generate captions.

### Fundamentals corner

**What a bond yield tells you about a stock.**

This week's selloff did not start with an earnings miss or a downgrade. It started in the bond market, and the signal was visible there days before the stock prices moved. That is a tool worth having, and it is exactly the kind of under-looked data Episode 213 is about.

When a company borrows, it issues a bond — a promise to pay a fixed amount on a schedule. Those bonds then trade between investors. The company's payments are fixed, but the **price** of the bond moves, and the yield is the return a buyer gets at today's price. **Price down, yield up.** So a rising yield on existing bonds means investors are selling them cheaper, which means they are less confident of being paid.

CoreWeave's 2032 bonds went to a yield of about 15 percent. Compare that to a US government bond, which has carried something like 4 percent. That gap — roughly 11 percentage points — is the **credit spread**, and it is the market's price for the risk that this particular borrower does not pay. A spread that wide is a serious warning. Investors demanding 15 percent to lend to a company are not expressing mild caution.

The credit default swap figure says the same thing from another angle. A CDS is insurance against a borrower defaulting. CoreWeave's five-year CDS widened to about 1,140 basis points, meaning roughly 11.4 percent of the insured amount per year to insure it. Nobody pays that for a company they consider safe.

**Why this matters to a stock investor who never buys a bond.** If a company must refinance at 15 percent instead of 5 percent, the extra interest comes out of profit — the profit the share price is based on. And bondholders are paid before shareholders. So when bondholders start demanding compensation for the risk of not being paid at all, shareholders are, by definition, in a worse position than they were. **The bond market often sees trouble first**, because bondholders only care about one question — will I be paid — while equity investors are weighing a growth story at the same time.

**The practical habit.** For any company carrying a lot of debt — and that is most of the AI build-out names — the useful question is not just "is the business growing." It is **"what does it cost this company to borrow, and is that cost rising."** You can get at this without reading bond tables: look for interest expense on the income statement, the debt load on the balance sheet, and news about refinancing or credit ratings. When a company's borrowing cost is climbing, a growth story can be completely true and the stock can still fall, because the financing has to work for the growth to reach shareholders.

That is the distinction this week drew. Memory companies sell a product at a profit. Neocloud companies borrow money to buy equipment and rent it out. Both benefit from artificial intelligence. Only one of them needs the credit market to stay friendly.
