### Fundamentals corner: multiple compression versus earnings decline

When a stock falls a long way, there are only two things that can have happened, and telling them apart is most of fundamental analysis.

A share price is, roughly, two numbers multiplied together: **the company's earnings per share**, and **the multiple** — how many dollars investors will pay for each dollar of those earnings, the price-to-earnings ratio. Price equals earnings times multiple. So when price falls by a third, either earnings fell, or the multiple fell, or both.

Those two causes could not be more different in what they mean.

**Earnings decline** means the business got worse. Fewer customers, thinner margins, a product losing to a competitor. The company is genuinely worth less, and the lower price is the market being right.

**Multiple compression** means the business is unchanged and investors have decided to pay less for the same earnings. Maybe because bonds now pay 5% and stocks must compete. Maybe because a risk appeared that nobody can quantify. The earnings are still arriving; the price tag on them shrank.

Nvidia right now is almost a laboratory specimen of the second kind. Revenue grew about 106% last quarter and is guided to grow about 91% this one. Not one of the reasons given for the fall is a business reason — they are all about a board investigation and a congressional subpoena. Earnings did not fall. The multiple did.

And here is the discipline, because the obvious inference is a trap. "It is only multiple compression, so it is a bargain" assumes the multiple will go back. Sometimes it does. Sometimes the market is pricing a real risk that has not shown up in the numbers *yet* — and when it does show up, the earnings fall too, and what looked cheap gets cheaper. In this case, the question is whether governance trouble at the top of a company eventually reaches its results. Nobody honestly knows.

So the question to carry into your own research is not "has this fallen a lot?" It is: **which of the two numbers moved, and do I have a reason to think it moves back?** Pull up the last two or three quarters of revenue and earnings per share, and ask whether those lines bend at the same moment the price did. When price falls and earnings keep rising, you are looking at multiple compression — and then your real job is working out what the market is afraid of, because something is doing the compressing.

That also connects to Episode 214's framework, from his own episode description: a wide gap between price and the 20- and 50-day moving averages, a stretched RSI, unusual volume. Those tools tell you a move has been *extreme*. They are silent on whether it was *justified*. The earnings-versus-multiple question is how you ask the second thing.
