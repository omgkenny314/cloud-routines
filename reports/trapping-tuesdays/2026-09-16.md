### One design change worth making

Even after the schedule is fixed, the current timing is thin. Your Mac's clock is Pacific, so the Wednesday 00:00 job lands at 2:00 AM Central — thirty minutes before this cloud routine reads the repository. The show runs three hours or more, and YouTube often needs another hour or two after a long stream before auto-captions exist. If captions are not ready at 2:00 AM, the script fails outright and nothing retries until the following week.

Add a second firing a few hours later, around 06:00, as a safety net. The script already handles this correctly on its own: when the transcript is current it logs "transcript already current," pushes anything still pending, and exits without making a churn commit. So a second run costs nothing when the first one worked, and rescues the week when it did not. You would get the recap late in the morning instead of never.

### Fundamentals corner: stale numbers, and the "as of" habit

Everything above is a story about acting on data that was correct once and quietly went out of date. That is also one of the most common ways new investors get hurt in fundamental analysis, so it is worth making the connection.

When you look up a company and see a P/E ratio, you are seeing a fraction. The top is the share price. The bottom is earnings per share. The price updates every second the market is open. **The earnings number does not.** It changes four times a year, when the company reports.

Most sites show you what is called the **trailing twelve months**, usually written TTM. That means the sum of the last four quarters the company has actually reported. If a company reported in early August, then the TTM figure you read today covers a period that ended in June. It is built on the past, and the most recent stretch of it is already a couple of months behind you.

That is fine, as long as you know it. It becomes a trap in two situations.

**The first is right before earnings.** A company about to report may be sitting on a quarter that looks nothing like the last four. The P/E on your screen describes a business that, in a week, will no longer exist in that form. This is why the same stock can look cheap on Monday and expensive on Friday without the price moving much at all — the denominator changed underneath it.

**The second is after a sharp move.** If a stock falls forty percent, every site will show you a P/E that dropped forty percent too, because only the top of the fraction updated. It will look like a bargain. But stocks usually fall for a reason, and often that reason is that future earnings are going to be lower. The ratio has not caught up yet. A low P/E created purely by a falling price is not the same thing as a cheap company, and telling those two apart is most of the work.

There is a companion number, **forward P/E**, which uses analysts' estimates for the next twelve months instead of the reported past. It has the opposite problem: it is current, but it is a guess, and estimates drift. Neither number is the truth. Looking at both, and noticing when they disagree sharply, tells you more than either alone — a big gap between trailing and forward P/E means the market expects earnings to change a lot, and that is worth understanding before you do anything else.

**The habit to build:** whenever you write down a fundamental figure, write down the date it describes next to it. "P/E 28, on earnings through June 30." It takes three extra seconds and it makes the staleness visible instead of invisible.

That is the same discipline that would have caught this relay a month ago. A number without a date attached will happily keep looking correct long after it has stopped being true.
