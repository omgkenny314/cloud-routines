**The most likely cause is a name mismatch, and it is visible in this repository.** Both launchd jobs ask for:

`~/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh` — with hyphens.

The file in this repo is named:

`fetch_show_transcript.sh` — with underscores.

### Fundamentals corner: why a cheap-looking cyclical can be the most expensive stock on the board

Micron reports tonight, so let us use it. This is the concept that matters most for everything in the memory section above, and it is the one most likely to cost you money if you have not met it.

Start with the arithmetic. The **price-to-earnings ratio**, or P/E, is the share price divided by earnings per share. It says how many dollars you pay for each dollar of annual profit. A P/E of 20 means you pay $20 for $1 of yearly earnings.

Micron trades near **$1,078**. It has guided to roughly **$31 of adjusted earnings in a single quarter**. If the company earned at that rate for four quarters, that is about $124 a year — and $1,078 divided by $124 is a P/E of about **nine**.

Nine. The S&P 500 as a whole typically trades in the high teens or low twenties. So Micron looks less than half the price of the average American company, while growing revenue over 350%.

**That should feel wrong, and it is the whole lesson.**

Memory chips are a **commodity**. A gigabyte of DRAM from one maker is interchangeable with a gigabyte from another. Nobody pays extra for the brand. When demand exceeds supply, prices spike, and because the factories are already built and mostly paid for, nearly every extra dollar of revenue falls to profit — which is how you get an 86% gross margin. But those profits are a signal flare. They tell Micron and every competitor to build more factories. Factories take two to three years. When the new capacity arrives, supply overshoots demand, prices collapse, and the same company that earned $31 a quarter can lose money. Micron has lost money in past troughs. This is not a hypothetical.

So the market does something that looks irrational and is not: **it refuses to pay a normal multiple for peak-cycle earnings, because it does not believe they will last.** The low P/E is not the market saying the stock is cheap. It is the market saying these earnings are temporary.

This produces a rule that inverts everything you learn about ordinary companies:

**A cyclical business looks cheapest at the top and most expensive at the bottom.**

At the peak, earnings are enormous and the P/E is tiny. At the bottom, earnings are near zero or negative and the P/E is huge or meaningless. An investor who buys cyclicals on a low P/E buys at peaks. An investor who refuses to look at them on a high P/E misses bottoms.

The basketball version: judging a cyclical by its current P/E is like signing a thirty-two-year-old to a maximum contract because he just had his best scoring season. The season was real. The price is being set by a number that is about to move the other way.

**What to do with this instead.** For a cyclical, the useful questions are not about the multiple. They are: where are we in the cycle, and how much new supply is being built? That is exactly why the guidance on tonight's Micron call matters more than the quarter. The quarter tells you where earnings are now. The guidance and any commentary on supply tell you how much longer they last.

One fair counterargument, so you have both sides. The bull case is that this cycle is different — that AI memory demand is structural rather than a normal inventory swing, that high-bandwidth memory is a genuinely differentiated product rather than a pure commodity, and that customers co-engineering roadmaps out past 2030 means these are long-term partnerships, not spot purchases. That case is not silly, and it is why the stock is up 256% rather than 26%. But it is a case that must be argued, not assumed. "The P/E is only nine" is not an argument. It is the trap.

Apply this the next time Trapper puts a low P/E on the screen. The first question is not "is that cheap?" It is **"is this a cyclical, and where in the cycle are we?"** For most of what he covers in the AI and semiconductor space, the answer to the first half is yes.
