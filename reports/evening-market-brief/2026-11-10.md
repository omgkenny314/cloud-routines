# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, November 10, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a sell-off with one very clear cause, and the cause was a single number released at 8:30 this morning. Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October, when forecasters expected 0.3%. Strip out food and energy and the "core" figure rose 0.3%, when forecasters expected 0.2%. That is a small miss in absolute terms, and it did something large: it convinced the bond market that the Federal Reserve is going to raise interest rates next month. The 10-year Treasury yield crossed 5% for the first time in weeks, and all three major stock indexes finished lower — the S&P 500 down 0.71%, the Dow down 0.57%, the Nasdaq Composite down 0.90%. That erased roughly a third of yesterday's big rebound.

The single biggest storyline is that word *raise*. For most of the last few years, the market's question was how fast the Fed would cut rates. That question is gone. The Fed's benchmark rate currently sits in a 3.75% to 4% range, it was held there on October 28 with two officials voting to hike, and after this morning's report traders priced in roughly a 99% chance of an increase at the December meeting. The odds of a *double-sized* half-point increase jumped from about 1% to somewhere in the low teens. A market that has to re-learn the word "hike" is a market that reprices almost everything, which is why bonds, gold, bitcoin and stocks all moved in the same unhappy direction today.

The second storyline is the one that refuses to end: Nvidia. The chipmaker fell about another 6% on continued fallout from the investigation into its chief executive, and in doing so it reached the most extreme oversold reading on its chart since June 2019. Microsoft briefly passed it to become the world's most valuable company. Tonight's lesson is built on that oversold reading, because it is a perfect example of a tool that tells you something real and is constantly misunderstood as telling you something it does not.

## Headlines that matter

**The October Consumer Price Index came in hot.** Headline prices rose 0.4% against 0.3% expected; core prices, excluding volatile food and energy, rose 0.3% against 0.2% expected and matched September. *Why it matters:* the Consumer Price Index is the main measure of what households pay, and the Fed's whole job is keeping it near 2% a year. A 0.3% monthly core reading annualizes to roughly 3.6% — not a crisis, but moving the wrong way. One wrinkle to know: this report had **no annual inflation figures**, because last year's government shutdown left gaps in the underlying data. That is unusual, and it means everyone is working from monthly changes alone, which are noisier.

**A Fed governor had pre-committed to what this number would mean.** Christopher Waller said *before* the release that he leaned toward a quarter-point increase in December regardless, and that a **half-point** increase should be considered if core inflation came in around 0.3% or higher. It came in at 0.3%. *Why it matters:* this is why the reaction was so sharp. The market did not have to guess how officials would interpret the data — one of them had published the threshold in advance, and the data hit it. Traders now price roughly a 99% chance of a December increase, with the odds of a half-point move jumping from about 1% to the low teens. The Fed meets **December 8 and 9**, announcing on the 9th.

**The 10-year Treasury yield crossed 5%.** *Why it matters, and this is the most useful thing to understand tonight:* a stock is a claim on profits arriving years from now, and to value it you discount those future profits back to today using an interest rate. Raise the rate and every future dollar is worth less now. Companies whose profits sit furthest in the future — fast-growing technology companies — get marked down hardest. That is the whole reason the Nasdaq fell more than the Dow today. It is not sentiment. It is arithmetic. A 5% risk-free yield also simply competes with stocks: you can collect it without owning any equity risk.

**Gold fell more than 2% and bitcoin hit a three-month low.** *Why it matters:* both are assets that produce no income. Their appeal is partly that cash and bonds pay little. When bonds pay 5% and may pay more, the cost of holding something that pays nothing rises. Watching gold and bitcoin fall *together with* stocks, while yields rose, is the signature of a day driven by interest rates rather than by fear.

**Nvidia's governance problem is now a market problem.** *Why it matters:* this is **key-man risk** — the possibility that a company's prospects depend heavily on one individual. Nvidia is large enough that its own troubles drag on the indexes, and the alleged export-control violations raise a second question beyond Huang's position: whether regulators tighten the rules on selling advanced chips abroad. Goldman Sachs analyst James Schneider said semiconductor stocks are "now clearly in oversold territory," and Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon argued the drop may be overdone if Huang stays. Those are opinions, not facts, and worth labeling as such.

## Today's lesson: RSI, or how to measure that a fall has been fast without learning whether it was right

Tonight's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning we read the behavior of the price itself rather than the company's financial statements. Today handed us a textbook reading of a tool called RSI, and an equally good illustration of what it cannot tell you.

**RSI stands for Relative Strength Index**, and the name is unhelpfully grand. Here is all it does. Take the last 14 trading days. Add up how much the price rose on the up days, add up how much it fell on the down days, and compare the two. Express the comparison on a scale from 0 to 100. If every one of the last 14 days was up, you get 100. If every one was down, you get 0. A stock in balance sits near 50. By long convention, a reading below 30 is called **oversold** and above 70 **overbought**. Note what is *not* in that recipe: no earnings, no revenue, no price-to-earnings ratio, no news. Nothing about the company at all. RSI compares a stock only to its own recent self.

**Today Nvidia's RSI fell to 23 — its lowest reading since June 2019.** Seven and a half years. That is a real, specific fact, and it says something precise: the selling in Nvidia has been unusually one-sided and fast. Not that the stock is down a lot — plenty of stocks are down a lot with ordinary RSI readings, because they fell gradually, with up days mixed in. A reading of 23 means the down days have overwhelmed the up days with almost no relief. Combine it with the measure from our November 6 lesson and you get the full picture: Nvidia closed about 14% below its 200-day moving average, the furthest below that line since 2025. The trend has broken, and the break has been violent.

Here is a basketball way to hold it. A shooter misses nine of his last ten. You would say he is ice cold, and you would be right — that is a true description of the last ten shots. It is not a statement about whether he is a good shooter, and it is not a prediction about the eleventh shot. Those are different questions needing different evidence. RSI is the shooting streak. It measures the *streak*, not the *shooter*. The mistake people make — and you will hear it constantly, including from analysts on television today — is sliding from "oversold" to "cheap," as though a fast fall were itself evidence of a bargain. It is not. "Oversold" describes the speed and lopsidedness of the decline. "Cheap" is a claim about price versus the value of the business, which is a fundamental question that RSI never touches.

Nvidia makes the gap between those two ideas unusually vivid, because the thing driving the stock down is not a number RSI could ever see: an investigation into the chief executive, and a question about export controls. RSI knows that selling has been extreme. It does not know, and cannot know, whether the risk being priced in is real. One analyst put it well today — the reading says nothing about whether the regulatory risk is justified. And a crucial practical point: oversold can *stay* oversold. When a market is genuinely repricing something, RSI can sit in the twenties for weeks while the price keeps falling, because each new low resets the clock. An extreme reading is a flag that says "something unusual is happening here, go find out what." It is not an answer. On November 17 Nvidia reports earnings, and the board's investigation will eventually conclude — those will supply answers. The chart only supplied the question.
