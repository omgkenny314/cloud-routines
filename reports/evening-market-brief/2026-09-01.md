# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, September 1, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

September opened the way August closed, only worse: the bond market pushed, and stocks fell over. The single biggest storyline today was not a company and not a headline out of Washington — it was the global government bond sell-off, which picked up speed and dragged the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield to roughly 4.78%, its highest level since January 2025. The 30-year yield sat around 5.28%. Feeding that was oil: two oil tankers, one Saudi and one South Korean-owned, were struck by projectiles overnight in the Strait of Hormuz, and Iranian state media reported a supertanker caught fire after hitting two naval mines in the southern part of the strait. Higher oil means higher expected inflation, higher expected inflation means a Federal Reserve that is already openly discussing a rate *hike* has one more reason to pull the trigger, and higher expected interest rates make every stock a little less valuable today. That chain ran straight through the market. Technology and anything tied to artificial-intelligence infrastructure took the worst of it, which is why the Nasdaq lost more than the Dow in percentage terms even though the Dow lost more points. Underneath the index-level damage, the day also delivered a genuinely split economic picture — factories are still expanding, but construction spending just fell to a nearly three-year low — and, after the closing bell, a heavy slate of technology earnings that mostly beat expectations and mostly got sold anyway.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: 7,631.47, down 0.71%** — a loss of about 55 points from Monday's 7,686.14. Rising yields hit the highest-priced parts of the market hardest, and this index is dominated by them.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 52,766.88, down 419.02 points, or 0.79%.** The biggest point loss of the three. Home Depot and other rate-sensitive names weighed; energy shares were the main thing pushing back.
- **Nasdaq Composite: 26,099.77, down 1.03%** — a decline of about 271 points, and the worst of the three in percentage terms. Chipmakers and AI-infrastructure suppliers led the decline. The VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH), a basket that tracks chip stocks, lost more than 2%.
- **10-year Treasury yield: around 4.78%, the highest since January 2025.** This was the number that drove the day. When the yield on a government bond rises, that bond competes harder against stocks for investors' money, and every future dollar a company earns is worth less in today's terms.
- **30-year Treasury yield: around 5.28%.** Worth its own line, because the long end of the bond market is where the real stress is. Bloomberg reported that the 30-year has now finished above 5% on 55 days since January — the most closes above that mark in any year since 2006. This is not a one-day event; it is a months-long grind.
- **Crude oil: U.S. West Texas Intermediate around $86.57 a barrel and Brent crude, the international benchmark, around $91.28, each up roughly 0.9% to 1%.** Both moved on the tanker strikes and the mine report in the Strait of Hormuz. The Brent figure checks out cleanly against Monday's $90.49 settle.
- **Bitcoin: roughly $77,200 to $78,000, drifting lower through the day.** It opened near $78,559, was $78,155 at 8 a.m. Eastern, and had slipped to $77,648 by 9:43 a.m. **I could not verify a precise 4 p.m. price against a second source, so treat this as a range rather than a close.** The pressure is the same as the stock market's: an asset that pays you no income looks worse when a Treasury bond pays you more.
- **Gold: roughly $4,440 an ounce, near a two-week low.** Same counterintuitive story as yesterday — war is good for gold, rising rates are bad for it, and rates are winning right now.

## Big movers and why

- **Fervo Energy (FRVO) rose about 24.9%, up $3.83 to roughly $19.21** — the day's standout gain. The geothermal power company announced what it called the world's largest enhanced geothermal power purchase agreement to date. A power purchase agreement, or PPA, is a long-term contract in which a buyer commits to purchase electricity at an agreed price; for a company that has to finance expensive projects up front, a signed PPA turns a hope into a bankable revenue stream. The project is Cape Station in Utah, expected to come online in 2028, and it is slated to support potential Google data center development. This is the AI-power-demand trade in its purest form.
- **Palo Alto Networks (PANW) fell about 6% during the session, closing near $359,** ahead of its earnings report after the bell. Nothing company-specific went wrong — this was a high-priced software stock in a risk-off tape, sold into an event. The stock is still up roughly 89% year to date.
- **Dell Technologies (DELL) fell about 6.5% during the session, to around $426,** also ahead of after-hours earnings, and for the same reason.
- **Moderna (MRNA) rose about 5% to 6%,** extending an enormous August in which it gained roughly 156%. That surge came after a cancer vaccine developed jointly with Merck showed positive initial results in its first late-stage trial. Today's gain came *despite* news that GSK is advancing a rival mRNA flu vaccine into Phase III trials — a good sign of how much conviction is currently behind the story.
- **Homebuilders and building-products stocks fell** after the July construction spending report. The iShares U.S. Home Construction ETF (ITB) lost almost 2%, Builders FirstSource (BLDR) slid about 4%, LGI Homes (LGIH) fell about 3%, and Home Depot (HD) dropped about 2%. Higher mortgage rates are a direct consequence of the higher 10-year yield, so this group is the most literal expression of today's bond story.
- **AI-infrastructure suppliers were hit hard.** Lumentum (LITE), which makes photonics components, dropped more than 5%. Teradyne (TER), a semiconductor test-equipment maker, fell 5%. Coherent (COHR) shed about 2%. These are the second-derivative AI names — they don't make the chips, they make the things that make and connect the chips — and they tend to fall further than the market on risk-off days.
- **Energy was the bright spot.** Chevron (CVX) advanced about 1%, with the sector rising broadly alongside oil.
- **Howmet Aerospace (HWM) rebounded** after Monday's drop on news that SpaceX plans to produce some gas-turbine components internally. Citi placed the stock on a 30-day upside catalyst watch, with analyst John Godyn writing that the SpaceX news "reinforces the critical nature of HWM parts." Bernstein separately maintained an Outperform rating and raised its target to $328. **I could not verify the exact size of today's gain**, so I'm reporting the direction only.

## Headlines that matter

**The global bond sell-off is now the market's main event.** For most of this year the story has been earnings and artificial intelligence. Today it was bonds, and it is worth understanding why this matters more than a normal down day. A government bond yield is the "risk-free rate" — the return you can get without taking any company risk. Every other asset is priced relative to it. When the risk-free rate climbs, three things happen at once: bonds become a real alternative to stocks, borrowing gets more expensive for companies and homebuyers, and the mathematical value of profits a company expects to earn years from now shrinks. That last point is why the Nasdaq fell more than the Dow. A company whose earnings are mostly far in the future loses more value when rates rise than a company earning its money now.

**The Strait of Hormuz escalated again, and this time it hit ships.** Two oil tankers were struck by projectiles overnight, and Iranian state media reported that a supertanker caught fire after hitting two naval mines in the southern strait. **Why it matters:** yesterday's escalation was an exchange of military strikes. Today's involved commercial vessels and mines actually in the water. Roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes through that chokepoint and there is no way to route around it. Markets are not pricing the damage to these particular ships — they are pricing a rising probability that shipping through the strait becomes untenable. That probability is what keeps feeding oil, which keeps feeding inflation expectations, which keeps feeding bond yields.

**Manufacturing is still expanding, but decelerating.** The Institute for Supply Management's Manufacturing PMI for August came in at 54.6%, down 1 percentage point from July's 55.6%. Any reading above 50 signals expansion, so this is the eighth straight month of growth, and the broader economy has now expanded for 22 consecutive months. But the New Orders Index — the forward-looking piece, because today's orders are tomorrow's production — fell to 53.7% from 56.7%, a 3-point drop. **Why it matters:** this is a "good but less good" report. It gives the Fed no reason to think the economy is weak enough to need help, which is exactly the reading that supports a rate hike.

**Construction spending fell to a nearly three-year low.** July construction spending dropped 0.5% to $2.158 trillion, the lowest level since October 2023; economists surveyed by Reuters had expected no change at all. Residential construction investment tumbled 1.3%, and single-family housing projects fell 3.2%. On a year-over-year basis, total construction spending is down 3.8%. (June was revised slightly better, to unchanged from a previously reported 0.1% dip.) **Why it matters:** housing is the part of the economy that responds to interest rates first and most violently. With the 10-year yield at a 20-month high, mortgage rates follow, and this is what that looks like in the actual data. It is also the clearest evidence so far that the Fed's higher-for-longer posture is biting somewhere real.

**The Fed backdrop.** Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said at Jackson Hole on Friday that recent good inflation readings had not convinced him the underlying trend had improved. As of Monday, fed funds futures put the odds of a quarter-point *hike* at the September 15–16 meeting at roughly 60%, per the CME's FedWatch tool. **I could not verify today's updated reading against a second source**, so I won't give you a number for it — but with oil higher and yields higher, the direction of that probability today was almost certainly not down. Note how unusual this framing is: for most of the past two years the debate was how fast the Fed would cut. The debate now is whether it hikes.

## After the bell

A heavy technology slate reported after today's close. The pattern is striking and worth sitting with: nearly everything beat, and most of it sold off anyway.

- **Palo Alto Networks (PANW), the cybersecurity company, reported fiscal fourth-quarter revenue of $3.41 billion, up 34% year over year,** ahead of the roughly $3.35 billion consensus. Adjusted net income was $853 million, or $1.02 per diluted share, beating the $0.98 estimate. On a GAAP basis — that is, under standard accounting rules, before the company's own adjustments — it posted a $282 million loss. Next-Generation Security annual recurring revenue reached $9.10 billion, up 63%, and remaining performance obligations (contracted revenue not yet recognized) hit $21.2 billion, up 34%. Fiscal 2027 revenue guidance of $14.10–$14.20 billion implies 23–24% growth and came in above Wall Street expectations.
- **Dell Technologies (DELL) reported second-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue of about $46.97 billion, up 58% year over year,** against roughly $44.9 billion expected, with adjusted earnings per share of $7.04 versus $4.95 expected — a very large beat. AI server backlog reached a record $95 billion. Management raised full-year revenue guidance to about $192 billion and lifted its fiscal 2027 AI server revenue target to roughly $60 billion. Gross margin, however, compressed to 17.8% from 21.1%. More on that in today's lesson.
- **MongoDB (MDB), the database software company, reported second-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue of $771.8 million, up 30% year over year** — its fastest growth in several years — with adjusted earnings per share of $1.91 against $1.61 expected. Atlas, its cloud product, grew about 29%. Guidance was raised. Shares nonetheless fell sharply in after-hours trading, reportedly down close to 14%.
- **GitLab (GTLB), the software development platform, reported second-quarter revenue of $286.3 million, up 21%,** with record gross bookings and net annual recurring revenue growth above 40%. Shares reportedly fell about 6.8% after hours.
- **Credo Technology Group (CRDO), which makes high-speed connectivity hardware for data centers, reported revenue of $479 million** against roughly $471.8 million expected, with adjusted earnings per share of $1.20 versus $1.17 expected.

One source reported Dell up about 4.3% after the report; another headline suggested Palo Alto Networks was surging. **I could not verify either move against a second reliable source, so I am not reporting after-hours percentage changes for those two.** The MongoDB and GitLab figures above each came from a single source as well, so treat them as indicative. The reported *results* — revenue, earnings, and guidance — are the parts I'd trust; the overnight price reactions frequently reverse by the opening bell anyway.

## Today's lesson: gross margin, or why growing revenue 58% isn't automatically good news

Dell reported that revenue grew 58% year over year, to nearly $47 billion. That is an extraordinary number for a company of Dell's size and age. In the same report, gross margin fell to 17.8% from 21.1%. Those two facts together are today's lesson, and once you can hold both in your head at the same time, you are reading financial statements like an analyst rather than a headline reader.

**Gross margin** is what's left of each sales dollar after you subtract the direct cost of producing the thing you sold — parts, materials, manufacturing labor. It does not subtract the cost of the sales team, the research lab, the executives, or the interest on the debt. Those come out later, giving you *operating margin* and then *net margin*. Gross margin is the first and cleanest read on one question: how much value does this company actually add to what it buys? Dell's 17.8% means that of every dollar of revenue, about 18 cents survives to cover everything else and become profit. Ninety days earlier, about 21 cents survived.

Here's why it dropped, and it's not that Dell got worse at its job. Dell's growth is coming from AI servers, and an AI server is mostly an expensive Nvidia chip that Dell buys, integrates into a rack, and resells. Dell adds real engineering value — power, cooling, networking, deployment at scale — but the chip is the bulk of the cost, and Dell doesn't control its price. So the more of Dell's revenue that comes from AI servers, the lower the blended gross margin goes, even if every single product line is performing exactly as well as before. Analysts call this a **mix shift**: the average changed because the composition changed, not because any component got worse. Dell's CFO David Kennedy said as much on the report — "Excluding the impact of AI mix, our gross margin outlook is better than it was 90 days ago." Notice that construction. Management is telling you the decline is mix, not deterioration. That is a claim worth checking over the next few quarters rather than simply accepting, but it is a specific, falsifiable claim rather than a dodge, and that's the kind you want to hear.

Think of a shooting guard who goes from 15 shot attempts a game to 24. His scoring average jumps, and the box score looks fantastic. But his field goal percentage drops from 48% to 43%. Is he better or worse? It genuinely depends. If the extra nine shots are producing more total points than the efficiency loss costs, the team is ahead — and if he's taking those shots because the offense finally has a reason to run through him, that's a good problem. If the percentage keeps sliding quarter after quarter with no floor in sight, you eventually have a volume scorer who isn't helping you win. For Dell right now, the volume is clearly winning: adjusted earnings of $7.04 per share blew past the $4.95 expected, so the extra revenue is more than compensating for the thinner slice. The thing to watch in coming quarters is whether 17.8% is a floor or a step on the way down — because a growing pile of low-margin revenue and a shrinking pile of high-margin revenue can look like a boom right up until it doesn't. That question is the single most important one about Dell's business, and you'd never know to ask it from the revenue line alone.
