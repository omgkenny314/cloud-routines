# Market Close Brief — Monday, October 26, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a relief day for the AI trade, and the relief came from an unusual place: a company that does not trade yet. Anthropic, the artificial-intelligence lab, publicly filed its IPO paperwork this morning, and the market took that filing as evidence that money is still willing to flow into AI at enormous scale — which is exactly the thing investors had started to doubt over the past three weeks. Chip and optical-component stocks led, the Nasdaq Composite closed up 0.82%, the S&P 500 up 0.7%, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average up a more modest 0.28%. All three indexes were coming off two straight losing weeks. Two things kept the day from feeling like a straightforward rally. First, the gains were concentrated: technology and AI-adjacent names did the work while the Dow's older industrial and consumer companies mostly sat still. Second, there was a genuine shock elsewhere in the world — Brazil's stock market fell more than 13% after an upset presidential election result, one of the worst single days any major market has had since 2020. And hanging over everything is Wednesday, when the Federal Reserve announces its interest-rate decision in an environment where the next move is expected to be a hike, not a cut. Today was a good day. It was not a resolution of anything.

## Big movers and why

- **Surgery Partners (ticker SGRY) jumped 47% — the day's largest move by far.** Bon Secours Mercy Health, a large nonprofit hospital system, agreed to buy the surgical-facility operator for $23.50 a share in cash, valuing it at $5.7 billion. When an acquirer offers cash at a fixed price, the target's stock typically leaps most of the way to that price immediately and then stops moving. The remaining gap is the market's estimate of the risk that the deal does not close.

- **Lumentum Holdings (ticker LITE) rose about 8.2%, closing at $818.32, up $61.94.** Lumentum makes the lasers and optical parts that move data around inside and between AI data centers. StoneX started coverage with a Buy rating and a $1,200 price target, citing "critical scarcity points" in high-speed lasers and optical switches and management's statement that AI-related components are sold out through 2029. The read-across hit its neighbors: Coherent (ticker COHR) rose about 7.6% and Applied Optoelectronics (ticker AAOI) about 10%. Reported figures for Lumentum ranged from 8% to 10.6% depending on when the snapshot was taken; the close is the number I trust.

- **Fair Isaac (ticker FICO) fell about 14% — the worst performer in the S&P 500.** Fair Isaac is the company behind the FICO credit score. Three things hit at once: the company said it would hold its wholesale mortgage-score price flat at $10 for lenders in its Preferred Program, which investors read as a defensive move to protect market share rather than a position of strength; Wells Fargo downgraded the stock to equal weight from overweight; and Bill Pulte, director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, pledged "more action" on credit-score competition, which accelerated the slide. This is a continuation of a story from late September, when the stock fell about 25% after regulators opened the door to a FICO competitor in mortgage lending.

- **JetBlue Airways (ticker JBLU) fell about 12%.** The Wall Street Journal reported that JetBlue's senior creditors are working with a restructuring advisor. Why that language matters: creditors hiring a restructuring advisor is a step taken when lenders begin preparing for the possibility that they will have to negotiate over who gets paid what. It is not a bankruptcy filing, but it is the market's cue to reprice the equity, because shareholders stand last in line.

- **Nvidia (ticker NVDA) rose roughly 2% to 3%, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index gained about 3.1%.** Reuters reported Nvidia "added nearly 3%" while the quote widget on the same page showed 2.28% — the spread is a timing difference. The whole chip complex moved together on the Anthropic filing, with Marvell, Broadcom and Micron up between 2.2% and 3.3%.

- **Two defense contractors rose on analyst upgrades: Lockheed Martin (ticker LMT) gained 3% after Morgan Stanley upgraded it to overweight, and CACI International (ticker CACI) rose 6% after Jefferies upgraded it to buy from hold.** Lockheed's upgrade followed a deal to keep producing F-35 fighter jets for the Defense Department.

- **Maplebear (ticker CART), the company that operates Instacart, fell almost 6%.** The Information reported Friday that it has been exploring a sale, including early-stage discussions with DoorDash and Uber. Note the direction: a company that might be sold usually rises. Falling on that news suggests investors read it as a sign of weakness rather than an imminent premium.

- **The AI "neocloud" stocks fell early and then recovered.** Elon Musk posted on Sunday that debt markets have "almost entirely shut off" lending to companies without substantial revenue independent of AI data centers, and that "the 200+ neoclouds are going to see guppy-level death and consolidation." CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN — companies that rent out AI computing capacity — fell between roughly 2% and 5% before the open, and CNBC reported they turned positive later in the day. The one-month damage is the real story: CoreWeave and Nebius are down more than 30% and IREN about 40%.

- **Brazil's market collapsed, and U.S. investors felt it through an ETF.** The iShares MSCI Brazil ETF (ticker EWZ), which holds a basket of large Brazilian stocks and trades in New York, fell roughly 20% to 22% intraday after President Lula won the runoff against Senator Flávio Bolsonaro; premarket prints were as bad as 27%. Brazil's Ibovespa index closed down somewhere between 13.4% and 13.9% depending on the source, having been halted for 30 minutes after falling 10% at the open. I could not verify EWZ's official closing price from the sources available to me, so I am giving you the intraday range rather than inventing a close. The cause was fiscal: investors had priced in a government that would cut spending, and the election result removed that expectation in a single night.

## Headlines that matter

**Anthropic filed publicly for an initial public offering, and this was the day's engine.** One disclosure first: I am Claude, made by Anthropic, so you should weigh my framing of this story accordingly. I have stuck to what outside sources and the company's own filing say. Anthropic filed a Form S-1 with the Securities and Exchange Commission and intends to list its Class A shares on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker **ANT**, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading the offering. The share count and price range have not been set. The filing disclosed revenue of about **$16.3 billion for the first six months of 2026**, against $4.6 billion for all of 2025. Compute and infrastructure — the cost of renting and running the computers — was $8.95 billion in the half, about 55% of revenue. Reports citing a Wall Street Journal review say roughly 20% of revenue came from just two customers. Reported target valuations cluster around $2 trillion with a listing possibly in mid-November, but I could not confirm any valuation figure from the filing itself, and the filing leaves the price blank. Why this matters beyond one company: for three weeks the market's central worry has been that the AI build-out is being funded with debt that lenders are losing their appetite for. An IPO is the opposite of debt — it is permanent money from stock buyers who cannot demand it back. A filing of this size says that channel is still open, and that is why chip stocks, not just AI software, rallied on the news.

**The Federal Reserve meets tomorrow and Wednesday, and the expected path is up, not down.** The decision comes Wednesday at 2:00 p.m. Eastern. Markets put the odds of no change at roughly 84% to 93%, holding the target range at 3.75% to 4.00%. The real question is December: one survey of 100 economists by Reuters found that all 100 expect an increase by the December 8–9 meeting, and September's Fed minutes showed most officials thought one more increase would likely be appropriate by year-end. Why it matters: a rate *hike* raises the return on holding cash and bonds, which mathematically lowers what investors should pay today for a company's future profits. This is the gravity pulling against every AI rally, and it is why a 10-year Treasury yield near 5% keeps showing up in market commentary.

**Brazil's election result was a genuine macro shock, and it is a useful reminder about a kind of risk that has nothing to do with earnings.** Lula's win over Flávio Bolsonaro reversed what investors had assumed about Brazil's fiscal direction; Bolsonaro refused to concede and said he would seek annulment of the result. Banks and oil companies led the decline. Why it matters for you: this is *political* or *sovereign* risk — the risk that a government changes the rules under which companies operate. No Brazilian company reported bad earnings today. The businesses are the same as they were Friday. The price people will pay to own them changed by a fifth in a day because the expected policy environment changed.

**Meta Platforms said it plans to raise up to $95 billion in equity to fund its AI expansion.** This is the continuation of the story from last week, when Meta announced a new class of non-voting shares and fell 7%. Why it matters: $95 billion of new stock is not a rounding error. When a company issues new shares, existing owners' slices get thinner — the opposite of the buybacks I taught you about earlier this month. It also tells you the scale of money the AI build-out still needs.

**Oil retreated from six-month highs on Iran diplomacy, but the risk premium has not gone away.** Brent finished last week up 6% and approached $117 at its high. Today's decline came on signals that immediate U.S. military action against Iran was not planned. Houthi claims of strikes on Saudi pipeline pumping stations and continued attention on the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes — kept a floor under prices. Why it matters: energy prices feed directly into inflation, and inflation is what is making the Fed consider another hike. The oil market and the Fed are the same story right now.

**This is the biggest earnings week of the quarter.** Microsoft and Alphabet report Wednesday after the close, and Amazon reports Thursday. Those three companies are both the largest buyers of AI infrastructure and among the largest weights in the S&P 500, which means their capital-spending plans move the chip stocks and their share prices move the index. Apple is expected the following Monday, November 2.

## Today's lesson: a loss and losing money are not the same thing

Today's biggest story came with a pair of numbers that look impossible side by side, and learning to reconcile them will protect you from being fooled by headlines for the rest of your investing life. This is a *fundamental analysis* lesson — we are reading the business's own financial statements rather than its price chart.

Here are the two numbers. Anthropic's filing shows revenue of about **$16.3 billion** for the first half of 2026 and a **net loss of about $80.7 billion** for the same six months. Read that again: a loss roughly five times the size of all the money that came in. If a company only had $16.3 billion of revenue, how could it possibly lose $80.7 billion? It could not — not in cash. And the filing explains why. Of that loss, **$72.4 billion** is a line item called "change in fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities, and SAFEs," which the filing says was driven primarily by an increase in the fair value of its outstanding convertible promissory notes. Meanwhile the company's **operating loss** for the half was about **$693 million**, and one source reports it actually made $974 million of operating income in the second quarter alone.

So let me define the terms once, from the top of the income statement down. **Revenue** is money coming in from customers. Subtract the costs of actually running the business — compute, salaries, research — and you get **operating income** or **operating loss**: the scoreboard for the business as a business. Then come all the financial items that are not about operations: interest, taxes, and accounting adjustments. After those you get **net income** or **net loss**, the famous "bottom line." A **non-cash charge** is a subtraction on that journey where no money left the building. A **convertible note** is money an investor lends that can later convert into shares instead of being repaid. Accounting rules require the company to re-measure what that obligation is worth each period and book the change as a gain or loss. Here is the part that trips everyone up: *when the company becomes more valuable, the shares those notes convert into become more valuable, so the obligation gets bigger, so the company books a loss.* The $72.4 billion "loss" is largely a mathematical consequence of the company's valuation going up. No cash moved. This is why the net loss can be a number that makes no physical sense while the operating line is a rounding error by comparison.

I will be honest about the edge of what I can verify: $80.7 billion minus $72.4 billion is $8.3 billion, which is not $693 million, so there are other items — taxes and further remeasurements — that I could not reconcile from the sources I read without the full filing in front of me. One source puts total non-operating expense at $80.0 billion. The lesson does not depend on closing that gap. The lesson is that the direction and the magnitude of the headline number were set almost entirely by something that is not a business result at all.

The basketball version fits unusually well here. Imagine you draft a player on a cheap rookie contract, and over one season he becomes an All-Star. Now suppose the league made you write down, as a loss on your books, the increase in what it would cost to buy out or extend that contract at his new market value. Your "books" would show a catastrophic loss, and the cause of that loss would be that your young player got *great*. No money left your account. Your actual operations — tickets, ads, payroll — barely moved. Anyone reading only the bottom line would think the franchise was collapsing, when what actually happened is that the thing you own got more valuable. The habit to build is simple: when you see a startling loss, do not stop at the bottom line. Walk up the income statement to the operating line, look for the words "non-cash" and "change in fair value," and ask the only question that matters — *did money actually leave, or did an accountant re-measure something?* It cuts both ways, too: companies can report cheerful net income that is propped up by one-time non-cash gains. The operating line is where the business lives.

To be explicit about what this lesson is and is not: it is a tool for reading a financial statement. It is not a judgment about whether any company is a good or bad investment, and nothing here is a suggestion to buy or sell anything. A $693 million operating loss is still a loss, concentration of 20% of revenue in two customers is a real risk the filing itself names, and a company that spends 55% of its revenue on compute has a cost structure no one has seen before. Those are the numbers worth arguing about. The $80.7 billion is mostly arithmetic.
