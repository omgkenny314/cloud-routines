# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, November 17, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today the market fell again, but the *order* of the falling changed, and that is the story. For two weeks the pattern has been Nasdaq worst, Dow least damaged. Today it flipped completely: the Dow was the worst of the three, down 1.64%, while the Nasdaq lost only 0.74%. The chips stopped bleeding and the software companies got taken apart instead. Salesforce fell nearly 10% and was the single worst stock in the S&P 500, ServiceNow and Intuit fell 6% to 7%, and the main software exchange-traded fund dropped 3.4% — its fifth decline of 3% or more since mid-October. The reason given is uncomfortable and somewhat circular: Anthropic prices its initial public offering tomorrow night, and investors are selling the established software companies on the theory that a very well-funded artificial-intelligence company with freshly raised public money is coming for their customers. Layered on top were a run of high-profile technology outages that made people think harder about software reliability, Home Depot beating its quarter and getting sold anyway, and a stack of Federal Reserve officials saying out loud that inflation is not behaving and more rate *hikes* are probably coming. And all of it was really just waiting room: Nvidia reported after the close tonight, its first quarterly report since its founder resigned. The S&P 500 finished at its lowest close since late July. Then, half an hour after the bell, Nvidia delivered revenue of $115.8 billion and guided the current quarter above expectations, and the stock rose — which means tomorrow may look nothing like today.

## Big movers and why

**Salesforce (CRM), down about 9.7% to roughly $194.09 — the worst performer in the S&P 500 and the biggest drag on the Dow.** Salesforce sells customer-relationship software to businesses. There was no bad news *from the company*. There were two external reasons. The first is competitive fear: Anthropic, an artificial-intelligence company, prices its initial public offering tomorrow night, and the market is increasingly treating general-purpose AI as a direct threat to companies that sell specialized business software. The second is a run of high-profile technology outages — reported to have affected airlines, banks and Microsoft's Office products, with a Microsoft 365 disruption last week taking down Outlook, Teams and Copilot for more than a day. Be careful with that second explanation: one report noted plainly that **no public link has been established** between those outages and the software companies whose shares fell. That is sentiment, not a confirmed cause, and it is worth knowing the difference.

**ServiceNow (NOW), down about 6% to 7%,** and **Intuit (INTU), down about 5% to 6%.** Same story, no company-specific news. One report put both among the five worst performers in the Russell 1000, an index of 1,000 large and mid-sized U.S. companies.

**The software group as a whole.** The iShares Expanded Tech-Software ETF (IGV), a basket of software companies, fell 3.4%. That was its fifth drop of 3% or more since October 16. When an entire industry basket moves that hard that often, you are watching the market reprice a *theme*, not react to any one business.

The company also named its chief financial officer, Richard McPhail, as its next chief executive, effective November 23.

## Headlines that matter

**October retail sales rose 0.2%.** The Census Bureau's advance report, released this morning, put retail and food-services sales at $748.2 billion, up 0.2% from September and up 6.8% from a year earlier, in line with forecasts and down from September's 0.8% jump. Underneath: motor vehicles and parts fell 1.6%, furniture fell 2.3%, gasoline stations rose 1.5%, and miscellaneous retailers rose 4.7%. *Why it matters:* two details matter more than the headline. First, the Census Bureau itself said the 0.2% gain **is not statistically different from zero** — the government is telling you its own number is inside the margin of error, which is a useful habit of mind to borrow. Second, and more important for markets, the "control group" — a narrower measure that strips out cars, gas, building materials and food services, and which feeds directly into the government's calculation of economic growth — rose 0.6% against a 0.4% forecast. So the headline looked soft and the underlying consumer looked firm. A firm consumer makes it easier for the Fed to keep raising rates, which is the opposite of what stocks wanted.

**Federal Reserve officials spent the day talking about higher rates.** Four spoke. New York Fed President John Williams said the economy is strong and strengthening but that inflation is not heading in the right direction and the Fed still has more to do to get it back to 2%. St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem said the Fed likely needs *several* rate hikes by spring to bring inflation under control in a timely way. Governor Christopher Waller spoke about payments and digital finance and deliberately did not address policy; Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack argued the Fed should set clear principles for intervening in the bond market during calm periods, and also avoided the policy outlook. *Why it matters:* the Fed's target range is 3.75% to 4%, and markets now put the odds of a quarter-point **hike** at the December 8–9 meeting somewhere around 90%, with Waller having previously floated that a half-point increase "should be considered" if core inflation ran hot. "Several hikes by spring" from a sitting Fed president is a materially more hawkish statement than what is currently priced in. Higher rates raise the return available on perfectly safe cash and bonds, which lowers what any investor will rationally pay for profits a company might earn years from now — and that falls hardest on exactly the long-dated growth stories leading this market.

**Anthropic's initial public offering prices tomorrow night.** The artificial-intelligence company plans to sell 825 million Class A shares — about 801.4 million new shares plus 23.6 million from existing holders — at $70 to $78 each, with pricing expected Wednesday evening and trading beginning Thursday, November 19, on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. On shares outstanding that implies roughly $917 billion to $1.02 trillion of value; on a fully diluted basis, reports put it as high as $1.405 trillion, against the $965 billion valuation from a funding round in May. The order book is reported as covered and by one account six times oversubscribed, with some reports suggesting pricing above the range — that last part is unconfirmed. *Why it matters:* this is the clearest link between today's two biggest facts. The money that buys a very large new listing has to come from somewhere, and some of it is being raised by selling the incumbent software companies that the new entrant competes with. That is a real mechanism, not a mood. Also note that earlier reporting had major investors arguing a $1.5 trillion valuation would need to come down after the AI selloff — so the price set tomorrow night is itself a piece of information about how much appetite is left.

**A run of technology outages is now a market factor.** Reported disruptions affected airlines, banks, the National Security Agency and Microsoft's products, including a Microsoft 365 outage last week that hit Outlook, Teams and Copilot for over a day. *Why it matters:* when the plumbing of corporate software visibly fails, buyers start asking about reliability and security rather than features, and that conversation tends to slow purchasing decisions. But hold this one loosely — reporting explicitly said the outages have not been attributed to AI-related attacks and that no public link has been established to the stocks that fell.

**The Nasdaq-100 is heading for its worst November since 2008.** *Why it matters:* 2008 is not a comparison anyone makes casually. It is a statement about the *month*, not a prediction, and the index remains up for the year — but it tells you the scale of the repricing going on in large-cap technology right now.

## After the bell

**Nvidia (NVDA) reported, and the numbers were strong.** Results for the quarter ended October 25, released just after the close:

- **Revenue of $115.8 billion**, up 103% from a year ago and 20% from the prior quarter. That beat both the company's own guidance of $108 billion and the roughly $109 billion analysts expected.
- **Data center revenue of $107.6 billion, up 110%** from the same quarter last year. This is the part that is really Nvidia now — selling the chips and systems that fill artificial-intelligence data centers.
- **Earnings of $2.70 per share** on standard accounting rules and $2.68 adjusted, against consensus near $2.47 to $2.49. **Net income was $65.3 billion.**
- **Gross margin of 74.8%**, slightly better than the roughly 74% it had guided to. Gross margin is the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of making the product.
- **Guidance for the current quarter: about $130 billion, plus or minus 3%** — above the roughly $125 billion analysts were carrying. Two important asterisks the headline will not give you: that quarter contains an **extra week**, so it is not a clean comparison, and the guidance **assumes no data-center compute revenue from China at all**.
- **Gross margin guided down to 71% to 72%** for the current quarter, from 74.8% today. That is the one number in this report that is moving the wrong way, and memory-chip costs are the usual suspect.
- The company said it returned **$28.1 billion to shareholders** through buybacks and dividends during the quarter. One report also said the board added $150 billion to the buyback authorization; only a single source carried that, so treat it as unconfirmed.

**The stock rose on the news.** I want to be honest about the limits here: it is very early, the figures I have are from live blogs rather than settled quotes, and one live-blog headline put the gain near 4%. Treat the direction as reliable and the size as not yet verified. Remember also the backdrop — the stock came into tonight down roughly 30% from its October high, so a good report is landing on a badly beaten-up price.

The earnings call began at 5:00 p.m. Eastern, hosted by chief financial officer Colette Kress in her role as interim chief executive — the first call since founder Jensen Huang resigned. ## Today's lesson: a beat is not a bar, it is a gap — and the gap that matters is in front of you

Tonight's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning we study the business itself, its sales and costs and outlook, rather than the behavior of its stock chart. The concept is **the difference between results and guidance**, and Home Depot handed us an almost perfect teaching case: it beat on nearly everything that already happened, and the stock fell anyway.

First, two pieces of vocabulary, defined once. **Consensus** is the average of professional analysts' forecasts for a company's sales and earnings — it is the bar the company is being measured against, and it is set by other people, not by the company. **Guidance** is what the company itself says to expect in the future; most large companies give it, and most update it every quarter. A "beat" means results came in above consensus. Here is the thing almost everyone gets wrong: a beat is not an absolute achievement, it is a *gap* between what happened and what was already expected. And since today's price already has today's expectations baked into it, closing that gap is worth very little. What moves a stock is the gap that is still in front of it.

Now look at Home Depot's two halves side by side. **The quarter that happened:** sales of $43.4 billion, up 4.9% and above the roughly $42.78 billion expected. Comparable sales — sales at stores open at least a year, which strips out the growth you get simply from opening new stores — up 2.2%, reported as the best in four years. Earnings per share up year over year on both standard and adjusted accounting. That is a good quarter by any fair reading. **The year the company now expects:** full-year comparable sales growth cut to about 1.4%, down from an earlier range of flat to 2%. Operating margin expectations trimmed. Adjusted earnings per share growing only about 2% from $14.69. And one report noted the implied fourth quarter sits solidly below the roughly $2.81 analysts had been carrying.

Sit with that combination, because it is strange and instructive. The best comparable sales in four years, and the company simultaneously *lowered* what it expects for the full year. There are only a few ways that arithmetic works, and all of them say something about the months ahead rather than the months behind. Notice too that the beat's *composition* mattered: the strong quarter arrived with a thinner margin, meaning more of the sales growth was bought rather than earned — through pricing, promotion or cost pressure. Growth you pay for is worth less than growth that drops to the bottom line, and the market is quite good at making that distinction even when a headline says "beat."

Here is the basketball version, and I think it is exact. A team trades for a veteran coming off a 28-point night. The 28 points are real, verifiable, already in the box score — and they are worth almost nothing to the trade, because everyone saw them and the asking price already reflects them. What changes the deal is the medical report and the minutes projection: what this player will do over the next 60 games. Home Depot's quarter was the 28-point night. The guidance cut was the medical report. The market looked straight past the box score. So when you read that a company "beat earnings" and the stock dropped, you have not found a mispricing or an irrational market — you have almost certainly found a company whose past was better than its future, and your next question should always be the same one: *what did they say about next quarter?*
