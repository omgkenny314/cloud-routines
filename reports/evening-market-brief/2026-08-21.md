# Market Close Brief — Friday, August 21, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Stocks bounced back on Friday, but it was a rescue mission, not a victory lap. All three major indexes rose — the Dow led with a gain of about 518 points — yet every one of them still finished the week lower, because Monday through Thursday had been rough enough that one good Friday could not undo it. The single biggest storyline of both the day and the week was the bond market. Long-term U.S. government borrowing costs have been climbing, and on Wednesday Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent stepped in with an unusual move: he doubled the size of the government's bond buybacks, from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation, to try to push those costs back down. It worked for about a day. By Thursday and Friday, yields had climbed most of the way back, which told investors something uncomfortable — the government tried, and the market shrugged. Underneath that, the actual U.S. economy looked strong: a closely watched business survey released Friday morning showed private-sector activity growing at its fastest pace in more than four years. And off to the side, bitcoin had an enormous week. That is the day in a sentence: a good economy, a nervous bond market, and a relief rally that did not quite cover the damage.

## The numbers

**S&P 500 — closed at 7,674.37, up 0.43%.** A broad rebound after Thursday's 0.9% drop. Strong economic data and calmer bond trading gave buyers room, but the index still lost ground for the week.

**Dow Jones Industrial Average — closed at 53,277.01, up 517.80 points, or 0.98%.** The strongest of the three, and the only one whose gain you could call decisive. Even so, it booked a second straight weekly loss.

**Nasdaq Composite — closed at 26,180.45, up 0.43%.** Technology stocks recovered in line with the market rather than leading it. High long-term interest rates weigh especially on fast-growing tech companies, and that pressure did not lift this week.

**10-year Treasury yield — around 4.71%.** This is the interest rate the U.S. government pays to borrow money for ten years, and it is the reference point for mortgage rates and corporate borrowing. It reached about 4.75% earlier in the week, a 20-month high, dipped on the buyback news, then climbed back. The 30-year yield sat near 5.25%, having given back nearly all of Wednesday's decline.

**Crude oil — West Texas Intermediate at about $86.30 a barrel, down roughly 0.55% on the day; Brent crude at about $93.40, down about 0.3%.** West Texas Intermediate is the U.S. benchmark grade of oil; Brent is the international one. Both slipped slightly Friday but posted a second straight weekly gain — Brent up more than 6% on the week, WTI up about 5.3% — because the U.S.–Iran conflict continues to choke off Middle East supply.

**Bitcoin — trading in the $77,000 to $79,000 range late in the U.S. afternoon, up roughly 6% to 7% on the day and about 22% on the week.** That was its best week since March 2024, and it touched its highest level since May. Note that bitcoin trades 24 hours a day and has no official closing price, so the exact figure depends on the moment you check it. The rally had three fuels: President Trump publicly pushing Congress to pass the CLARITY Act, a crypto-regulation bill the industry wants; the Treasury buyback news making risky assets more appealing; and a cascade of forced buying as traders betting against bitcoin were squeezed out of their positions.

## Big movers and why

**Moderna (ticker MRNA) — closed at $145.13, up 8.86%.** Moderna was the stock of the week, and its week was violent. On Wednesday it rose about 177% after its personalized mRNA cancer vaccine, developed with Merck, succeeded in a late-stage trial — the treatment helped block the return or spread of melanoma, a serious skin cancer, across a study of more than 1,000 patients. On Thursday it gave back a large chunk as traders took profits. Friday it climbed again, at one point up more than 9%. When a single event changes what a company might be worth by that much, the market does not find the new price in one day. It argues about it for a week.

**Merck (ticker MRK) — up about 3.4%.** Merck is Moderna's partner on that same cancer vaccine. The move is far smaller for a simple reason: Merck is an enormous, diversified drugmaker with many products, so one breakthrough is a meaningful addition rather than a transformation. Moderna is much smaller and far more dependent on this one program.

**Robinhood Markets (ticker HOOD) — up roughly 12%.** The trading app rose on a combination of its push into private-market and tokenized investing products, momentum in its international crypto business, and a raised price target from Goldman Sachs. Reported figures ranged from about 12% to nearly 14% depending on when they were captured during the session; roughly 12.4% is the best close figure I could confirm.

**Coinbase Global (ticker COIN) — up about 8.1%.** The crypto exchange rose with bitcoin. Coinbase earns fees on trading volume, so a 22% week in bitcoin is directly good for its revenue, which is why it tends to move more than bitcoin itself in both directions.

**BJ's Wholesale Club (ticker BJ) — up about 4%.** The warehouse retailer beat expectations for its second quarter: adjusted earnings of $1.36 per share on revenue of $6.09 billion, against analyst forecasts of $1.17 per share on $5.97 billion. It also raised its full-year earnings guidance to a range of $4.60 to $4.80 per share, up from $4.40 to $4.60.

**Ross Stores (ticker ROST) — up more than 4%.** The off-price retailer reported Thursday after the close and the stock rose Friday in reaction. Earnings came in at $2.66 per share against an estimate near $1.94, on revenue of about $6.3 billion. The company raised full-year guidance to $8.61 to $8.77 per share, though it noted roughly 60 cents of that reflects tariff refunds rather than ongoing operations.

## Headlines that matter

**The Treasury's bond buybacks, and the market's verdict.** On Wednesday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced the government would more than double its buybacks of long-dated U.S. debt — bonds maturing in 10 to 30 years — from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation. A buyback means the Treasury repurchases its own outstanding bonds, adding a buyer to the market and, in theory, pushing prices up and yields down. Yields did fall Wednesday. By Thursday they had reversed, and by Friday the 30-year had nearly erased the whole move. Why this matters: long-term yields are the price of money for the entire economy. When the Treasury Secretary personally intervenes and the effect lasts a day and a half, investors read it as a sign that the pressure on yields comes from something bigger than a temporary shortage of buyers — namely the sheer volume of government debt being issued. That worry is what has been sitting on stock valuations all week.

**U.S. business activity hit a four-year high.** S&P Global's flash purchasing managers' index for August came in at 56.0, up from 54.5 in July, the highest since April 2022. A purchasing managers' index, or PMI, is a monthly survey of business managers; any reading above 50 means activity is expanding, and the further above 50, the faster. The services side jumped to 56.8 from 54.6, a 20-month high, and hiring rose at its strongest pace since January 2025 while price pressures eased. Manufacturing was the soft spot, slipping to 53.2 from 53.9. Why this matters: it is a genuinely strong reading, and it cuts both ways. Growth is good for corporate earnings. But an economy running hot gives the Federal Reserve less reason to cut interest rates — which is part of why bond yields have not come down.

**Trump escalates against Iran.** The President declared what he called "economic warfare" and warned of an "economic D-Day," promising the "most crushing economic operation ever taken against any country," including penalties on any nation helping Iran evade sanctions. The stated goal is to force Iran to end its nuclear program and fully reopen the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow shipping channel that a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes through. The United Arab Emirates, one of Iran's most important trading partners, suspended all trade and financial dealings with Tehran. Why this matters: this is the reason oil has now risen two weeks running. Higher oil feeds into inflation, and inflation feeds back into the interest-rate picture. The bond story and the Iran story are not separate stories.

**The Fed, and the wait for Jackson Hole.** There was no Federal Reserve policy news Friday, and that is itself the point. Chair Kevin Warsh, who took office in May, has deliberately stopped the practice of signaling the Fed's intentions in advance. That makes his keynote at the Kansas City Fed's Jackson Hole symposium — the annual central-banking conference in Wyoming, running August 27 to 29, with his speech Friday morning, August 28 — unusually consequential. It will be his first Jackson Hole address as Chair, and it lands 19 days before the September 16 policy meeting. Note that market expectations here have been unusual: with the economy strong and inflation not fully tamed, futures markets have at times priced a September rate *increase* as more likely than a cut. I could not verify a precise probability figure as of Friday's close, so treat the direction as the point rather than any specific number.

**Gold quietly had a big week.** Gold futures rose about 2% Friday to roughly $4,661.70 an ounce, a three-month high, and gained nearly 5% on the week. Gold pays no interest, so it typically struggles when yields rise. Rising anyway, alongside rising yields, suggests investors are buying it specifically as insurance against U.S. debt and dollar concerns. That is worth noticing.

## After the bell

The after-hours session has been quiet, and there is a straightforward reason: Friday evening is the emptiest slot on the earnings calendar. Companies overwhelmingly report before the Monday-to-Thursday open or right after the Monday-to-Thursday close, because reporting on a Friday night buries the news over a weekend.

As of early Friday evening I found no notable after-hours earnings reports or significant post-close stock moves, and no major companies are scheduled to report tonight. Friday's earnings news came in the morning rather than the evening: BJ's Wholesale Club reported before the open, as did Ubiquiti (ticker UI), the networking-equipment maker, which posted fiscal fourth-quarter adjusted earnings of $4.73 per share against $3.54 a year earlier.

If anything meaningful crosses the wire later tonight, it would be unusual. The real after-hours action resumes Monday.

## Today's lesson: support and resistance, and the level the bond market keeps bumping into

Our last two lessons were fundamental ones — operating margin, and dilution. Today let's do a technical one, because Friday handed us an unusually clean example.

**Support and resistance** are two of the oldest ideas in technical analysis, and the concept is simpler than the vocabulary suggests. A **resistance level** is a price that an asset keeps trying to rise above and keeps failing to hold. A **support level** is a price it keeps falling toward and keeps bouncing off of. They are not laws of physics. They are levels where enough buyers or sellers have shown up before that people expect them to show up again — and that expectation becomes part of what makes it happen.

Here is the basketball version. You are driving to the basket and there is a defender who has stopped you at the free-throw line three possessions in a row. Nothing physically prevents you from getting past him. But you start pulling up for the jumper instead of driving, because you have learned what happens at that spot on the floor. Other players watch and start doing the same thing. That spot on the court is now a real feature of the game, created entirely out of what everyone remembers happening there. That is resistance.

Now look at this week's bond market, where the roles are reversed in an interesting way. The 10-year Treasury yield pushed up to about 4.75% early in the week — a 20-month high, meaning it had not been that high since late 2024. That level acted like a ceiling. On Wednesday, when the Treasury announced doubled buybacks, the yield dropped away from it. By Friday it had climbed back to about 4.71%, right up under the ceiling again. The 30-year did nearly the same thing around 5.25%. Twice this week the market approached those levels, retreated, and came back.

What makes this a genuinely useful example rather than a chart-reading curiosity is that you can see the human mechanism directly. The Treasury Secretary announced a specific, concrete action explicitly intended to push yields down, and the effect faded within about a day and a half. That is a level being tested and holding. When a market absorbs real news pointed in one direction and ends up back where it started, the level itself is telling you something about where the weight of opinion sits. Traders watching those figures will be paying close attention to whether the 10-year decisively breaks above 4.75% next week, because a clean break through a level that has held repeatedly often brings in a fresh wave of activity — the defender finally got beat, and now everyone drives.

Two cautions worth carrying with you. First, support and resistance are descriptive, not predictive — they tell you where the argument has been happening, not who is going to win it. Second, the levels are approximate. Anyone who tells you resistance is at exactly 4.7500% is selling you precision that does not exist. Think of it as a zone, the way a defender guards an area rather than standing on a painted dot.
