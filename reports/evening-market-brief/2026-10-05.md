# Market Close Brief — Monday, October 5, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a tug-of-war between two markets that disagreed with each other, and the stock market won. On one side, the bond market had a rough day: the 10-year Treasury yield climbed to its highest level since April 2002, and the 30-year went to its highest since 2002 as well. Normally that is exactly the kind of thing that knocks stocks down, because higher yields make safe bonds more attractive and make companies' future profits worth less in today's money. On the other side, technology shares simply ignored it. The Nasdaq Composite closed at a record high, Nvidia hit an all-time high, and the market shrugged. The reason stocks could ignore the bond market is the hangover from Friday's weak jobs report, which made another Federal Reserve rate increase look much less likely — and that view survived today intact. Underneath all of that, the day's most eye-catching news was a burst of corporate dealmaking: two large acquisitions were announced before the open, and the market's verdict on them was brutally lopsided. The companies being bought soared. The companies doing the buying got punished. That contrast is the single biggest storyline of the day, and it is also today's lesson.

## The numbers

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 51,267.90, up 90.94 points, or 0.18%.** The clear laggard. The Dow holds only 30 companies, leans toward older industrial and financial names, and carries less of the big technology that led today.

At one point today it jumped about 7 basis points to 5.349%, the highest since April 3, 2002. The 30-year reached 5.70%, also a 2002 high. The driver was inflation worry buried inside today's services data (see below), plus nerves ahead of Wednesday's Fed minutes.

Treat the direction as modestly lower and the exact level as approximate.

## Big movers and why

- **PTC Inc. (PTC), up about 33.6% to roughly $192.49.** This was the day's headline move. Schneider Electric, the French industrial and energy-management company, agreed to buy PTC — a maker of engineering and product-design software — for **$205 a share in cash**, valuing PTC's equity at about $22.6 billion. That price is a 42.3% premium to Friday's close of $144.03. It is Schneider's largest acquisition ever, and the logic is artificial intelligence: Schneider wants PTC's industrial software to sit alongside its data-center and electrical equipment business. Schneider expects about $250 million in annual cost savings by the third year and roughly $800 million in added revenue.
- **Schneider Electric itself fell sharply.** The buyer is funding this with roughly €5 billion to €6 billion of newly issued stock and €16 billion to €17 billion of new debt, backed by a bridge loan from Morgan Stanley and Société Générale. Its own shareholders did not like paying a 42% premium and taking on that much debt to do it.
- **RXO Inc. (RXO), up roughly 20%.** C.H. Robinson agreed to buy this freight-brokerage and last-mile delivery company. RXO holders get $17.25 in cash plus 0.0856 C.H. Robinson shares for each share, worth about $30.25 — a 29% premium to Friday's close.
- **C.H. Robinson Worldwide (CHRW), down about 10.9%.** The buyer, and the mirror image of RXO. The logistics company is paying an implied $5.8 billion, creating a combined business with an enterprise value above $25 billion, and betting that AI can wring efficiency out of a weak freight market — it is targeting $300 million of cost savings within two years. Investors objected to three specifics: about 43% of the price is being paid in C.H. Robinson stock, which dilutes existing owners; the cash half requires new debt that pushes leverage above the company's own target range; and share buybacks are suspended until leverage comes back down, which the company does not expect until the end of 2028. It traded down as much as 12.7% intraday.
- **Nvidia (NVDA), up about 2% to roughly $238.30, an all-time high.** The chipmaker is now up about 28% for 2026 and its market value is approaching $6 trillion. There was no single new announcement; this was the AI trade reasserting itself, and because Nvidia is so enormous it was a major contributor to both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq's record.
- **Moderna (MRNA), up roughly 6% to 7%.** Nasdaq said the biotechnology company will join the **Nasdaq-100 index** before the open on October 9, replacing Warner Bros. Discovery. This is a mechanical reason, not a business reason: a very large amount of money sits in funds that simply track that index, and those funds are now obliged to buy Moderna shares whether they like the company or not. Moderna has already risen more than 500% in 2026 as investors reframed it from a vaccine story into a cancer-treatment story.
- **Lennar (LEN), down about 7%.** The homebuilder fell as the 30-year fixed mortgage rate sat around 7.4%. Mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury yield fairly closely, so a 24-year high in that yield lands directly on housing. One caveat worth being honest about: D.R. Horton, a comparable builder, fell only about 1.1% today. Lennar dropped far more than the sector, which suggests something company-specific was also at work, and I could not verify what it was with confidence.
- **Chipotle Mexican Grill (CMG), down about 4.6%.** Two explanations were circulating and I could not establish which dominated. One is a resurgence of food-safety concern tied to reports of a multistate salmonella outbreak linked to jalapeño peppers. The other is simple profit-taking and cautious positioning ahead of its third-quarter results on October 28. McDonald's and Starbucks also slipped, which hints at some broader restaurant-sector softness rather than a purely Chipotle problem.

## Headlines that matter

**The ISM services report, and why the headline number was the least important part.** The Institute for Supply Management's services index registered 54.9 in September, down from 55.4 in August and a touch below forecasts near 55.7. Any reading above 50 means the sector is growing, so 54.9 is still expansion — the 27th consecutive month of it. That part was unremarkable. The part that moved markets was buried inside: the **prices index** rose 1.4 points to **74**, the highest since July 2022, with its 12-month average the highest since March 2023. This index measures how many purchasing managers are paying more for what they buy. Why it matters: the services sector is where most Americans work and spend, and it is the hardest place for inflation to be dislodged once it settles in. A hot prices reading there tells the bond market that inflation is not finished, which is precisely why yields rose on a day when the growth number was soft. It is a good reminder that a data release is a document, not a number.

**Why the Fed question dominates everything right now.** Remember the unusual setup: the Fed is *raising* rates, not cutting them. It lifted its benchmark to a 3.75% to 4.00% target range on September 16, its first increase since 2023, and Chair Kevin Warsh has said inflation has been too high for too long. The committee's own projections pencilled in one more increase before year-end. Friday's weak jobs report — just 29,000 jobs added, with the unemployment rate up to 4.2% — made that hike much harder to justify, and markets repriced it hard. Today stocks were still living off that repricing. The next formal checkpoint is the **minutes of the September meeting, due Wednesday, October 7 at 2 p.m. Eastern**, which will show how close the vote was and how worried individual officials are. The next actual rate decision is October 27-28.

**The bond market is the real risk to this rally.** This deserves saying plainly: equities at record highs and the long end of the Treasury market at 24-year-high yields is an uncomfortable combination. Higher government borrowing costs raise the cost of credit for everyone — mortgages, car loans, corporate refinancing — regardless of what the Fed decides. Today the stock market chose to look past it. That choice is not permanent, and the divergence is the thing to keep an eye on.

**Dealmaking is back, and it is being financed with debt.** Two multibillion-dollar acquisitions announced on the same morning, both explicitly justified by artificial intelligence, both funded substantially with borrowing. That tells you something about corporate confidence. It also tells you something about risk: companies taking on large amounts of new debt at 2002-level interest rates are making an expensive bet that the synergies arrive.

**Oil eased, which quietly helped.** Crude slipping was one reason stocks could rally. Expensive oil has been feeding the inflation that has the Fed hiking, so a softer oil price loosens that chain slightly. Supply constraints around the Strait of Hormuz remain the background worry and have kept prices historically elevated.

## Today's lesson: the gap, and the one kind that never closes

Today's lesson is a technical one — technical analysis meaning the study of price and trading behavior itself, rather than the business underneath it. And PTC handed us a textbook example.

Start with the concept. A **gap** is a jump in a stock's price with no trading in between. The stock market is only open part of the day, but news does not keep office hours. PTC finished Friday at $144.03. Over the weekend and early this morning, the Schneider Electric takeover was agreed and announced. By the time trading opened, PTC was changing hands near $198. Nobody bought or sold that stock anywhere between $144 and $198 — there was no price discovery through that range, just an empty space on the chart. That empty space is the gap.

Why technical analysts care is that most gaps eventually get **filled** — the price drifts back through that empty range later, because the move was driven by emotion or a temporary surge of buying rather than by a genuine change in what the company is worth. A basketball analogy: a team that goes up 15 points in three minutes because every shot happened to drop is probably going to give some of it back, since nothing fundamental about either roster changed. That is an ordinary gap.

But today's PTC gap is the exception that teaches the rule, and this is the part worth remembering. This gap will almost certainly never fill, because nothing emotional happened. A specific buyer signed a binding agreement to pay a specific price — $205 a share in cash — for the entire company. PTC's stock is no longer really tracking PTC's business at all. It is tracking a legal document. The old price of $144 is simply gone, the way a score correction after a video review is gone: the scoreboard did not jump because of how the game was being played, it jumped because an outside authority made a ruling.

Here is the detail I would most want you to notice, and it is the reason I am teaching gaps rather than just reporting the move. PTC did not gap all the way to $205. It opened near $198, drifted down, and closed at $192.49 — about 6.5% below the agreed price. If someone is definitely going to pay $205, why would anyone sell at $192.49? Because "definitely" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The deal is not expected to close until the third quarter of 2027, nearly a year away. Antitrust regulators have to approve it. Financing has to hold together. PTC's own shareholders have to vote yes. That roughly 6.5% shortfall is the market pricing two things at once: the risk the deal collapses, and the fact that money you receive a year from now is worth less than money in your hand today. That shortfall is called the **deal spread**, and reading it is how professionals tell you, in a single number, how confident the market is that a merger will actually happen. A spread that narrows over the coming months means confidence rising. A spread that suddenly widens means somebody smells trouble.
