# Market Close Brief — Monday, September 21, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a risk-on day, and an unusually clean one: almost everything that has been scaring this market eased at the same time. Oil fell for a fourth straight session and dropped back below $100 a barrel, Treasury yields came off last week's 19-year high, and with those two pressures lifting, money poured straight back into the artificial-intelligence trade. The Nasdaq Composite jumped 2.26% to a record close — its first record since June — the S&P 500 gained about 1.5%, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average, which owns far less technology, tagged along with a 0.71% rise. The single biggest storyline is what set the chip rally off: Meta Platforms' new personal AI assistant, Muse, climbed to the top of Apple's U.S. App Store, and traders read that as the first hard evidence that consumers will actually use these agents at scale. If they do, someone has to buy an enormous number of processors to answer all those requests. Advanced Micro Devices touched a $1 trillion market value for the first time, Intel had one of its best days in years, and the whole semiconductor complex went with them. The counterweight came from Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee, who spent the day warning that inflation may now be coming from strong demand and not just from oil and tariffs — which would argue for higher rates, not lower. Traders nudged the odds of another rate increase in October up to about 53%. So: a very good day for stocks, on a Fed backdrop that is not getting any friendlier.

## The numbers

**S&P 500: 7,764.70, up about 114 points, or 1.49%.** Broad strength led by technology and communication services. As an internal check, Friday's close of 7,650.50 plus today's gain lands exactly on 7,764.70.

**Dow Jones Industrial Average: 52,048.83, up 366.19 points, or 0.71%.** A solid day, but a third of the Nasdaq's gain. The Dow holds only 30 companies and is light on the chipmakers that did the heavy lifting.

**Nasdaq Composite: 27,122.09, up 599.55 points, or 2.26%.** A record close, the first since June. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index rose between 3% and 3.9% depending on the source, its fifth straight winning session.

**10-year Treasury yield: about 4.967%, down roughly 3 to 4 basis points.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. The 2-year fell about 1 basis point to 4.729% and the 30-year fell about 3 basis points to 5.306%. Yields fell because oil fell — cheaper energy means less inflation pressure — and because global borrowing costs eased broadly. Last week the 10-year touched 5.041%, a 19-year high, so this is a step back from the edge rather than an all-clear. One source quoted the 10-year as low as 4.93% intraday; 4.967% is the figure two separate sources agreed on.

**Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate settled near $97.56, down about 2.7%; Brent near $101.94, down about 1.9%.** I saw WTI quotes ranging from $97.50 to $98.27 and percentage drops from 2% to 3.9%, so treat the exact settle as approximate — but $97.56 is the one that reconciles precisely with Friday's $100.30 settle, and $101.94 reconciles with Friday's Brent close of $103.87. This was the fourth consecutive decline and an 11-day low. Oil fell because President Trump signaled he would be willing to meet Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian at this week's United Nations General Assembly, and because Saudi exports have recovered above 4 million barrels a day after dropping to roughly 2.4 million in August.

**Bitcoin: roughly $85,000 to $86,000, up about 5% to 6% over 24 hours.** It opened near $81,162, was at $85,134 by 10:07 a.m. Eastern and topped $86,000 later in the day — the highest since January. Bitcoin trades around the clock and has no official close, so take a range rather than a single number. Ethereum rose about 5.9% to roughly $2,715. The same forces lifted crypto as lifted stocks: lower oil, lower yields, and a wave of forced buying as bearish traders who had bet on lower prices were squeezed out.

## Big movers and why

A caveat before the list: several of the "biggest mover" summaries I found for today turned out, on inspection, to be recapping Friday's session. I have kept only the moves I could tie to today.

**Advanced Micro Devices (AMD)** was the headline. It hit an all-time high of $613.92 intraday and crossed $1 trillion in market value for the first time, though at least one report noted it held that mark only briefly. It is now the fourth U.S. chipmaker to reach $1 trillion, after Nvidia, Broadcom and Micron Technology. Sources put today's gain between about 7.8% at midday and as much as 10% at the peak. The stock has risen roughly 24% in five sessions and more than 180% this year.

**Intel (INTC)** was the other star, and the range of reported moves is wide: it opened up 7.2%, one close-of-day recap said 12%, and Bloomberg reported it gained more than 14% intraday and was the best performer in the S&P 500. Call it "up double digits" rather than a precise number. Three things were pushing it: the Muse-driven enthusiasm for server processors, a Tigress Financial price-target increase to $145 from $118 citing an AI-driven turnaround, and reports that SK Hynix may produce memory at Intel's Ohio plant — which would validate Intel's contract-manufacturing ambitions. Separately, Intel is reportedly planning a roughly 10% price increase on PC processors in early October.

**Arm Holdings (ARM)** rose somewhere between 11% and 17% on the same server-processor logic. Arm designs the chip architecture that much of the world's low-power computing runs on.

**Coinbase Global (COIN) rose about 11.7% and Robinhood Markets (HOOD) about 5.4%, to roughly $126.26.** Both are leveraged to crypto trading volume, so a bitcoin move of this size pulls them along. Robinhood also got help from a joint Commodity Futures Trading Commission and Securities and Exchange Commission proposal on September 17 that would set a clearer oversight framework for crypto trading.

**SanDisk (SNDK)** was added to the S&P 100 index today. Index inclusion matters mechanically: funds that track the index must buy the stock, regardless of what anyone thinks of the business.

One thing worth noticing: at least one report pointed out that **Nvidia (NVDA)** did not participate meaningfully in today's chip rally. When the biggest name in a theme sits out while the rest of the group runs, it usually means money is rotating *within* the theme rather than flooding into it — which is a different and less powerful signal than everything rising together.

I could not verify a trustworthy list of today's biggest decliners. The one list I found naming Nucor, Qualcomm and Alexandria Real Estate appears to be recapping Friday's session — Nucor's roughly 6% drop followed weak third-quarter guidance issued last Thursday evening, and Qualcomm's 5.8% slide was a Friday close. I am not going to report Friday's losses as today's.

## Headlines that matter

**Meta Platforms' Muse agent reached number one on Apple's U.S. App Store.** Muse launched September 8 and connects to a user's email, calendar, payments, health data, shopping and smart home, then carries out multi-step tasks with little supervision. Why it matters for markets, and this is the whole chain of reasoning behind today's rally: when an AI model answers a live user request, that is called *inference*, as distinct from *training*, which is the up-front work of building the model. Training is a fixed cost. Inference is a cost that scales with every single thing users ask. An agent that millions of people use daily means an enormous, recurring inference bill — and inference leans more heavily on conventional central processing units working alongside graphics chips than training does. That is why Intel and Arm moved harder than Nvidia today. Meta has said its 2026 spending on AI chips and infrastructure will top $130 billion.

**Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee warned that inflation may now have a demand problem, not just a supply problem.** Speaking at an Official Monetary and Financial Institutions Forum event, he said supply shocks — higher oil from the Iran war, tariffs — are proving more persistent than expected, and that strong spending and AI investment may be adding a second, separate source of inflation. If that is true, he said, there is "no ambiguity" about the need for higher interest rates. He also said the Fed may have to accept higher unemployment to bring inflation down. Why it matters: a supply shock is something central banks often look through, because raising rates does not produce more oil. Demand-driven inflation is the kind rate increases are actually designed to fight. Goolsbee is saying the diagnosis may be shifting toward the kind that requires action.

**Traders now put the odds of a quarter-point rate increase at the October 27-28 Fed meeting at about 53%,** per the CME FedWatch tool — up from roughly 49% on Friday. The Fed's target range is currently 3.75% to 4.00% after last Wednesday's increase. Why it matters: a genuine coin flip means every incoming data point and every Fed speech moves the number, which is why the bond market has been so twitchy.

**The Chicago Fed National Activity Index fell to -0.04 in August from an upwardly revised +0.08 in July.** This index bundles 85 separate economic indicators into one number, where zero means the economy is growing at its historical average pace and negative means slower than that. Underneath: production-related indicators contributed -0.07 (down from 0.00), sales, orders and inventories contributed 0.00 (down from +0.15), employment contributed +0.01, and personal consumption and housing contributed +0.01. The three-month average actually ticked up to +0.01 from -0.01. Why it matters: it says the economy is growing at almost exactly its normal pace — not overheating, not stalling. That is an uncomfortable reading for anyone hoping weak growth would talk the Fed out of another hike.

**President Xi Jinping arrives in Washington Wednesday for a summit with President Trump.** He lands at Joint Base Andrews Wednesday with an arrival ceremony at the White House, and the main bilateral meeting plus a state dinner follow Thursday. The agenda covers trade, Taiwan, rare earth minerals, artificial intelligence and the Iran war. Jeff Bezos, Sundar Pichai, Sam Altman, Tim Cook, Elon Musk and Jensen Huang are all expected at the dinner. Why it matters: the guest list itself was cited as a reason for today's AI optimism. More substantively, rare earth minerals and chip export rules are the two levers each side can pull against the other, and both run directly through the semiconductor supply chain that just led the market to a record.

## Today's lesson: the Relative Strength Index, or how to measure a stock against its own recent past

Recent lessons have covered gross margin, volume, the discount rate and support and resistance. Today's is a technical one — about the behavior of price itself rather than the business underneath — and AMD handed us the example: up roughly 24% in five trading sessions and more than 180% this year.

The tool for thinking about a run like that is the **Relative Strength Index**, usually just called RSI. Note the name carefully, because we covered something called "relative strength" in an earlier brief and it is a *different* idea. Relative strength compares a stock to the market — is this stock beating the S&P 500? RSI compares a stock to *itself*. It asks: over the last fourteen trading days, how much of this stock's total movement has been upward versus downward?

The arithmetic is simple in concept. Take fourteen days. Add up all the gains on the up days, add up all the losses on the down days, and express the up-portion as a number from zero to one hundred. If every one of the last fourteen days was an up day, RSI would be 100. If every one was down, it would be 0. A stock that has risen and fallen in roughly equal measure sits near 50. By long convention, a reading above 70 is called *overbought* and below 30 *oversold*. Those are just labels for "this stock has been going up (or down) far more than it has been going the other way, lately."

Here is where I have to be honest with you about the data. I looked for a current RSI reading on AMD and got three different numbers from three sources — 46.30, 44.83 and 63.5 — at least one of which was dated September 1, before this rally began. None of them can be right for tonight after a 24% week, and I am not going to quote a number I cannot stand behind. So take the concept, not the figure: a stock that rises five sessions in a row with no meaningful down days is, by construction, pushing its RSI toward the top of the scale.

Now the part that actually matters, and it is the part most people get backwards. **Overbought does not mean "about to fall."** It means "has been going up a lot." In a genuinely strong trend, RSI can sit above 70 for weeks while the stock keeps climbing, and every trader who sold at 71 watches the rest of the move from the sidelines. The basketball version: a shooter who has hit eight in a row is having a hot streak, and describing it as "unsustainable" tells you nothing about whether the ninth shot goes in. What the streak *does* tell you is that if he misses two, the change will feel dramatic — the crowd was pricing in a make. That is the real use of RSI. It measures how much good news is already in the price, so you understand why an ordinary disappointment can produce an outsized drop.

Two cautions to carry away. First, fourteen days is an arbitrary convention that has simply stuck; the number itself is not sacred. Second, RSI measures price and nothing else — it knows nothing about Meta's Muse, about inference demand, or about whether AMD's earnings will justify a trillion-dollar valuation. It is a thermometer, not a diagnosis. Pair it with the fundamental questions from earlier lessons and it is genuinely useful. Use it alone and it will tell you a stock is "overbought" right through the best part of a multi-year run.
