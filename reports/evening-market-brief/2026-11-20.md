# Market Close Brief — Friday, November 20, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a rout, and not a quiet one. The selling that has been building all week turned into the worst day of it: the Nasdaq Composite fell more than 5%, the S&P 500 lost 3.7%, and both the S&P 500 and the Dow Jones Industrial Average finished more than 10% below their early-October record highs — which puts them officially in what Wall Street calls a correction. The S&P 500 has now fallen seven sessions in a row, its longest losing streak since February 2020, and the Nasdaq has given back every bit of its gain for 2026. Reuters reported that all three indexes posted their largest weekly percentage losses since April 2025.

The single biggest storyline was Nvidia (NVDA), and it was not about its business. Reuters reported Friday, citing two people familiar with a confidential inquiry, that the Securities and Exchange Commission — the SEC, the federal agency that polices financial disclosure — is examining Nvidia's accounting practices. The stock fell roughly 11% to 12%. That landed on a company already unsettled by the departure of longtime chief executive Jensen Huang, and it landed on a market already nervous that the enormous spending on artificial-intelligence hardware has gotten ahead of itself. The second storyline fed the first: Anthropic (ANT), which went public Thursday in the largest initial public offering on record and closed its first day up 17%, gave almost all of it back on its first full day of trading, sinking about 14% and at one point touching $83.05 — a nickel above the $83 price its IPO shares were sold at. When the most anticipated new listing of the year cannot hold its opening price for 48 hours, that tells you something about who is left willing to buy.

## Headlines that matter

**The SEC is examining Nvidia's accounting (Reuters).** Why it matters: Nvidia has been the engine of this entire bull market, and the AI trade rests on believing its reported revenue and margins are a true picture of real demand. An accounting inquiry does not change a single reported number, but it puts a question mark next to all of them, and question marks are expensive when a stock is priced for perfection. Reuters could not determine when the inquiry opened or precisely what it covers. The Wall Street Journal had previously reported an SEC investigation existed without establishing its focus.

**Anthropic's IPO failed to lift the AI trade, and then failed to hold its own price.** Why it matters: an IPO is a market-sentiment thermometer. Anthropic priced at $83 — above its marketed $70-to-$78 range — raised roughly $81 billion, the largest IPO ever, and was valued near $1.5 trillion on a fully diluted basis. All of that says demand was ferocious on Wednesday. Two days later the stock is back at the offering price. The lesson is not about Anthropic's business; it is that the pool of buyers willing to pay up for AI exposure at these prices appears to be drying up.

**The Federal Reserve is expected to raise rates, not cut them.** Why it matters: this is the fact that reframes everything else. The Fed's benchmark rate sits at 3.75% to 4.00%, and CME FedWatch pricing puts the odds of a quarter-point *increase* at the December 8–9 meeting above 95%, with roughly a 10% chance of a half-point move. Governor Christopher Waller has called for aggressive action on inflation and Governor Lisa Cook has said policy is not restrictive enough. Higher interest rates hurt expensive, fast-growing companies much more than cheap, steady ones, because a growth stock's value depends on profits far in the future and higher rates make future money worth less today. That asymmetry is exactly why the Nasdaq fell 5.3% while the Dow fell 1.9%.

**Consumer sentiment improved.** Why it matters: it was the day's one genuinely encouraging number, and the market ignored it. The University of Michigan's final November sentiment index came in at 51.5, up from 48.0 in October and above the 51.0 consensus, and consumers' expectations for inflation a year out dropped to 3.7% from 4.7%. Falling inflation expectations matter because the Fed watches them closely — if people stop expecting high inflation, the case for more rate hikes weakens. Sentiment remains historically low all the same.

**Oil's slide and its cause.** Why it matters: oil is usually read as a growth signal, but this decline is a supply story — recovering Middle East export flows and rising U.S. inventories, pointing to a surplus. Cheaper energy eventually cools inflation, which is the kind of help this Fed would welcome.

## Today's lesson

Tonight's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning we study the business itself, its sales and profits, rather than the behavior of its stock chart. The concept is the **price-to-earnings ratio**, usually written P/E, and what happens when it shrinks. Today was an almost pure demonstration, because the thing that collapsed was not earnings.

The P/E ratio is simple division: the price of one share divided by the profit the company earns per share in a year. If a stock trades at $100 and earns $5 per share, its P/E is 20 — you are paying twenty dollars for each dollar of annual profit. That number is not really a fact about the company; it is a measurement of what the crowd is willing to pay. A high P/E means investors expect those earnings to grow a lot, and are paying today for profits that have not happened yet. A low P/E means they doubt it, or they want a discount for the risk.

Here is what makes today instructive. Nvidia's actual earnings did not get worse this week — they got better. When it reported on November 17 it posted adjusted earnings of $2.68 a share on $115.8 billion of revenue, beating expectations of $2.47 on $109.15 billion, and it guided the current quarter above estimates too. Earnings up, and yet the stock is down roughly 11% today and has shed an enormous amount of value over the past week. If the bottom half of the fraction grew while the whole thing fell, then the only thing that can have changed is what the crowd will pay per dollar of profit. That shrinking is called **multiple compression**, and it is one of the most underappreciated ways to lose money: you can be completely right about a company's profits and still lose, because you were wrong about what others would pay for them.

Think of it as the difference between a player's scoring and his contract. Suppose a guard averages 30 points a game. The team signs him to a max deal because everyone believes he will keep scoring 30 for five more years. Now suppose nothing changes about his scoring — he still puts up 30 — but the league starts wondering whether his knee will hold, or whether the stat-keeper was generous. His points per game are identical. What he is worth per point has fallen. That is multiple compression, and it is why today's news fit so neatly: an SEC inquiry into accounting does not lower the points on the scoreboard, it makes people less sure the scoreboard is accurate. And the Fed's expected rate hike pushes the same direction, because when safe bonds yield nearly 5%, nobody needs to pay as much for a dollar of uncertain future profit. Two forces, both acting on the multiple, neither touching earnings.

The practical habit to build from this: when you see a stock fall hard, ask which half of the fraction moved. Did the business get worse, or did the price people will pay for the same business get smaller? Those are genuinely different situations, and the headlines almost never distinguish between them for you.
