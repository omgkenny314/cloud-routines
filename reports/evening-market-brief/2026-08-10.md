# Market Close Brief — Monday, August 10, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a pause, not a turn. After Friday's record close and the best week since April, the market spent Monday doing very little: the S&P 500 finished a fraction below where it started, the Dow gave back about 61 points, and the Nasdaq drifted down about a third of a percent. Almost nothing happened at the index level, and that itself is the story — traders are waiting. The single biggest storyline was the Strait of Hormuz. Over the weekend Iran said it was close to an agreement with Oman on new shipping lanes through the strait, which sounds like progress, but Tehran added that any deal would not reopen the waterway right away, and it rejected direct talks with the United States for now. President Trump said the conflict could end "pretty soon" while also describing the U.S. as "only semi-negotiating" with Iran. Traders read all of that as slower going than they had hoped, so oil rose for a third straight session, and rising oil is exactly what the stock market does not want right now — because expensive oil feeds inflation, and inflation is what might force the Federal Reserve to raise interest rates. Underneath the flat surface there was one genuinely large single-stock event: Intel announced it would sell $15 billion of new shares, and its stock dropped. And everyone is really waiting for Wednesday, when the July inflation report lands.

## Big movers and why

Intel (ticker INTC), down about 4%, and as low as roughly $96.97 during the session — dropping back below the $100 mark. This was the day's biggest corporate news. Intel announced a proposed $15 billion public offering of common stock, meaning the company is creating and selling brand-new shares to raise cash. Underwriters get a 30-day option to buy up to $2.25 billion more. Intel says the money is for general corporate purposes including capital spending, as it races to build capacity for artificial intelligence demand while protecting its investment-grade credit rating. Reports suggest this may be Intel's first public share sale since it listed in 1971. The stock fell on dilution — more on exactly what that means in today's lesson. ## Headlines that matter

The Strait of Hormuz remains the market's central macro story. Iran said talks with Oman on establishing new shipping lanes are nearing agreement, but warned the arrangement would not mean an immediate reopening of the strait. Iran also declined direct negotiations with the United States for now, citing breaches of the interim peace deal struck in June, and repeated its demands: an end to the U.S. naval blockade, the lifting of sanctions, and compensation for war damages. Why this matters: roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes through that waterway. As long as it stays constricted, oil prices carry a risk premium, that premium flows into gasoline and shipping and manufacturing costs, and that keeps U.S. inflation elevated. The strait is not really a geopolitical story for investors — it is an inflation story wearing a geopolitical costume.

The Federal Reserve picture shifted meaningfully, and it is worth being clear about the direction. In 2026 the Fed is not debating whether to cut rates; it is debating whether to raise them, because inflation has stayed above its 2% target. After Friday's surprisingly weak July jobs report — payrolls contracted by 23,000 — futures markets now price about a 52% chance the Fed raises rates at its September meeting, down from roughly 67% a week earlier. Why this matters: higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive for companies and consumers, and they make safe bonds more attractive relative to stocks. A falling probability of a hike is a tailwind for stock prices, which is a large part of why Friday was so strong and why today held its ground despite the oil move.

Intel's $15 billion equity raise matters beyond Intel. A company of that size choosing to sell stock rather than borrow says something about both the scale of AI-related capital spending and about management's view of its own share price. It is also a live case study in dilution, which is today's lesson.

This week's inflation data is the main event, and today was largely a waiting room for it. The July Consumer Price Index — the government's primary measure of what consumers pay for goods and services — arrives Wednesday, August 12, at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. The June reading showed prices up 3.5% over the prior twelve months, with "core" inflation, which strips out volatile food and energy prices to show the underlying trend, at 2.9%. The Producer Price Index, which measures prices at the wholesale level before they reach consumers, follows Thursday. Retail sales and the University of Michigan consumer sentiment survey come Friday.

## Today's lesson: dilution, and why Intel fell on good news

Here is something that looks backwards until you see the mechanism. Intel announced it was raising $15 billion — a huge pile of fresh cash, to be spent chasing the biggest growth opportunity in the industry. That sounds like good news. The stock fell about 4%. Why?

The answer is dilution. When you own a stock, you do not own "Intel" in some abstract sense — you own a specific fraction of Intel. That fraction is your shares divided by the total number of shares that exist, a figure called shares outstanding. Intel is not borrowing this $15 billion; it is creating brand-new shares and selling them. Reports suggest the sale could increase Intel's share count by around 3%. Every existing share therefore represents a slightly smaller slice of the same company. You did not sell anything, but you own less of it than you did Friday.

This is why so much of company analysis is expressed per share rather than in totals. Earnings per share, the number you hear quoted every earnings season, is simply total profit divided by shares outstanding. A company can grow its total profit and still deliver falling earnings per share if the share count grows faster. That is the trap dilution sets, and it is why experienced investors check the share count in every quarterly report, not just the revenue and profit lines. Share buybacks — where a company purchases its own stock and retires it — run this movie in reverse, shrinking the count so each remaining share represents more.

The basketball version: dilution is the coach adding three players to the rotation. The team may well be better — more depth, more options, a real shot at the title. But if you were averaging 30 minutes a night, you are not averaging 30 anymore. The pie may be growing; your slice of it is smaller. Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on how good the new players are.

So the real question the market was asking today is not "is $15 billion good or bad?" It is: will the returns Intel earns on that $15 billion more than make up for the 3% of the company existing shareholders just gave away? Today's roughly 4% decline is the market's provisional answer — skeptical, but not damning. And notice the useful control in the data: Advanced Micro Devices was down only about 1% and Broadcom was flat. If AI chip demand had suddenly looked worse, they would have fallen too. They did not. This was a story about Intel's capital structure — how a company funds itself — not about its industry. Learning to separate those two is one of the most valuable habits you can build.
