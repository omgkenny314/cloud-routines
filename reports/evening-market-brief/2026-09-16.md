# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, September 16, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates today for the first time in three years, and the stock market spent the last two hours of the session deciding how much it minded. The answer was: a lot, if you own a bank; barely at all, if you own big tech. The Fed lifted its benchmark rate by a quarter of a percentage point to a range of 3.75% to 4%, a unanimous 12-0 vote, and new Chair Kevin Warsh used his press conference to make clear this was not a one-off. All three major indexes had been higher earlier in the day. By the close, the Dow Jones Industrial Average had given up 631 points while the Nasdaq Composite finished essentially unchanged — an unusually wide split between two indexes that normally move together. The single biggest storyline is the rate hike itself and what sits behind it: inflation driven largely by an oil shock, with crude still above $100 a barrel after attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure and continued disruption around the Strait of Hormuz. This is a Fed raising rates into a supply shock rather than into a boom, which is a genuinely uncomfortable place for a central bank to be.

## The numbers

**S&P 500: 7,551.81, down 0.45%.** Gave up an earlier gain after the Fed decision and Warsh's press conference. The damage was concentrated rather than broad — financials did most of the work.

**Dow Jones Industrial Average: 51,461.90, down 631.21 points, or 1.21%.** The worst of the three. The Dow holds only 30 companies and several of them are large banks, so a bad day for banks is a bad day for the Dow.

**Nasdaq Composite: 25,978.42, down 0.01%.** Effectively flat — a rounding error. Technology and the memory-chip story (see below) offset the rate-driven selling almost exactly.

**10-year Treasury yield: about 5.008%, roughly unchanged on the day.** It sat right at the 5% line after the decision. Worth noting: the yield did *not* jump on a rate hike, because the hike was already expected. Markets price in what they see coming.

**Crude oil: WTI in the $103–$105 range, lower on the day.** Sources I checked put it between "below $103" and "near $105" at different points in the session, and I could not pin down an exact settlement price against two sources, so treat the level as approximate. Brent was around $108. Oil fell back because U.S. government inventory data showed a crude drawdown of only 640,000 barrels against expectations for 1.4 million, with gasoline and distillate stockpiles actually building.

**Bitcoin: around $75,900, down roughly 1.4% over 24 hours.** I could only verify prices from before and around the Fed announcement, not a post-close print, so treat this as a daytime level rather than a closing one. The pressure came from Washington, not the Fed: the Senate failed to advance the CLARITY Act, the crypto regulatory bill the industry had been counting on.

## Big movers and why

**Goldman Sachs (GS)** was the single biggest drag on the Dow, falling about 4%. Big banks overall had their worst day since February. Bank of America (BAC), Wells Fargo (WFC) and Citigroup (C) each fell more than 3%; JPMorgan Chase (JPM) fell about 1.5%. The SPDR S&P Bank ETF (KBE), a basket that tracks bank stocks, dropped 2.6% — its worst session since February 27. The fear is not this hike; it is the next one. Banks borrow short-term and lend long-term, and a Fed that keeps tightening squeezes that spread while also raising the odds that borrowers stop paying.

**Intel (INTC)** rose 4.34%, one of the day's clearest winners, on a Reuters report that South Korea's **SK Hynix** is in talks to manufacture memory chips on U.S. soil for the first time — possibly by taking over part of Intel's long-delayed Ohio facility, or through a joint venture with large cloud companies hunting for guaranteed memory supply. SK Hynix shares finished 4.1% higher in Seoul; its U.S.-listed shares rose 1.31%. Both sides describe the talks as exploratory with no decisions made, so this is a story about possibility, not a signed deal.

**Homebuilders** lagged the market. The 30-year mortgage rate has climbed to 7%, a fresh one-year high, which makes the incentives builders use to close sales more expensive to fund. Several Wall Street firms have been trimming price targets on the group.

## Headlines that matter

**The Fed raised rates 25 basis points to 3.75%–4%.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 25 of them is a quarter point. This is the first increase since July 2023 and it reverses the direction the Fed has been moving for two years. Why it matters: the entire market has spent that period assuming rates only go down from here. Today that assumption broke. Every valuation model, every mortgage, every corporate borrowing plan built on "rates are falling" now has to be redone.

**The vote was unanimous, 12-0, and the projections point to more.** Of the 18 officials who submit forecasts, 16 expect at least one more hike this year. Warsh has chosen not to submit a projection of his own since taking the job. Why it matters: unanimity removes the hope that a divided committee might be talked out of the next move. The dissent that would signal "this is nearly over" simply isn't there.

**Warsh said the quiet part directly: "This summer's inflation readings do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved."** Why it matters: markets were positioned for a "hawkish hike" — raise once, then signal patience. He declined to give them the second half. Stocks were higher before he spoke and lower after. That sentence is most of the day's decline.

**August retail sales rose 1.2%, well above the 0.7% economists expected**, after a revised 0.5% decline in July. Total retail and food-services sales hit $773.9 billion, up 6.0% from a year ago. Online retailers gained 2.6%, furniture 0.9%, clothing 0.7%, autos 0.6%. Excluding gas stations, sales still rose 1.1%. Why it matters, and why it cuts against you: strong consumer spending is good news for company earnings, but it also tells the Fed the economy can absorb higher rates. A weak number would have given the Fed a reason to pause. This was not that number.

**The oil shock is the root cause of all of it.** Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline remains offline after attacks on energy infrastructure, the Strait of Hormuz is still disrupted, and Houthi forces are advancing toward the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Two of the world's most important oil chokepoints are compromised at once. Why it matters: this is inflation the Fed cannot fix. Raising rates does not produce more crude. It can only cool demand hard enough to offset a supply problem — which is why hikes driven by supply shocks tend to be painful.

## Today's lesson: the discount rate, or why a number in the bond market changes what your stock is worth

Recent lessons have covered dilution, the four prices of a trading day, backlog, and support and resistance. Today's is a fundamental one — about what a business is actually worth — and it explains the single strangest thing that happened today.

Start with the core idea. A share of stock is a claim on money the company will earn in the future. To decide what that claim is worth *today*, you have to answer one question: how much is a dollar arriving in 2033 worth to me right now? Less than a dollar, obviously — because if you had the dollar today you could put it somewhere safe and earn interest on it for seven years. The rate you use to shrink those future dollars back to present value is called the **discount rate**, and the starting point for it is the yield on U.S. Treasury bonds — the risk-free return you could get without owning any stock at all.

Here is why that matters right now. The 10-year Treasury yield is sitting at 5%. Not long ago it was well under 4%. When the risk-free alternative gets more attractive, every future dollar a company promises you becomes worth less today, because you're now comparing it against a better safe option. The company hasn't changed. Its factories, its customers, its products are all exactly the same. But the arithmetic used to value it has changed, and so the price changes. That's the mechanism behind a huge amount of what looks like random market movement.

The basketball version: imagine two players, one 24 years old and one 34. Most of the 24-year-old's value is a decade away; most of the 34-year-old's is right now. Raise the cost of waiting — a shorter contract window, a win-now owner, a shrinking title window — and the young player's trade value falls much harder than the veteran's, even though neither has changed as a player. Cash flows work the same way. Money far in the future is the young player.

Now the wrinkle, which is where today gets interesting and where I'd rather be honest with you than tidy. If long-dated cash flows suffer most from higher discount rates, technology stocks — the classic "most of the payoff is years away" group — should have been today's biggest losers. Instead the Nasdaq finished flat and the banks got destroyed. That tells you the discount rate is real but it is never the only thing operating. Banks fell on a *business* problem: more hikes squeeze their lending spread and raise the risk their borrowers default. Tech held up on a *business* story: the Intel–SK Hynix memory news gave the sector something concrete to buy. So the lesson has two halves, and the second half matters as much as the first. Learn the discount rate, because it's the invisible gravity underneath every valuation. Then remember that on any given day, the specific news about a specific business can easily outweigh it.
