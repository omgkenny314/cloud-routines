# Market Close Brief — Thursday, November 5, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Yesterday's post-election celebration was cancelled, and then some. Wednesday the Dow Jones Industrial Average had its best day of the year on the midterm results; today the market gave the whole thing back and kept going. The S&P 500 fell about 4.2%, the Dow about 3.4% — roughly 1,800 points — and the technology-heavy Nasdaq Composite about 5.3%, its worst single day in a year and a half. The Nasdaq also closed in what is called a correction, meaning more than 10% below its record high. Something like $2 trillion in stock market value disappeared in one session. The single biggest storyline is that the artificial-intelligence trade, which has driven this market for two years, came apart in a day. The companies that sell the equipment for AI data centers were hit hardest: chipmakers fell 9% to 15%, and the smaller companies that build and rent out AI computing capacity fell further. Three things hit at once. First, traders decided a Democratic Congress plus a Democratic governor-elect in Texas means real political friction for data-center construction. Second, Qualcomm, a major chipmaker, forecast weaker profits than Wall Street expected. Third, and most concretely, Bloomberg reported that Microsoft is tightening oversight of outside data-center partners and walking away from some projects — which is the AI industry's biggest customer being publicly more careful about spending. Here is the detail worth holding onto: there was no bad news about AI demand today. What changed was the price people were willing to pay for it.

## Big movers and why

On top of that it forecast fourth-quarter revenue falling in the low teens percentage-wise from the prior quarter. ## Headlines that matter

- **The AI trade broke, and the trigger was political rather than economic.** Traders concluded that a Democratic Congress, plus a Democratic governor-elect in Texas — a major hub for planned data centers — means tighter AI regulation and more friction for data-center construction. **Why it matters:** data centers need permits, enormous amounts of electricity, and local political cooperation. None of that is federal policy exactly, but all of it is political. Notably, several strategists called today an overreaction. Truist's Keith Lerner described it as a knee-jerk reaction rather than a real signal about technology under a Democratic Congress. Keep both ideas in mind at once: the move was real, and the people who follow this closely are not unanimous that the reasoning was sound.

- **Bloomberg reported that Microsoft (ticker MSFT) is tightening oversight of outside data-center partners — and this is the most concrete news of the day.** Microsoft is scrutinizing projects run by Nebius, IREN and Nscale, and weighing whether to sign new deals of this kind at all. It declined an option to more than double its computing capacity with Nscale at a Texas site, and walked away from a roughly $3 billion UK data-center project that had been delayed by difficulty securing grid power. Cabling problems at IREN's Texas campus disrupted construction timelines. Microsoft's response was that adjusting plans as circumstances change is normal for large infrastructure projects. **Why it matters:** this is the difference between a fear and a fact. Every other worry today was about what politicians might do later. This was the AI industry's biggest spender being publicly more selective right now. One third-party tally of those three companies' SEC filings put their combined disclosed Microsoft contract value at $70.9 billion — that is a calculation by an outside analyst, not a Microsoft figure, so weight it accordingly. Note also the detail about grid power: the binding constraint on AI is starting to look like electricity and construction, not chips.

- **Weekly jobless claims came in at 189,000 for the week ended October 31, below expectations near 196,000 and down from an upwardly revised 195,000.** The four-week average slipped below 195,000. **Why it matters:** jobless claims are the fastest read available on whether people are losing jobs, and this one says the labor market is holding up well. In a normal year that is good news. Right now it cuts the other way for stocks, because a strong job market gives the Federal Reserve room to raise interest rates.

- **The Federal Reserve is expected to raise rates in December, not cut them — and this is the piece most likely to surprise you if you have not been following it closely.** The Fed's target range is currently 3.75% to 4.00%, held steady in October after its first increase in three years. Market-implied odds of a quarter-point hike at the December 8-9 meeting have been running roughly 70% to 80%. The driver is inflation that has not cooperated: headline PCE inflation, the Fed's preferred measure, ran 3.6% over the year to September with the core measure at 3.1%, and the Cleveland Fed's tracking model points higher still for October and November. Energy costs are the main culprit. **Why it matters:** higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive, and the AI build-out is heavily financed with borrowed money. That is the quiet link between the Fed and today's selloff: several reports noted AI-related stocks have been swinging for months on worries that data-center construction may slow because borrowing has become costly. The political story grabbed the headlines, but the financing story has been there the whole time.

- **Oil rose about 2.3% as hopes for a quick end to the Iran war faded.** Vice President JD Vance reportedly reached out to Iran's Rezaei about ceasefire terms, but no meeting has been scheduled. The consultancy Eurasia Group trimmed its estimated probability of renewed U.S. strikes on Iran to 60% from 65%, while also cutting the odds of a negotiated agreement to 10% from 20% — in other words, less chance of escalation and less chance of resolution. **Why it matters:** oil is the main channel through which that war reaches your grocery bill and the Fed's decision. Higher energy prices feed inflation, and inflation is exactly what is keeping a December rate hike on the table.

- **A technical marker worth noting: Nvidia, Micron and eight other AI-related stocks fell below their 200-day moving averages.** The 200-day moving average is simply the average closing price over the last 200 trading days, and many investors treat it as a rough dividing line between a healthy long-term uptrend and a broken one. **Why it matters:** it does not predict anything. But a lot of money is managed by rules that reference that line, so when a whole group of stocks crosses below it at once, it can bring in more selling that has nothing to do with the companies themselves.

## Today's lesson: multiple compression, or how a stock can get much cheaper without the business changing at all

Here is the puzzle from today. Nvidia fell somewhere between 6% and 8%. It released no news. No customer cancelled an order. Nothing about the business changed between Wednesday's close and Thursday's close. Yet roughly $1.4 trillion of market value has now vanished from the stock since its record high. If the business did not change, what did?

To answer that, you need one piece of arithmetic: the **P/E ratio**, or price-to-earnings ratio. You take a company's share price and divide it by its earnings per share — its annual profit divided across all its shares. If a company earns $10 per share and trades at $150, its P/E is 15. The plain-language meaning is "the market is paying $15 today for every $1 of annual profit." It is the market's price tag on a dollar of this company's earnings. When people say a stock is expensive or cheap, this ratio is usually what they mean, and a **forward** P/E simply uses next year's expected earnings instead of last year's actual ones.

Now watch what that arithmetic does. A share price is, roughly, earnings multiplied by the P/E. So the price can fall for two completely different reasons: because expected earnings went down, or because the multiple people will pay went down. Today was entirely the second kind. Nvidia's expected earnings did not drop — the market simply decided it would pay less for each dollar of them. That is called **multiple compression**, and it is one of the most useful ideas in fundamental analysis, because it separates "this business got worse" from "this business got less popular."

The numbers make the point vividly. Depending on whose data you use, Nvidia now trades at roughly 13 to 15 times expected earnings. The S&P 500 as a whole trades around 18 to 19 times. So the company at the absolute center of the AI boom is now priced more cheaply than the average American company — and one analysis pointed out it is cheaper on this measure than Kimberly-Clark, which makes toilet paper and tissues, and cheaper than most consumer staples companies. I will flag honestly that these figures vary by provider, from about 12.9 to 15.2, because different data shops calculate expected earnings differently; one outlier said 24.9. The exact number is debatable, the direction is not.

A basketball way to see it. Think of earnings as a player's actual production — points, rebounds, assists — and the multiple as what a team will pay for that production. A player can put up the same numbers in back-to-back seasons and have his contract value collapse, because the league now doubts his knees will hold up for five more years. His production did not change; the market's confidence in its durability did. That is precisely what happened to the AI chipmakers today. Nobody argued Nvidia will sell fewer chips next quarter. People started doubting how many years this spending boom runs — and a multiple is essentially a bet on how long the good times last.

Here is the part that keeps this honest, and I am not telling you what to do with it. A low multiple is not automatically a bargain. It can mean the market is wrong and you are being handed a discount, or it can mean the market is right and those expected earnings are about to be revised down, which would make today's P/E a mirage built on estimates that no longer hold. Both stories look identical on the day. The way you tell them apart is by watching whether the earnings estimates themselves start falling in the weeks ahead — and that is exactly why November 17, when Nvidia reports, is the date that matters.
