# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, September 9, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was the third down day in a row, and it was the same two villains as the last two sessions: oil and interest rates. Brent crude, the international oil benchmark, pushed back above $100 a barrel for the first time since July after U.S. Central Command said American forces destroyed five Iranian oil tankers, and the 10-year Treasury yield climbed to about 4.86%, its highest level since November 2023. Both of those are inflation signals arriving one week before the Federal Reserve decides whether to raise interest rates, and traders currently think that decision is close to a coin flip. The damage was mild in percentage terms — the S&P 500 lost less than half a percent — but it was broad, and it was worst in the parts of the market most sensitive to a slowing consumer: small companies, retailers, industrials, and consumer staples. Underneath the index numbers, though, this was a genuinely eventful day. Apple held its big iPhone launch event. Meta Platforms had one of its best days of the year on an artificial-intelligence product announcement. And three companies that reported earnings got violently repriced, in both directions. If you want the single storyline: the macro picture is getting tighter, and the market is now paying very close attention to which companies can still deliver.

## Big movers and why

**Meta Platforms (ticker META) was the day's big winner, closing at $650.69, up 6.07%.** It traded as high as roughly $653.61, up about 6.6%, and touched a gain of 7.2% at one point in the morning. The cause was Muse, the personal artificial-intelligence agent Meta unveiled Tuesday. Muse is not a chatbot that answers questions — it is an agent that does things on your behalf: writes and sends email, books travel, fills out forms, and completes purchases once you give it permission. It runs on a dedicated virtual machine in Meta's cloud with a browser you can watch, and a separate system called Sentinel has to approve anything Muse sends out to the internet. There is a free tier plus subscriptions at $20 and $100 a month. Mizuho Securities and KeyBanc Capital Markets both wrote favorably about it. *Why the market cared so much:* Meta has been spending staggering sums on artificial intelligence, and investors have spent two years asking a simple question — where is the revenue? A paid consumer subscription is a direct, legible answer. Worth noting the caution some analysts raised: this is sentiment moving ahead of evidence. The subscription has not yet proven it can cover a capital budget of that size.

**Alphabet (ticker GOOGL), the parent of Google, fell about 2%.** The two moves are related. Muse plugs into Google Workspace, Spotify, OpenTable and Apple Health at launch, and the market read a Meta agent that books, buys and organizes on your behalf as a direct competitive threat to Google's place as the thing you open when you want to get something done.

**Apple (ticker AAPL) fell about 1.1%** on the day it held its biggest event of the year. Apple announced the iPhone 18 Pro at $1,199, the iPhone 18 Pro Max at $1,299 — each $100 above last year's equivalent — and a foldable iPhone starting near $1,999, the most expensive iPhone ever made. All three run Apple's new A20 Pro chip, built on a 2-nanometer manufacturing process. This was the first launch event under new chief executive John Ternus. *Why the stock fell on good news:* this is such a well-worn pattern it has a name, "sell the news." Everything announced today was extensively leaked and reported for weeks, so it was already reflected in the price before the event started. Historically Apple has drifted lower right after launch events and recovered over the following one to two months, though that is a tendency and not a rule.

**Signet Jewelers (ticker SIG) jumped about 19%,** the day's standout gainer. Signet owns Kay Jewelers, Zales and Jared. It earned an adjusted $2.19 per share in its second quarter against the roughly $1.74 analysts expected, and — the part that mattered more — raised its full-year earnings guidance.

**ServiceTitan (ticker TTAN) collapsed more than 30%.** ServiceTitan makes software for home-services contractors like plumbers and electricians. It reported last night, and I flagged in yesterday's brief that I could not yet verify the numbers. Here is what happened: the quarter itself was not the problem. The company's revenue *guidance* for the coming quarter came in below what analysts expected, and the stock lost nearly a third of its value in a single session.

**Casey's General Stores (ticker CASY) fell more than 15%,** also reporting last night. Casey's runs convenience stores across the middle of the country and is one of the largest pizza chains in the United States. Results were mixed: fuel sales fell 0.3% from a year earlier, and sales of prepared food and dispensed beverages — the high-margin part of a convenience store — grew slightly less than expected.

**Chewy (ticker CHWY) fell roughly 10%.** Chewy sells pet food and supplies online. Its quarter was fine: adjusted earnings of 36 cents per share matched expectations exactly, revenue of $3.33 billion came in at the high end of guidance and slightly above the $3.32 billion consensus, active customers grew 3.8% to 21.7 million, and the company *raised* both its full-year revenue and profit-margin outlook. The stock fell anyway, on doubts about whether the margin improvement is durable. **The 10.16% figure and the $20.91 price I found were quoted at 12:22 p.m. Eastern, not at the close, so treat the exact number as intraday rather than final.**

**Tesla (ticker TSLA) rose somewhere between 4% and 5%,** the clear upside outlier among the mega-cap names. **Sources gave me 3.98% and "about 5%, to roughly $372," so I cannot give you one exact closing figure.** The reasons were Slovenia becoming the sixth European country to approve Full Self-Driving Supervised — Tesla's driver-assistance system, which steers, accelerates and brakes but still requires the driver to pay attention — plus a bounce back from last Friday's sharp decline.

**Elsewhere in the large caps:** Nvidia (ticker NVDA) fell about 2.0%, Microsoft (ticker MSFT) about 1.2%. On the Dow, International Business Machines (ticker IBM) gained about 2.8% and Chevron (ticker CVX) about 2.0% on the oil move, while Nike (ticker NKE) fell about 2.2%, Amazon (ticker AMZN) about 1.9% and Procter & Gamble (ticker PG) about 1.9%. **These individual percentages come from single sources and some may be intraday snapshots rather than closing prints.**

**By sector, energy was the only reliable place to hide,** with utilities also holding up, while industrials, consumer discretionary and consumer staples were the worst. **The precise sector percentages I found were from early in the session, so I am describing the ranking rather than quoting numbers.**

## Headlines that matter

**The Treasury announced a bigger bond buyback, and yields went up anyway.** The Treasury Department said it would buy back up to $6 billion of longer-dated government bonds in a September 10 operation — triple the size of its standard $2 billion operation. The 10-year yield promptly rose to about 4.857%, the highest since November 2023. *Why this matters, and why it is confusing:* a buyback means the government is purchasing its own outstanding debt, taking bonds off the market. Fewer bonds available should mean higher bond prices and therefore lower yields, since bond prices and yields always move in opposite directions. That is not what happened. The market read it the other way: if the Treasury has to triple the size of an operation whose stated purpose is supporting liquidity in the long end of the bond market, that is evidence the bond market needs support — that buyers for long-dated government debt are getting scarcer. The size of the rescue told investors something about the size of the problem. This is worth filing away as a general pattern: an intervention can be read as bad news about the thing it is intervening in.

**Oil went back above $100 as the U.S.-Iran conflict escalated.** U.S. Central Command said American forces destroyed five Iranian oil tankers. The conflict has been constricting the flow of oil through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne crude passes. *Why it matters:* $100 is more than a big round number here — oil is an input to nearly every price in the economy, from gasoline to diesel to freight to airfares, and those flow into the Consumer Price Index. So a tanker strike in the Persian Gulf becomes, three steps later, an argument for the Federal Reserve to raise interest rates, which is an argument for lower stock prices. That chain is why oil headlines have been driving stock prices for two weeks straight.

**The Federal Reserve meets September 15 and 16, and it is genuinely close to a coin flip.** Fed officials are in their communications blackout and cannot speak publicly until the decision. As of today, the CME FedWatch tool, which reads probabilities out of interest-rate futures prices, put the odds of a quarter-point rate *hike* at roughly 56% to 57.5%. Prediction markets were slightly lower: Kalshi around 48%, Polymarket around 49% to 52%. *Why it matters:* a few weeks ago the market was near 70% confident the Fed would do nothing. That flipped after Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech and last week's unexpectedly strong jobs report. A market that is split roughly 50-50 on a major decision is a market that is going to move sharply when the answer arrives, in whichever direction it lands.

**Apple launched the iPhone 18 line and its first foldable.** Beyond the stock reaction, the pricing is the economically interesting part: $100 increases on both Pro models and a foldable near $1,999. *Why it matters:* Apple is one of the largest companies in the world and a major weight in all three indexes, but it is also a live experiment in consumer pricing power in an inflationary, tariff-affected year. Whether people pay $100 more is a data point about the American consumer, not just about Apple.

**Meta's Muse launch reframed the artificial-intelligence trade.** *Why it matters:* for two years the AI story in the stock market has mostly been about companies that sell the tools — chips, servers, cloud capacity. Muse is a large company trying to charge ordinary consumers a monthly fee for AI directly. If that works, it changes which companies are considered AI winners. If it does not, today's 6% gain was borrowed from tomorrow.

## Today's lesson: what makes a trend, and how it differs from a bad day

Every source covering today's market used the same phrase: "the third straight day of declines." That framing is doing real analytical work, and it is worth understanding why professionals reach for it.

A single down day tells you almost nothing. Markets fall on roughly 45% of all days. That is closer to a coin flip than to a signal. But a *sequence* of down days is a different kind of information, and the technical term for what analysts are looking for is a **trend** — a persistent direction that holds across multiple sessions, rather than a one-day wobble.

The classic definition of a downtrend is **lower highs and lower lows**: each peak is below the last peak, and each trough is below the last trough. Let me show you this with the actual S&P 500 closing levels from the last seven trading days:

- September 1: 7,631.47
- September 2: 7,666.60
- September 3: 7,747.71
- September 4: 7,718.60
- September 8: 7,673.52
- September 9: 7,636.36

Read that from the top down. The index climbed for three days, peaked at 7,747.71 on September 3, and has gone down every session since. Three consecutive lower closes, totaling a decline of 111.35 points, or 1.44%, from that peak. Each close is lower than the one before it. That is the textbook shape.

Here is the part that requires judgment rather than arithmetic, and where beginners often go wrong. **A three-day decline of 1.44% is not, by itself, a broken market.** Look at where today's close landed: 7,636.36. Now look at September 1: 7,631.47. After three straight down days, the S&P 500 is sitting almost exactly where it was six trading sessions ago. All the market has really done is give back a three-day rally. That is normal breathing, not a collapse.

The basketball version: a player missing three shots in a row is not in a shooting slump. Three misses happen to good shooters constantly — it is inside the range of normal variation. What would tell you something is actually wrong is if the misses kept coming *and* the shots themselves started changing: rushed, off-balance, from spots he does not usually shoot from. In market terms, the equivalent of "the shots are changing" is what would happen if the S&P 500 broke decisively below that September 1 level of 7,631 on heavy volume, or if the declines got steeper rather than shallower. Notice that today's loss, 0.48%, was smaller than yesterday's 0.58%, which was smaller than Friday's context of a broader slide. Selling that gets less intense as it continues is a different animal from selling that accelerates.

So the honest read on today: yes, this is a three-day downtrend, and the direction is real and it has an identifiable cause — oil and yields, both pointing at inflation, one week before a Fed meeting that is a coin flip. But the magnitude is small and the market has not yet given up ground it held a week ago. A trend tells you the direction. It does not tell you the destination, and it says nothing at all about how long it will last. The value of naming it is that it tells you what to watch next, which is exactly why tomorrow's PPI number matters so much: it is the kind of event that either extends the trend or ends it.
