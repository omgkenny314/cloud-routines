# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, November 11, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a rebound, and a narrow one. One company's earnings report — released last night, after yesterday's close — reversed the mood of the entire technology sector. CoreWeave, which rents out artificial-intelligence computing power, told investors it expects to turn profitable in the first half of 2028, raised its revenue outlook, and reported a backlog of future contracted business worth $132.5 billion. The market had spent the previous week terrified that AI spending was about to slow down. This was evidence it is not. CoreWeave's own stock rose something close to 20%, chipmakers and memory makers rose with it, Nvidia bounced about 4% after two ugly days, and the Nasdaq Composite finished up 1.65% — enough to erase all of its losses since the midterm elections. The S&P 500 rose 1.36%. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose just 0.52%, because it holds far less of what was rallying.

The single biggest storyline, though, is a split screen. Stocks spent the day celebrating, while the market for interest rates spent the day doing the opposite. Yesterday's hot inflation report has not gone away, and today a parade of Federal Reserve officials made the December rate increase sound close to certain — one of them called the inflation data "borderline disturbing." Here is the odd part: **the U.S. bond market was closed today for Veterans Day.** Stocks traded normal hours; Treasury bonds did not trade at all. So the repricing of Fed expectations happened in futures markets while the cash bond market took the day off. That is worth knowing because it means one of the six numbers in tonight's brief simply does not exist for today.

And it sets up tonight's lesson perfectly. CoreWeave rallied on growth. But buried in the same report is an interest bill of $846 million for one quarter against operating profit of $207 million — a company paying four times more to its lenders than its operations earned. Tonight I want to teach you the one ratio that measures exactly that, because it is the bridge between the Fed story and the AI story, and almost nobody explains how they connect.

## Headlines that matter

**The bond market was closed, and that is itself the headline.** Stocks traded 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern as normal; U.S. Treasury trading was shut for Veterans Day and reopens tomorrow. *Why it matters:* bonds are where the market expresses its view on the Fed, so on a day when Fed expectations moved, the usual signal was unavailable. Thin, holiday-affected conditions also tend to exaggerate moves in whatever is still trading — something to keep in mind before concluding that today's 1.65% Nasdaq jump reflects the considered view of the entire investing world. Tomorrow the bond market reopens and gets to vote on two days of news at once.

**Fed officials spent the day making a December rate increase sound settled.** Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee called yesterday's inflation report "borderline disturbing," though he would not say how he will vote. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said she supports **two more** quarter-point increases and that the Fed could "move more rapidly this winter" than it has so far this fall. Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson, who has a vote, backed "further gradual tightening." And Governor Christopher Waller had already said a **half-point** increase should be considered. *Why it matters:* the Fed's benchmark rate sits at 3.75%–4.00% today. Market-implied odds of an increase at the December 8–9 meeting ran between about 89% and effectively 100% depending on the source and the hour, with roughly 15% odds on a double-sized half-point move. A Reuters poll found **all 101 economists surveyed** expect a quarter-point increase. When the entire profession agrees, the risk shifts: the damage comes from the Fed doing something *other* than what everyone expects.

**October inflation, released yesterday, is the thing none of this gets away from.** Consumer prices rose 0.4% on the month against 0.3% expected; core prices, which strip out food and energy, rose 0.3% against 0.2% expected. *Why it matters:* this is why officials are talking about raising rates rather than cutting them, and it is a genuinely unusual situation — inflation is accelerating even though oil has collapsed from about $117 in October to the mid-$80s. One wrinkle: the report carried **no annual inflation figures**, because last year's government shutdown left holes in the underlying data. Everyone is navigating on monthly changes alone, which are noisier.

**The AI-spending scare that defined last week has been at least temporarily answered.** The Nasdaq fell more than 5.5% on November 5, and a basket of chip stocks has shed trillions in value since early October, on fear that the enormous capital spending on AI data centers was about to be cut. CoreWeave's report was the first hard evidence against that fear — raised guidance, a backlog that grew by $28.5 billion in three months, and prices that rose 25% in July and another 10% later in the quarter. *Why it matters:* this correction has been a referendum on one question — is AI demand real and durable? Today the market got one data point saying yes. Nvidia's report on November 17 is the much bigger one.

**Iran, the Strait of Hormuz, and oil.** Prices initially fell more than $3 a barrel when U.S. officials played down the need for further strikes on Iran, then reversed higher when an Iranian security official said no talks were underway. Attacks on shipping continue — roughly ten so far in November, 37 vessels in October, about 130 since the conflict began — including a tanker struck near Oman on Sunday. *Why it matters:* roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes through that strait. Cheaper oil has been the one thing holding inflation down; if the Hormuz premium comes back, the Fed's problem gets harder, not easier.

## Today's lesson: interest coverage, or the head start you give away before tipoff

Tonight's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning we study the business itself, what it sells and owns and owes, rather than the behavior of its stock chart. The concept is **interest coverage**, and CoreWeave handed us the most extreme example I have seen all year.

Start with two numbers from a company's income statement. **Operating income** is what the business earns from actually doing its business — revenue minus the costs of running it — before paying lenders or the government. **Interest expense** is what the company owes its lenders for the money it borrowed. The interest coverage ratio is simply the first divided by the second: *how many times over could operating profit pay the interest bill?* A healthy, ordinary company runs at 3x or better. Below about 1.5x, lenders start getting nervous. Below 1x means the business is not earning enough to cover its interest, and the gap has to be filled from somewhere else — cash on hand, new borrowing, or selling something.

Now CoreWeave's quarter. Operating income: **$207 million**. Interest expense: **$846 million**, up from $310 million a year ago. That is a coverage ratio of about **0.24x** — the business earned roughly 24 cents of operating profit for every dollar of interest it owed. Stretch it across the first nine months of the year and it gets stranger: about $2.02 billion of net interest against $14 million of operating income. The company also guided next quarter's interest bill *higher*, to $1.05–$1.15 billion. And the net loss for the quarter was $709 million, which is mostly that interest bill showing up where you would expect it.

Here is the basketball version. Imagine your team is genuinely excellent — great offense, improving every month, up 168% in scoring year over year. Now imagine that before the tip-off of every game, the other team is handed a fixed head start, and that head start gets a little bigger every season. Interest expense is that head start. It does not care how well you play. You can be the better team and still lose on the scoreboard, and the coverage ratio is just the head start measured against how fast you score.

So why did the stock go *up* about 20% today instead of down? Because interest coverage tells you how much pressure a company is under, not whether it will survive. CoreWeave's answer to the pressure is that $132.5 billion backlog — contracted future revenue, much of it from very large customers — plus about $9 billion of expected customer prepayments in the next three months, plus the fact that its borrowing is largely secured against the graphics processors it owns, which lenders can take if things go wrong. Investors decided today that the growth arrives in time. The company's own target is adjusted profitability in the first half of 2028, and one word there deserves your attention: **adjusted**. That is a company-defined measure that excludes certain costs. Its *adjusted* operating income this quarter was $379 million, against $207 million on the standard accounting rules. Neither number is a lie, but only one of them follows a rulebook, and when a company tells you when it will be profitable, always ask which number it means.

The last piece is why this connects to the Fed, and it is the reason I chose this lesson tonight. CoreWeave said it **will not issue unsecured debt at current yields** — meaning it finds today's borrowing costs too expensive. Those costs are high because the 10-year Treasury yield is at 5%, and that yield is at 5% because inflation came in hot and the Fed is expected to raise rates in December. So the same decision made in a conference room in Washington next month lands directly in the interest-expense line of a company in New Jersey. When you hear that rising rates hurt growth companies most, this ratio is the mechanism. It is not a mood. It is a bill.
