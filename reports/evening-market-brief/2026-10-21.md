# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, October 21, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today technology took the wheel back. All three major indexes rose, but the technology-heavy Nasdaq Composite gained about twice as much as the Dow Jones Industrial Average, which is close to a mirror image of yesterday, when industrial and defense companies did all the lifting and the Nasdaq finished flat. The S&P 500 notched a third straight gain. The single biggest storyline is SpaceX (ticker SPCX), which reported last night and turned in the first profit of its life as a publicly traded company, with revenue up 161% from a year ago — and the market treated that not just as good news for SpaceX but as evidence that the enormous spending on artificial intelligence is actually turning into revenue for somebody. That one report pulled up a whole chain of companies that sell the physical guts of AI: Super Micro Computer, Dell, and others. Underneath the rally, two things stayed stubbornly unhelpful: the 10-year Treasury yield did not fall, and oil barely budged despite more attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. And the day's real event may not have happened yet — Tesla, Meta Platforms and IBM all report after the bell, and two of those three are already out.

## Big movers and why

- **SpaceX (ticker SPCX), the rocket and satellite company, rose somewhere between 8% and 11% — the day's headline move.** It reported last night and delivered the first quarterly profit in its history as a public company. Revenue jumped 161% from a year ago to $14.58 billion, against roughly $12.7 billion expected, and earnings were 35 cents per share against 12 cents expected. The part that moved the whole market was the artificial-intelligence segment, where revenue rose roughly ninefold to $7.56 billion from $813 million a year earlier. On the exact move I have to be straight with you: Yahoo Finance's headline said "nearly 8%," CNBC's midday report said 10%, and another said 10.7% in early trading. The stock went into the day at $147.79, so the close landed somewhere around $160 to $163, but I could not verify the precise closing print.

- **Super Micro Computer (ticker SMCI), which builds the servers that AI chips get installed into, closed up 9.18% at $35.45 — the single biggest gainer in the S&P 500.** The cause was a guidance raise announced after yesterday's close: it lifted its revenue outlook for the period to a range of $18.5 billion to $19.5 billion, up from a previous range of $14.5 billion to $15.5 billion. That is an increase of about $4 billion at the midpoint, which is an unusually large revision. Some sources reported the gain as high as 12% in early trading before it settled back. Today's lesson is built on this stock, so hold onto it.

- **Dell Technologies (ticker DELL), the computer and server maker, rose about 6% in early trading.** No company-specific news — it moved because it sells into the same AI data-center build-out that SpaceX's and Super Micro's numbers just validated. This is what people mean by a read-across: one company's results get applied to its neighbors.

- **Roivant Sciences (ticker ROIV), a drug developer, rose more than 4%.** Its immune-modifying pill Lisraya met its primary effectiveness goals in two late-stage trials for non-infectious uveitis, an inflammatory eye condition. - **Netflix (ticker NFLX), the streaming company, fell somewhere between 2.5% and 5%.** This is the regular-session reaction to the results it reported last night, which I covered in yesterday's brief: profit beat expectations at 87 cents per share, but fourth-quarter revenue guidance came in light and the company cut its 2026 advertising revenue expectation to about $2.7 billion from roughly $3 billion. ## Headlines that matter

**The Federal Reserve meets one week from today, and the market has almost stopped worrying about it.** The Fed's target interest rate sits at 3.75% to 4.00% after a quarter-point *increase* in September — its first hike since 2023. Prediction markets and futures now put the odds of a hold on October 28 at roughly 84% to 86%, with a quarter-point hike at only about 13% to 15%. Hike odds have been falling, down about 7 percentage points over the past week. Bank of America is the notable holdout, still formally calling for an October increase on the grounds that underlying inflation has not cooled enough.

Why this matters: a week ago the possibility of another hike was a live weight on stock prices, and the minutes released on October 7 showed most officials still expecting one more increase by year end. The market has since decided those officials will wait. That is a large part of why stocks have strung together three up days. It also means the market is now exposed — if the Fed surprises anyone next Wednesday, the reaction will be bigger precisely because so few people are positioned for it. The Fed is in its pre-meeting blackout period, so no official will say anything until the decision.

**Mortgage demand fell again, and housing remains the clearest casualty of high rates.** The Mortgage Bankers Association reported this morning that mortgage applications dropped 5.3% in the week ending October 16. The average 30-year fixed rate it tracks eased to 7.41% from 7.50%, and Freddie Mac's separate survey held at 7.40%.

Why this matters: rates came *down* slightly and applications still fell 5.3%. That is the uncomfortable part. It suggests the problem is no longer just the weekly rate wiggle but the accumulated level — at roughly 7.4%, enough buyers are simply priced out that small improvements do not bring them back. This lines up with the housing starts and builder-confidence data from the past two days, all pointing the same direction. Housing is the most interest-rate-sensitive part of the economy, so it is the first place Fed policy shows up in real life.

**U.S. crude inventories fell much more than expected, and oil shrugged.** The weekly government petroleum report showed U.S. crude stockpiles down 3.06 million barrels, against an expected draw of about 820,000 barrels. A bigger-than-expected drop in stored oil normally pushes prices up — more was consumed or less was produced than the market thought.

Why this matters: prices went nowhere anyway, which tells you the oil market is currently being driven almost entirely by Middle East headlines rather than by supply-and-demand data. When a bullish inventory number plus fresh tanker attacks cannot lift the price, it means the market has already priced in a lot of risk, and traders are watching the diplomatic track — the reported Qatar and Pakistan effort to restart U.S.–Iran talks — more closely than the inventory tables. Separately, traders are weighing the prospect of U.S.-mediated Russia–Ukraine talks resuming as soon as next month, another potential source of supply.

**Elon Musk's two companies are formally exploring a merger.** Announced after yesterday's close: Tesla and SpaceX have each formed an independent special committee to evaluate combining, and Musk publicly called the idea a "good idea." Prediction markets have started quoting odds on it.

Why this matters: an *independent special committee* is the corporate governance mechanism used when the people on both sides of a deal overlap — here, the same person runs both companies and holds large stakes in each. The committee's job is to represent outside shareholders who might otherwise get a bad price. The existence of the committees does not mean a deal will happen; it means the process has moved from talk to procedure. It also helped lift Tesla's stock today ahead of its earnings report, which muddies how much of Tesla's recent move is about its actual business.

## Today's lesson: the gap, or why some prices never trade

Today's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning the study of price and trading behavior itself, rather than the business underneath it. And Super Micro Computer handed us a textbook example of something that confuses almost every new investor the first time it happens to them.

Here is the sequence. Super Micro Computer finished trading on Tuesday at $32.47. Then, after the market closed, it announced that it was raising its revenue outlook to $18.5 billion to $19.5 billion from $14.5 billion to $15.5 billion. This morning, the stock did not open at $32.47 and climb. It *opened* several dollars higher and finished the day at $35.45, up 9.18%. Now ask the question that matters: at what point could you have bought it at $33? The answer is never. No share changed hands at $33, or $34, during that entire transition. The price teleported. That empty space on the chart — where the stock closed at one price and opened at a meaningfully different one, with no trading in between — is called a **gap**. When the open is higher, it is a *gap up*; lower, a *gap down*.

Gaps exist because news does not wait for business hours, but trading mostly does. The regular session runs from 8:30 a.m. to 3:00 p.m. Central, and companies deliberately release earnings outside those hours so everyone has time to read the numbers. So the price has to make up all its ground at once, at the opening bell, through an auction that matches every accumulated overnight buy and sell order at a single clearing price. In basketball terms: normally you watch a player dribble the length of the floor and you can see every step, and a defender can step in front of him at any point. A gap is that player vanishing at the free-throw line and reappearing under the opposite basket. Nobody got a chance to guard the space in between, because the space never existed.

Three practical things follow from this. First, a gap tells you something happened that was *not already priced in* — the bigger the gap, the more the news differed from what the market expected, which is why Super Micro's $4 billion guidance raise produced a gap and a routine quarterly report usually does not. Second, the gap is where most of the move often lives: Super Micro traded as much as 12% higher early before settling at 9.18%, so someone who bought at the open paid nearly the day's best price. Third, traders watch whether a gap *holds* or *fills*. A gap that holds and keeps going is sometimes called "gap and go"; a gap that gets retraced as the day wears on, with the price drifting back toward where it closed the day before, is said to be "filling the gap." Super Micro did neither cleanly — it gapped up, pushed higher, then gave some back. Netflix gapped down this morning on last night's guidance and kept sliding, which is the bearish version of the same pattern.

And here is why this is the right lesson for tonight specifically: Meta Platforms is up about 7% after hours and Tesla about 2%, on reports released after the close. Those are not tomorrow's opening prices — after-hours trading is thin, with few participants and wide spreads, so it is a rough poll rather than a verdict. But unless the mood changes overnight, both will gap tomorrow morning, and so will IBM once its numbers land. You are about to watch three of them at once.
