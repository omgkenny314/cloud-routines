# Market Close Brief — Friday, September 18, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

On the surface this was the quietest day of a loud week: the S&P 500 finished up about a sixth of a percent, the Dow slightly down, the Nasdaq slightly up. Underneath it was one of the largest single days of mechanical trading in market history. Roughly $7 trillion of index futures, index options and single-stock options expired at once — the quarterly event called quadruple witching — and that expiration landed two days after the Federal Reserve's first interest-rate increase in three years and a few hours after the Bank of Japan raised its own rates to a 31-year high. The single biggest storyline, though, is what the bond market did with all of that: the 10-year Treasury yield climbed back above 5%, and traders moved to roughly a coin flip on whether the Fed hikes again in October. That is the number quietly setting the tone for everything else. Stocks essentially stood still while the cost of money kept creeping up, and for the week the Dow lost more than 1.5%, the S&P 500 eked out a small loss, and only the Nasdaq finished green.

## The numbers

**S&P 500: 7,650.50, up 12.74 points, or 0.17%.** A flat close on an enormous-volume expiration day. Gains in technology hardware offset weakness in health care and materials.

**Dow Jones Industrial Average: 51,682.64, down 95.40 points, or 0.18%.** The Dow holds only 30 companies and carries a heavy weight in health care and industrials, both of which lagged. UnitedHealth was among the drags.

**Nasdaq Composite: 26,522.55, up 104.25 points, or 0.39%.** The leader again, on the strength of computer and server makers. Note that all three of these closes line up exactly with Thursday's levels plus today's point changes, which is a useful internal check that the figures are right.

**10-year Treasury yield: about 5.004%, up more than 5 basis points.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. The 2-year rose more than 5 basis points to 4.743% and the 30-year rose about 4 basis points to 5.336%. Yields rose because traders raised their odds that the Fed hikes again on October 28. When the whole curve rises together like this, it is a story about policy expectations, not about any single day's news.

**Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate fell 1.6% to settle near $100.30 a barrel; Brent fell 0.9% to about $103.87.** For the week, U.S. crude finished roughly flat and Brent lost close to 1%. Oil eased because Middle East shipments have held up better than feared after last week's drone strike on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline — JPMorgan estimated total Middle East flows averaged around 17 million barrels a day over the past ten days.

**Bitcoin: roughly $78,000 to $80,900, up about 5% over 24 hours.** I found $77,979 at 8 a.m. Eastern, $78,215 late morning, and $80,861 in the early afternoon, so treat this as a strong up-move within a wide range rather than a single price. Bitcoin trades around the clock and has no official close. It rose despite higher yields, which is itself notable — it had been trading as a rate-sensitive asset all month.

## Headlines that matter

**About $7 trillion in options expired today.** Bloomberg called it the second-largest such expiration on record; Citigroup's data put the notional figure at $7.1 trillion and called it the biggest ever. Those two sources disagree, so I am giving you both. Why it matters: on a witching day, an enormous share of the buying and selling happens for contractual reasons — positions being closed out, rolled into the next quarter, or settled — rather than because anyone changed their view of a company. It inflates volume and can push prices around in the final hour. The practical lesson is to distrust the *story* attached to a big move on expiration day. Sometimes the market is just doing paperwork.

**The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate by a quarter point to 1.25%, the highest since 1995.** The vote was 7 to 2, with two members preferring to hold because core inflation, at 1.7% in August, is still below the 2% target. The yen weakened about 0.45% to around 156.64 per dollar and the Japanese 10-year government bond yield actually *fell* about 4.9 basis points to 2.947%. Why it matters, and this is worth understanding: Japan has been the world's source of cheap money for thirty years. Investors borrow in yen at near-zero rates and buy assets elsewhere, including U.S. bonds and stocks. When Japanese rates rise, that math gets less attractive and some of that money goes home — which puts upward pressure on yields everywhere, including here. The two dissenting votes are why the yen *fell* on a rate hike: the market read the split as a sign the BOJ may not keep going.

**U.S. industrial production was unchanged in August**, after rising 0.2% in July. Underneath, manufacturing output fell 0.3%, mining rose 0.1% and utilities rose 1.8%. Total industrial production stood at 103.1% of its 2017 average, 1.4% above a year earlier. Why it matters: this is the Fed's own measurement of what American factories, mines and utilities physically produced. Flat output with manufacturing shrinking says the industrial economy is stalling, not booming — which is exactly what higher interest rates are supposed to do, and exactly what makes the Fed's job uncomfortable when inflation is coming from oil rather than from too much demand. Note the utilities figure too; hot weather and data-center electricity demand both show up there.

**Traders now put the odds of another rate hike at the October 27-28 Fed meeting at roughly 50-50.** The CME FedWatch tool showed about 49%; prediction markets were clustered right around half. The Fed's target range is currently 3.75% to 4.00% after Wednesday's quarter-point increase. Why it matters: a genuine coin flip is the most uncomfortable setting for markets, because half of all positioning is wrong regardless of outcome. It also explains why yields rose today on no fresh news — when the probability is 50%, every incoming data point moves it, and the bond market reprices constantly.

## Today's lesson: gross margin, and who eats a price increase

Recent lessons have covered volume, the discount rate, support and resistance, and dilution. Today's is a fundamental one — about the business underneath the stock — and the day handed us an unusually clean example, because two groups of companies in the same industry moved in opposite directions.

Start with the definition. **Cost of goods sold** is what a company spends on the actual stuff it sells — the raw materials and components that go into the product, not the salaries of the sales team or the research lab. **Gross margin** is what's left of each sales dollar after paying that cost, expressed as a percentage. Sell a server for $100 when the parts cost $75, and your gross margin is 25%. It is the first profitability number on the income statement, and it is the one that tells you the most about the *physics* of a business, before management's choices about overhead enter the picture.

Now watch what a single price does to two different companies. Memory chip prices have been rising violently — as noted in an earlier brief, Samsung raised the price of a 32-gigabyte DDR5 memory module to $239 from $149 in September. For Micron, SanDisk or Seagate, memory *is* the product; a higher memory price is higher revenue landing on the top line. For Dell, HPE or Super Micro, memory is a purchased component; a higher memory price is a higher cost of goods sold, squeezing the gross margin on every machine they ship. Same fact, opposite effect, depending entirely on which side of the transaction you sit.

Here is where today gets interesting, and where I would rather show you the puzzle than a tidy answer. The simple version of that logic says the chipmakers should keep winning and the assemblers should keep losing. Today the market did precisely the reverse: assemblers up around ten percent, storage makers down three to four. The concept that explains the reversal is **pass-through**, sometimes called pricing power — the ability to raise your own prices when your costs rise. Dell, HP, Cisco and Lenovo have all raised hardware prices this year. If a company can pass a cost increase all the way to the customer, a rising input price does not have to hurt the margin at all; it can even help, because prices often get raised by a little more than the cost went up. If it cannot, the increase comes straight out of gross margin.

The basketball version: think of wins as revenue and payroll as cost of goods sold. Two teams win 50 games; one spends $180 million on the roster and the other $120 million. Same result on the scoreboard, very different efficiency, and the efficient one has money left for everything else. Now suppose the price of talent rises league-wide. The team in a big market raises ticket prices and renegotiates its TV deal and absorbs it. The small-market team eats it. The cost increase was identical; the consequence was not. That difference is pricing power, and it is usually a proxy for how badly customers need you.

Two cautions to carry away. Gross margin is not profit — research, sales and administrative costs all come out below it, so a company can have a fat gross margin and still lose money. And a low gross margin is not automatically bad; grocery stores run on thin margins and enormous volume, and that works fine. What you actually want to watch is the *trend* in the percentage, quarter after quarter, alongside revenue. Revenue rising while gross margin falls means a company is buying its growth by giving up profitability on every sale. That single comparison will tell you more about a business than almost any headline about it.
