# Market Close Brief — Thursday, September 17, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Yesterday the market got scared of the Federal Reserve. Today it got un-scared, and the reason had almost nothing to do with the Fed. Oil fell, Treasury yields dropped back below 5%, and stocks took off — the Nasdaq Composite up 1.69%, the S&P 500 up 1.14%, the Dow Jones Industrial Average up 0.61%. Advancing stocks beat declining ones by better than three to one on the New York Stock Exchange, so this was a broad rally rather than a handful of names dragging the averages up. The single biggest storyline is Saudi Arabia finding a workaround. After a drone attack knocked out the country's East-West pipeline last week, the Saudis began making extra crude cargoes available to Asian refiners through ship-to-ship transfers just outside the Strait of Hormuz, near Oman's Sohar port — shuttle vessels carry the oil through the strait, big tankers wait safely outside. That one piece of logistics took the fear premium out of crude, and because this whole inflation scare started with an oil shock, cheaper oil immediately loosened everything else: bond yields fell, and the technology stocks most sensitive to interest rates had a big day. Yesterday's lesson was that the Fed sets the discount rate. Today's reminder is that oil sets the Fed.

The story from yesterday — that South Korea's SK Hynix is in talks to manufacture memory chips on U.S. soil, possibly using part of Intel's delayed Ohio site — kept running. On top of that came a wave of analyst upgrades: Tigress Financial raised its price target to $145 from $118 while keeping a Buy rating, Northland moved to Outperform with a $120 target, and Melius laid out a path to $165. Volume was 147.2 million shares, about 1.6 times Intel's average over the prior 20 sessions. Hold that volume figure — it is today's lesson.

**Hewlett Packard Enterprise (HPE)** jumped 9.02% to $61.64, leading a rally in companies that build and assemble artificial-intelligence servers. **Super Micro Computer (SMCI)** rose 6.08% and **Dell Technologies (DELL)** gained 3.36%. What happened is that a bid that started earlier in the week in memory-chip stocks widened out into the companies that put those chips into finished machines. Worth noting the tension: memory prices are rising sharply — Samsung raised the price of a 32-gigabyte DDR5 memory module to $239 from $149 in September — and Super Micro buys memory to build servers. Rising memory prices are the reason its stock went up today and also a squeeze on the thin profit margins it earns on each server. Both things are true at once.

## Headlines that matter

**Saudi Arabia routed around its own broken pipeline.** After the East-West pipeline was damaged by a drone attack launched from Iraq, the Saudis started offering additional crude to Asian refiners via ship-to-ship transfers outside Hormuz. Why it matters: the entire inflation problem the Fed is fighting traces back to an oil supply shock. Raising interest rates cannot create more oil. But a shipping workaround can, in effect, un-create part of the shortage — and when it did, oil fell, bond yields fell, and stocks rose within hours. This is the cleanest evidence you will get that the market's problem right now is energy supply, not monetary policy.

**Weekly jobless claims fell to 196,000 from 206,000, well below the 208,000 economists expected.** The four-week average fell to 203,250, and continuing claims — people still collecting benefits — dropped to 1.730 million from 1.769 million. Why it matters: claims are the fastest read anyone gets on whether the job market is cracking under higher interest rates. This says it is not. That is genuinely good for company earnings, and it also removes the "the economy is breaking" argument for the Fed to stop hiking. Good news and awkward news in the same number.

**The Philadelphia Fed's September manufacturing index came in at 37.8, down from 47.4 in August but well above the 30.5 expected.** Two components inside it matter more than the headline. The prices-paid index rose to 48.6 from 40.9 — factories in that region are paying more for inputs, which is inflation showing up at the source. The employment index fell hard, to 11.8 from 27.9 — they are hiring far less aggressively. Why it matters: rising input costs plus slowing hiring is exactly the uncomfortable combination the Fed was worried about yesterday.

**Housing data was weak across the board.** August housing starts fell 2.6% to an annual rate of 1.275 million, below the 1.309 million expected. Building permits fell 2.7% to 1.394 million. Inside those numbers, single-family starts actually rose 7.6% to 918,000 while multifamily — apartment buildings — collapsed 21.7% to 357,000. Pending home sales were down 4.7% from a year ago, though the index ticked up 0.3% for the month against an expected decline. Why it matters: the 30-year mortgage rate is near 7%, and housing is the part of the economy that feels interest rates first and hardest. This is what the Fed's tightening looks like on the ground.

**Lennar's results confirmed the housing squeeze in dollar terms.** Third-quarter net earnings were $284 million, or $1.19 per diluted share, down from $591 million, or $2.29 a share, a year ago. Adjusted earnings were $1.23 against the $1.30 Wall Street expected, on revenue of $8.05 billion against roughly $8.31 billion expected. Home deliveries fell 3% to 20,840 and the average delivered price fell 3% to $372,000. New orders dropped 9%. Most tellingly, the gross margin on home sales compressed to 15.8% from 17.5%, while overhead costs rose to 9.2% of home sales from 8.2%. The company cut its full-year delivery target to about 80,000–81,000 homes from 82,000–83,000 — its second reduction this year. Why it matters: a builder facing weak demand has two choices, sell fewer homes or cut prices and pay buyers' closing costs to keep volume up. Lennar did some of both, and you can see the cost of that choice in the margin line.

**President Trump called for the Fed to cut rates to 1% "or less,"** hours after the Fed did the opposite. He said he retains confidence in Chair Kevin Warsh but blamed a "hostile" board for the hike, which was in fact unanimous. Warsh has publicly defended the increase as appropriate. Why it matters: not because it changes policy — it did not, and the vote was 12-0 against the administration's wishes — but because Fed independence is itself something markets price. A central bank that looks like it takes orders on rates loses credibility on inflation, and that shows up in long-term bond yields. So far, yields are not showing that worry.

## What to watch tomorrow

**Friday, September 18 is quadruple witching**, sometimes called triple witching — the quarterly date when stock index futures, index options, single-stock options and single-stock futures all expire on the same day. It falls on the third Friday of March, June, September and December. Why you care: it is usually the heaviest-volume session of the quarter, and large amounts of trading happen for mechanical reasons — contracts being closed, rolled forward or settled — rather than because anyone changed their mind about a company. Big moves on expiration day often mean less than they look like. This one arrives two days after the Fed's first rate hike in three years, into a market still rearranging itself.

**Industrial production and capacity utilization** for August is the scheduled data release, from the Federal Reserve's own G.17 report. It measures how much American factories, mines and utilities actually produced, and how much of their capacity they used doing it.

One source indicated the Bank of Japan's September meeting also concludes tomorrow, which would matter for global bond yields. I could only verify that from a single source, so treat it as unconfirmed.

## Today's lesson: volume, or how to tell conviction from a twitch

Recent lessons have covered the discount rate, support and resistance, dilution and the four prices of a trading day. Today's is technical — about the behavior of trading itself rather than the business underneath — and Intel handed us the example.

Here is the concept. **Volume** is simply the number of shares that changed hands. Every price you hear quoted is the result of one buyer and one seller agreeing. Volume tells you how many such agreements it took to get there. On its own it means nothing; a share count is not an opinion. It becomes information the moment you compare it to what is normal for that particular stock.

Intel closed up 7.67% today on 147.2 million shares, about 1.6 times its average over the prior 20 trading sessions. Read those two facts together and you learn something neither one gives you alone. A 7.67% move on thin volume would mean a small number of people pushed the price around in a quiet market — real, but fragile, and prone to snapping back. A 7.67% move on 1.6 times normal volume means a genuinely large number of participants showed up and transacted at those higher prices. Analysts publish, institutions rebalance, index funds adjust. Technical analysts call this **confirmation**: the price move and the participation agree with each other, which makes the move more likely to stick.

The basketball version. A team wins by 20. That is the price change. Now ask who scored. If one guy went off for 45 and everyone else was quiet, you have a hot hand, and hot hands cool off — next game could look nothing like this one. If eight players scored in double figures against a good defense, that 20-point win is telling you something durable about the team. Volume is your box score. It answers "how many people were actually involved in this?"

Today gave you the market-wide version of the same reading, and it is worth learning to listen for. Advancing stocks outnumbered declining stocks 3.07 to one on the New York Stock Exchange and 2.8 to one on the Nasdaq. That figure is called **breadth**, and it is volume's cousin — instead of counting shares, it counts companies. The S&P 500's 1.14% gain could in principle have come from five enormous stocks rising while four hundred others fell. Breadth of three to one says no: almost everything went up. The index gain and the participation agree. That is a healthier-looking rally than the same percentage gain would be on narrow breadth, and now you have a specific number to ask for whenever you hear that the market went up.

To be clear, this is a lens for reading what happened, not a signal to act on. Heavy volume confirms that a lot of people committed to a price. It does not tell you they were right.
