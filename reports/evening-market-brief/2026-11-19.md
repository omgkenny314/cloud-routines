## The day in one paragraph

The most anticipated stock market debut in history happened today, it went well for the company, and the market fell apart anyway. Anthropic — the artificial intelligence company behind the Claude assistant — priced its initial public offering at $83 per share, above its own expected range, raised roughly $81 billion, which is the largest IPO ever completed, and opened for trading at $111.50, a 34% jump. Then the stock faded all afternoon, finishing up around 15% from the offer price rather than 34%, and the rest of the market went down with it. The Dow Jones Industrial Average was down more than 1,000 points at its worst, the S&P 500 looks to have logged a sixth consecutive losing day, and the single clearest read of the day came from Capital Economics: the debut "failed to rekindle enthusiasm for tech stocks." That is the storyline. For three weeks this market has been asking whether the AI trade still has buyers, and today it got the biggest possible test — a $2 trillion company arriving on the public market — and the answer was that investors used the occasion to sell. Underneath that, three other things pressed in the same direction: health insurers kept falling on a federal audit proposal, Advanced Micro Devices was hit by a short-seller report, and a Federal Reserve that is expected to *raise* rates next month kept bond yields above 5%.

## Big movers and why

## Headlines that matter

**The biggest IPO in history did not rescue the AI trade — it became the referendum that the AI trade lost.** Anthropic raised about $81 billion, opened up 34%, and by the closing bell the enthusiasm had drained to 15% while the S&P 500 fell for a sixth day. *Why it matters:* for weeks the open question has been whether money is still willing to flow into AI at these prices. A listing like this is the cleanest possible test, because it forces real investors to write real checks at a real valuation on a single day. They did — the deal was massively oversubscribed — but the people who already owned AI stocks used the moment to sell, and a chunk of the buyers of the new stock sold it the same afternoon. That pattern, enthusiasm at the open and exhaustion by the close, is what analysts mean when they say sentiment has turned. It does not tell you the AI business is bad. It tells you the *price* of AI assets no longer has automatic buyers behind it.

**A December rate hike is now essentially priced in, and a half-point move is on the table.** Futures markets put the odds of a quarter-point increase at the Fed's December 8–9 meeting at roughly 90% to 92%, taking the target range to 4.00% to 4.25%, with a small but real probability of a half-point move. Yesterday's October minutes showed almost all officials expect further increases, and Governor Christopher Waller has publicly floated either a half-point hike in December or a quarter-point hike paired with a clear signal of another in January. *Why it matters:* this is the gravitational force under everything else. When the yield on risk-free government debt is above 5% and heading higher, every dollar of future corporate profit is worth less today, and the companies hurt most are the ones whose profits are furthest in the future — which is exactly the description of an AI company. The Anthropic fade and the 5%-plus 10-year yield are not two stories. They are one story told twice.

**Jobless claims rose, and the Philadelphia Fed survey fell off a cliff.** Initial claims for unemployment benefits came in at 203,000 for the week ended November 14, up 1,000 and above the roughly 196,000 to 198,000 expected. Continuing claims — people still collecting after the first week — jumped 70,000 to 1,763,000. Separately, the Philadelphia Fed's manufacturing index collapsed to 6.3 from 20.9 in October, against expectations near 20, the weakest reading since May. Its prices-paid component, however, *rose* to 58.2. The Conference Board's leading economic index fell 0.3% in October. *Why it matters:* this is the uncomfortable combination. The index measuring regional factory activity fell hard while the index measuring what those factories pay for inputs went up. Slowing growth with rising input costs is the setup that leaves a central bank with no good options — cut to help growth and you feed inflation, hike to fight inflation and you deepen the slowdown. It also explains why a weak survey did not produce a stock rally: in a hiking cycle, bad economic news is just bad news.

**Walmart beat, raised, and fell — and that is a signal about the market, not about Walmart.** *Why it matters:* this is the third major retailer this week to deliver good results and get no credit for them, after Target and Lowe's. When beats stop being rewarded, it usually means expectations have run ahead of reality and valuations are doing the work that earnings used to do. Watch for it as a pattern: a market that stops paying for good news is a market whose mood has changed, regardless of what the companies report.

**A small practical item worth knowing.** The new Series I savings bond rate was set at 5.51%. I Bonds are U.S. government savings bonds whose rate adjusts with inflation. That is context, not a recommendation — but it is a useful benchmark for what "safe" currently pays, and when safe pays 5.51%, every riskier asset has to clear a higher bar.

**The dollar kept climbing**, with the dollar index touching a 19-month high near 103.96. *Why it matters:* a strong dollar follows directly from expected rate hikes — higher U.S. rates attract foreign money, which must buy dollars first. It also quietly reduces the overseas earnings of big U.S. multinationals when converted back home.

## Today's lesson: a stock with no chart, and where support and resistance come from

Tonight's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning we read the behavior of the price itself rather than the company's financial statements. The concept is **support and resistance**, and today handed us the rarest possible teaching case: a stock that had none, because it had never traded before.

Start with the jargon, defined once. **Support** is a price level where buyers have reliably shown up in the past, so the price has tended to stop falling there. **Resistance** is the mirror image: a level where sellers have reliably shown up, so the price has tended to stop rising there. Both ideas depend entirely on *memory*. They are not laws of physics. A level is support because a lot of people bought there before and remember doing it, and because a lot of other people watched the price bounce there and expect it to happen again. The level works, when it works, because enough participants share the same reference point.

Now consider Anthropic this morning. At 9:30 a.m. the stock had no trading history whatsoever — no prior closes, no moving average, no previous high, nothing. Every tool in the technical toolbox was unavailable, because every one of them is computed from past prices. This is what traders mean by **price discovery**: the process of a market finding a price when there is no prior price to anchor to. It took until 11:48 a.m. for the exchange to match enough buyers and sellers to print the first trade, and that trade — 108 million shares at $111.50 — was the stock's entire history as of that moment.

Watch what happened over the next four hours, because it is the clearest demonstration you will ever get of support and resistance being *created*. The stock ran up to $114.99 and stopped. It then fell all the way to $92.72 and stopped. It finished around $95.50 to $96.90. By the closing bell this stock had three reference levels it did not have at breakfast: a high near $115, a low near $92.72, and the $83 IPO price, which is the number every early buyer measures their gain against. Tomorrow, those levels will matter — not because they are magic, but because thousands of people now share them as reference points. Someone who bought at $110 and watched it fall to $93 has a price in mind at which they will sell to get their money back. Someone who missed the IPO and watched it bounce off $92.72 has a price in mind at which they would buy. Those intentions are what support and resistance actually are.

Here is the basketball version. A technical analyst reading a stock chart is a scout watching game tape. For a veteran, you have years of film: you know he cannot finish with his left hand, you know he disappears in the fourth quarter, you know exactly where on the floor he is dangerous. That accumulated knowledge is what support and resistance represent — a crowd's memory of how this thing has behaved before. Anthropic this morning was a rookie who had never played a minute of organized basketball in front of anyone. There was no tape. Nobody knew where his range ended. So the first game *became* the tape, and the whole arena spent four hours finding out together. That is why the price swung from $115 to $92.72 in a single session: when there is no shared memory, the only way to find the level is to overshoot it in both directions.

The practical habit to build from this is a skeptical one, and it will protect you. When someone tells you a stock has "strong support" at some price, the question to ask is: support built out of what? How many shares actually traded at that level, and how long ago? A level where millions of shares changed hands over months is a real shared memory. A level touched once, briefly, in thin trading is a line somebody drew on a chart. And for a stock that has existed for one day, there is no such thing as technical support at all — only a range, discovered in public, that everyone is now watching.
