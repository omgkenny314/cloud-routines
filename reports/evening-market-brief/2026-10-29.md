# Market Close Brief — Thursday, October 29, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a day when the headline index number told you almost nothing about what actually happened. The S&P 500 slipped three tenths of a percent, which sounds like a quiet session. It was not. Underneath that small number, the market pulled itself apart: Alphabet — the parent company of Google — fell about 12% and took roughly half a trillion dollars of market value with it, its worst single day since it went public in 2004. Software and cybersecurity names were destroyed, with ServiceNow and Fortinet each down around 20% or more. And yet eight of the eleven S&P 500 sectors finished *higher*, small-company stocks rallied about 1%, and healthcare closed at a record on the back of a very strong Eli Lilly report.

The single biggest storyline is what you might call the great AI sorting. For two years the market treated "spending enormous sums on artificial intelligence" as automatically good news. Today it stopped doing that and started asking a harder question: is this spending actually producing profit yet? Alphabet spent heavily and reported negative free cash flow, and was punished. Microsoft spent heavily and beat on both revenue and earnings, and was rewarded — briefly, before giving the gain back. ServiceNow got hit not for spending too much but for a fear running the other direction, that AI will disrupt traditional software companies rather than enrich them. Meanwhile money that left big technology did not leave the market; it moved into drug makers, industrials' cheaper cousins, and small caps, helped along by inflation data that was not as bad as feared and a growth report that was better than expected. A rotation, not a retreat.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: closed at 7,720.39, down 23.59 points, or about 0.30%.** A record high the day before became a modest step back. The decline was almost entirely the work of a handful of giant technology names; the typical stock in the index had a decent day.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: closed at 52,342.56, down 132.54 points, or about 0.25%.** Caterpillar did most of the damage here, falling roughly 8% after its forecast underwhelmed. Because the Dow holds no Alphabet shares, it largely sidestepped the day's main event.

- **Nasdaq Composite: closed at 26,184.73, down 365.13 points, or about 1.38%.** More than four times the S&P's percentage decline, because the Nasdaq is dense with exactly the big technology names that were being sold. The technology sector overall fell about 1.75%.

- **Russell 2000 (small-company index): closed at about 2,859, up roughly 1.01%** — a more-than-three-week high. Worth including tonight even though it is not on your usual list, because it is the clearest evidence that today was a rotation rather than a sell-off.

- **10-year Treasury yield: about 4.99%, down roughly 5 to 6 basis points from 5.05%.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. This snapped a four-day run of rising yields. Yields eased because the inflation report, while higher than last month, came in no worse than economists expected — which takes a little pressure off the case for another Fed rate hike. Two sources put the close at 4.99% and 4.988%; a third had it at 5.01% earlier in the session.

- **Crude oil: U.S. West Texas Intermediate settled at about $102.00 a barrel, down roughly 78 cents, or about 0.76%; Brent, the international benchmark, settled near $114.88.** I should be straight with you about this one: that settlement figure comes from a single source. Two other reports had WTI lower during the day, near $100.44 and $101.33. The direction — modestly lower, after a big jump the day before — is consistent across all of them, but treat the exact settle as unconfirmed.

- **Bitcoin: roughly $80,000 to $81,600.** Bitcoin trades around the clock and has no official close, so this is a range rather than a settlement, and the sources genuinely disagree within it. It is holding roughly where it has been all week. Expensive oil and yields near 5% are both headwinds for it, and neither changed much today.

## Big movers and why

- **Alphabet (GOOGL), the parent of Google: down about 12%.** The day's defining move, and by one account the largest single-day loss of market value in the company's history, around $500 billion. Reported percentages ranged from 11.8% to about 13% depending on when they were measured; the 11.8% figure appeared in end-of-day coverage. Two things did the damage. First, free cash flow came in at *negative* $9.07 billion — the company spent more cash than its operations brought in, because of the enormous build-out of AI data centers. Second, and more fundamentally, core Search revenue was $63.35 billion against expectations of roughly $64.9 billion to $65.0 billion. Search is the engine. When the engine misses while the fuel bill soars, you get today.

- **ServiceNow (NOW), a business-software company: down roughly 20% to 25%**, described in multiple reports as its worst day on record. It reported after Wednesday's close, so today was the reaction. Adjusted earnings came in at $1.00 per share against $1.02 expected, and while revenue of $4.11 billion beat, the guidance for next quarter's revenue and backlog disappointed. The reason this one landed so hard is a worry analysts have started calling the "SaaSpocalypse" — the fear that AI will let customers build their own tools instead of renting software, turning a reliable growth business into a shrinking one. The exact percentage varies a lot across sources, from about 15% to 27%, because they measure different moments.

- **Fortinet (FTNT), a cybersecurity company: down roughly 20% to 27%** *despite beating expectations* — adjusted earnings of $0.91 per share against $0.86 expected, on revenue of $2.08 billion. What mattered was slowing product-revenue growth and a trimmed outlook for service revenue, plus at least two analyst downgrades. Rival cybersecurity names including Palo Alto Networks, CrowdStrike and Zscaler slipped in sympathy. A beat is not a guarantee; the outlook usually matters more than the quarter.

- **Caterpillar (CAT), the construction and mining equipment maker: down about 8%,** to roughly $656.14. It fell as much as 12% during the session. Caterpillar also beat on its results, but left its annual sales forecast unchanged when investors wanted it raised. As a Dow member with a high share price, its decline was the single largest drag on that index.

- **Eli Lilly (LLY), the drug maker behind Zepbound and Mounjaro: up about 6% to 7%, to a record high.** The clear winner. Third-quarter revenue grew 39% to $24.4 billion, it beat expectations comfortably, and it raised its full-year revenue outlook to roughly $91 billion to $92 billion. Lilly is large enough that this single stock lifted the entire healthcare sector to a record close.

- **Microsoft (MSFT): rose about 2% early, then gave up the gain.** It beat on both lines — earnings of $4.89 per share on revenue of $92.28 billion, against expectations of about $4.73 on $90.63 billion. That it could not hold the gain on a clean beat tells you something about the mood: today the market was not in a paying mood even for good AI news.

## Headlines that matter

**Inflation came in hot but not surprising, and that distinction moved markets.** The September PCE price index — Personal Consumption Expenditures, the inflation measure the Federal Reserve actually targets — rose 3.6% from a year earlier, up from 3.4% in August, and 0.5% for the month. Core PCE, which strips out food and energy because those are volatile, rose 3.1% annually and 0.3% monthly. **Why it matters:** both headline and core are now at their highest since May, which is genuinely uncomfortable. But core landed exactly on expectations, and that is why bond yields *fell* on a hot inflation print. Markets trade on the gap between reality and expectation, not on the level itself. Nothing in this report forced the Fed's hand further than it already was.

**The economy grew faster than anyone expected.** Third-quarter GDP — gross domestic product, the total value of everything the economy produces — came in at a 3.4% annualized rate, against estimates of 3.1% to 3.2%, and accelerating from 2.2% in the second quarter. **Why it matters:** this is a double-edged number. Strong growth supports company earnings, which is why cyclical and small-company stocks rallied today. But strong growth alongside 3%-plus inflation is exactly the combination that keeps a central bank thinking about raising rates rather than cutting them. Note that this is the *advance* estimate; the Bureau of Economic Analysis will revise it twice.

**The job market is still tight.** Initial jobless claims fell to 194,000 for the week ending October 24, below forecasts of about 198,000 to 200,000, and by one description near the lowest levels since the late 1960s. **Why it matters:** few layoffs means workers keep bargaining power, which means wages keep rising, which feeds back into inflation. It is good news for people with jobs and awkward news for anyone hoping for rate cuts.

**Yesterday's Fed decision is still the backdrop.** The Federal Reserve held its benchmark rate at 3.75%–4.00% on Wednesday in a 10–2 vote. The two dissenters — Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack and Governor Lisa Cook — wanted a rate *hike*, not a cut. **Why it matters:** dissents in the hawkish direction, meaning toward higher rates, are unusual and they tell you where the internal argument sits. Markets are now pricing a high probability of a December increase; one report put those odds at 85%. For context on how much the world has changed: a year ago the debate was about how fast to cut.

**The European Central Bank held rates too, and the euro fell to a 17-month low against the dollar.** **Why it matters:** mostly through the dollar. A stronger dollar makes U.S. exports more expensive abroad and trims the overseas earnings of big American multinationals when converted back into dollars — a slow headwind rather than a daily one, but a real one for companies like Caterpillar that sell heavily outside the United States.

## After the bell

It is early in the after-hours session, so these are first reactions and the numbers may be revised. Two large reports are already out, and both were taken badly.

**Amazon (AMZN)** reported revenue of about $201.9 billion, up roughly 12% from a year ago and broadly in line with expectations. Amazon Web Services, its cloud division, grew 39% to about $46.0 billion with $17.8 billion in operating income, and management guided to over 40% growth in both 2027 and 2028. Total operating income was $28.1 billion, up sharply from $17.4 billion a year earlier. The problems were below the headline: earnings came in at $1.89 per share, a few percent short of the roughly $1.96 consensus, and advertising revenue of $19.64 billion badly missed the $20.82 billion expected. The company is holding 2026 capital spending at about $220 billion for AI infrastructure, and free cash flow turned negative. The stock fell roughly 8% to 10% after hours, from a base that was already down about 3.7% in the regular session. Note how closely this rhymes with Alphabet: strong cloud growth, heavy spending, negative free cash flow, and a miss in the advertising business. The market is applying the same test to both.

**Intel (INTC)** actually beat, and fell harder than anyone. Revenue was about $16.5 billion, up 21% year over year, with adjusted earnings of $0.52 per share against roughly $0.40 expected. Then came the guidance: fourth-quarter revenue of $15 billion to $16 billion against about $17 billion expected, and a chief financial officer saying the slowdown in the personal-computer market should last at least through the first half of next year. Shares fell more than 16% after hours by one account, more than 20% by another before paring losses. Intel had already dropped about 20% coming into the report.

**Still scheduled to report tonight:** Coinbase (COIN), the cryptocurrency exchange; Reddit (RDDT); and SanDisk (SNDK), the memory-storage maker. I did not find results from any of these yet. Forecast is around +0.8% for the quarter, after 0.9% in the second quarter. Given the Fed's current posture, this is the most important number on the calendar: wage growth is the mechanism by which inflation becomes self-sustaining.

Watch whether the after-hours declines hold or fade, and watch whether the rotation out of big technology and into smaller and more defensive names continues for a second day. One day is an event; two days is a trend.

## Today's lesson: market breadth, or how to tell a rotation from a sell-off

Here is tonight's puzzle, and it is a good one. The S&P 500 fell 0.30% today. If that were all you knew, you would reasonably conclude that most stocks went down a little. Now add one more fact: **eight of the eleven sectors in that index closed higher.** Healthcare closed at a record. Small-company stocks rose 1%. So most stocks went *up* on a day the index went *down*. How?

The concept that resolves this is **market breadth**. Breadth asks a question the index level cannot answer: *how many* stocks participated in today's move, as opposed to *how much* the index moved. The simplest measure is the advance-decline count — how many stocks rose versus how many fell. Sector counts, like today's eight-of-eleven, do the same job at a coarser level. You already know the mechanical reason the two can disagree: the S&P 500 is weighted by company size, so a $2 trillion company moves it hundreds of times more than a $20 billion one. Breadth is what you do with that knowledge. Weighting explains how the gap opens; breadth is the signal you read in the gap.

So read today. The index fell, but it fell because a very small number of very large technology companies fell hard — Alphabet alone shed something like half a trillion dollars. Strip those few names out and you find a market that was broadly, quietly fine: drug makers at records, small caps up, eight sectors green, bond yields easing. That is the signature of a **rotation**, where money moves from one part of the market to another. It looks very different from a **sell-off**, where the index falls *and* breadth is bad — most stocks down, most sectors red, nowhere to hide. Both produce a red number on the index. They mean close to opposite things about the market's health.

The basketball version: your team loses by three, and you want to know whether to worry. One version is that everybody shot badly, the bench gave you nothing, and you got outrebounded all night. That is a sell-off — the whole roster underperformed, and you have a real problem. The other version is that nine players had fine games, you controlled the glass, and your All-Star went 2-for-17 from the field. Same three-point loss on the scoreboard. Completely different film session. Breadth is how you check which game you just watched.

Two honest cautions, because breadth is a tool and not an oracle. First, good breadth underneath a falling index is reassuring about the *average* stock, but it does not make the giants irrelevant — those companies are a huge share of most people's index funds, so a day like today genuinely costs money even while most stocks rise. Second, breadth is most useful as a pattern over weeks, not as a single reading. One day of rotation tells you today's decline was narrow. It does not tell you whether the money leaving big technology is coming back. That is what the next several sessions are for, and now you know what to look at while you wait.

As always, none of this is a recommendation to buy or sell anything. It is a way of reading a red number so the red number has less power over you.
