# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, September 30, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

The market got the inflation number it wanted, rallied on it for most of the day, and then handed the gains back in the last hour. That is the whole story of the final session of September and of the third quarter. The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge came in cooler than expected on every line that mattered, Treasury yields eased, and the S&P 500 was up as much as roughly 0.7% during the morning. By the closing bell the S&P 500 was down 0.25%, the Dow Jones Industrial Average was down 443 points, and only the Nasdaq Composite hung onto a small gain. The most commonly offered explanation for the fade is quarter-end rebalancing — large funds mechanically selling what has run up and buying what has lagged, to get their portfolios back to target weights before the quarter's books close. That is a plausible and largely unsatisfying reason, and it is worth being honest that "quarter-end flows" is the explanation people reach for when there is no single headline to point at. The second storyline is the split between the three indexes: the Nasdaq up, the S&P 500 slightly down, the Dow down hard. That gap of more than a full percentage point between the Dow and the Nasdaq in one session is not noise, and the section below on the numbers explains where it comes from. The third storyline arrives after the bell, where Micron Technology reported a very large quarter and guided even higher.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: 7,651.54, down 0.25%** (about 19 points). Up as much as roughly 0.7% intraday on the cool inflation report, then sold off into the close. For September the index fell about 0.5%; for the third quarter it gained about 2%.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 50,906.05, down 443.87 points, or 0.86%.** By far the weakest of the three. The Dow is *price-weighted*, meaning a component's influence depends on its share price rather than the size of the company — so a handful of high-priced industrial and financial names moving down can outweigh a broad advance in technology. September was a bad month for the Dow: sources I could reach put the monthly decline between 4.3% and 4.9%, so read it as "down roughly four to five percent."
- **Nasdaq Composite: 26,861.06, up 0.24%** (about 64 points). The only index to finish green, and the only major index to finish September higher — up about 1.7% for the month. Chips and large-cap technology led all day.
- **10-year Treasury yield: about 5.23%, down roughly 2 basis points.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Yields eased because softer inflation reduces the pressure on the Fed to keep raising rates.
- **30-year Treasury yield: about 5.56%, down about 1 basis point.** Essentially flat, and still parked near its highest level since 2002. Note what did *not* happen: good inflation news did not pull the long end of the bond market down. That end of the market is worried about something slower-moving than next month's Fed meeting.
- **Crude oil: roughly $90 to $91.50 for West Texas Intermediate during the session — modestly higher, but I could not verify a settlement price.** WTI is the U.S. benchmark grade. Tuesday was the big move: WTI fell 3.5% to settle at $89.38 and Brent, the international grade, fell 2.6% to $102.59, as Middle East crude exports recovered to about 16.3 million barrels a day, the most since the Iran conflict began in February. For Wednesday itself my sources conflicted badly on Brent — one aggregator showed $97.09, others had it holding above $100 — so I am not going to give you a Brent number I cannot stand behind.
- **Bitcoin: roughly $83,000 to $84,500.** Bitcoin trades around the clock, so there is no closing price. My cleanest late-session read was about $83,500, roughly flat to slightly lower on the day. The quarter, though, was excellent: bitcoin ended June at $58,524, so it closed the third quarter up very sharply.

## Big movers and why

- **Micron Technology (MU), up about 1.1% to roughly $1,072 in the regular session — and up far more after the bell.** Memory chips are the story of this market. Micron rose into its own earnings report on continued optimism about AI-driven demand for high-bandwidth memory. The report itself is covered in the "After the bell" section, and it was a big one.
- **Moderna (MRNA), down about 7%, falling toward $185.** Citigroup downgraded the stock two steps, from Neutral all the way to Sell. The reasoning is worth sitting with, because the analyst was not saying the science is bad — he was saying the price already assumes the science is good. Moderna has run up more than 200% since positive late-stage results last August for intismeran autogene, a personalized cancer treatment developed with Merck. Citi's point was that at roughly $80 billion of market value, Moderna is worth about what Regeneron is worth, while being expected to earn considerably less. Citi actually *raised* its price target, from $60 to $80, and still called it a Sell — the target implies roughly 60% downside from where the stock trades.
- **Jabil (JBL), down 6.11% to $299.35 — after beating expectations.** Jabil is a contract manufacturer; it builds electronics for other companies. Fiscal fourth-quarter core earnings came in at $4.40 a share against roughly $4.06 expected, on revenue of $10.6 billion against about $9.69 billion expected. Revenue grew 29% from a year earlier and core earnings grew 34%. The stock fell anyway. This is one of the most useful things a beginner can watch happen: a company can clear the bar and still fall, because the stock price going in already assumed it would clear the bar. What moves a stock is the gap between results and expectations, not the results themselves.
- **Large-cap technology led the advance.** Intraday, Alphabet, Apple and Microsoft were each up around 2%; Apple traded up about 1.75% near $335 and Nvidia up about 1.48% near $231, with Intel higher by as much as 3% at one point. These are midday marks rather than closing prices — with the late-day fade, the closing figures were likely smaller, and I could not verify individual closes.

## Headlines that matter

**The Fed's preferred inflation gauge came in cooler than expected, across the board.** The August personal consumption expenditures price index — PCE, the measure the Fed actually targets — rose 0.3% for the month, putting the twelve-month rate at 3.4%. Economists surveyed by Dow Jones expected 0.3% and 3.7%. The core reading, which strips out food and energy because those two bounce around too much to show the underlying trend, rose just 0.2% for the month and 3.0% over the year, against forecasts of 0.3% and 3.3%. *Why it matters:* the Fed raised rates on September 16 for the first time since 2023, and most officials have signaled at least one more increase this year. A 3.0% core reading, down from 3.3% in July, is the first real evidence in a while that the tightening is working. Rate-hike odds for the October meeting fell after the release; markets still expect an increase by December. *One honest caveat:* part of the improvement came from the Bureau of Economic Analysis's annual methodology overhaul for how it measures software, legal services and portfolio management fees — not purely from prices cooling. When the ruler changes at the same time the measurement improves, be a little careful about how much credit to give the economy.

**Hiring picked up, and wage growth did not.** ADP reported that private employers added 90,000 jobs in September, comfortably above the roughly 68,000 expected, reversing a three-month slowdown. Education and health services contributed 55,000, leisure and hospitality 22,000, manufacturing 17,000 and construction 15,000. Base pay for workers who stayed in their jobs rose 3.2% from a year earlier. *Why it matters:* this is close to the best combination the Fed could ask for. More people working means the economy is not cracking, while pay growth in the low 3% range means employers are not being forced into the wage-price spiral that makes inflation self-sustaining. ADP processes payroll for roughly 400,000 U.S. businesses, so this is built from real payroll records rather than a survey — but it is a private estimate, and the government's own jobs report is the number that carries more weight.

**Second-quarter economic growth was revised sharply higher.** The Bureau of Economic Analysis's third and final estimate put real gross domestic product growth at 2.2% annualized for April through June, revised up from 1.5% in the previous estimate. GDP is the total value of everything the economy produced, adjusted for inflation. The upward revision came from investment, consumer spending and government spending. *Why it matters:* growth of 2.2% alongside cooling inflation is the "soft landing" combination — the economy expanding while price pressure fades. It also complicates the tightening case: it is harder to argue the economy needs to be slowed down when the last read on inflation improved on its own.

**A government shutdown was not a risk tonight, and that is worth knowing.** Federal fiscal year 2027 begins October 1, which in most years means a funding deadline and a market holding its breath. Not this time: Congress passed a continuing resolution that was signed into law on September 2, funding the government at prior-year levels through December 11. The Senate vote was 90 to 6 and the House vote was 370 to 48. *Why it matters:* mostly because of what it removes. Last fiscal year opened with the longest shutdown in modern history. Markets got an ordinary quarter-end this year instead of a political one, and the next funding fight lands in December.

**Context on the Fed.** On September 16 the Fed raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00%, in a unanimous 12–0 vote, its first increase since 2023. Chair Kevin Warsh has argued that underlying inflation was not slowing. Today's PCE report is the first significant piece of data to push back on that. *Why it matters:* one month is not a trend, and the Fed has said so repeatedly. But a 3.0% core reading is the kind of number that, repeated twice more, changes the conversation from "how many more hikes" to "when do they stop."

## After the bell

It is early in the after-hours session. Here is what is out so far.

**Micron Technology (MU) reported fiscal fourth-quarter results, and they were enormous.** Revenue came in at $54.23 billion against roughly $51.07 billion expected. Adjusted earnings were $33.42 a share against about $31.35 expected. Adjusted gross margin — the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of making the product — was 87.0%, ahead of the 86.1% expected. For a company that manufactures physical memory chips in multibillion-dollar fabrication plants, an 87% gross margin is a remarkable number, and it tells you demand is running far ahead of supply. Adjusted free cash flow was $33.20 billion for the quarter and $62.31 billion for the fiscal year, and the company finished the year with about $73.48 billion in cash, marketable investments and restricted cash.

The guidance was the bigger news. For the fiscal first quarter Micron guided to revenue of $61.5 billion, plus or minus $1.5 billion, against roughly $57.02 billion expected, and adjusted earnings of $38.15 a share, plus or minus $1.00, against about $35.40 expected. Read those against the quarter just reported: the company is telling investors the *next* quarter will be roughly 13% larger than the one it just described as a record. The one soft spot was the gross margin outlook of about 86.25%, a shade under the roughly 86.4% consensus.

Shares rose sharply in after-hours trading — I saw a figure of roughly 14% higher, toward $1,200. After-hours quotes this early are thin and move around, and the specific figure I found did not reconcile cleanly against the regular-session close, so treat the exact percentage as approximate. ## Today's lesson: guidance, or why the market pays for next quarter and not last quarter

Here is something that happened twice today, in opposite directions, and it teaches one idea.

Jabil reported a fiscal fourth quarter with revenue up 29% and core earnings up 34%, both comfortably ahead of what analysts expected. The stock fell 6.11%. Micron reported a quarter that beat expectations by more than 6% on both revenue and earnings, and the stock jumped roughly 14% after hours. The difference was not in the quarters. It was in what each company said about what comes next. Micron told investors the coming quarter would bring about $61.5 billion in revenue — bigger than the record $54.23 billion it had just reported, and about $4.5 billion more than analysts had pencilled in. That forward statement is called **guidance**: a company's own forecast of its next quarter or year. And guidance, far more often than results, is what actually moves a stock on earnings day.

The reason is that a share of stock is a claim on a company's *future* profits, not its past ones. Last quarter's earnings have already been earned; that cash is in the bank and already reflected in the stock price. What you are buying, at any price you pay today, is everything the company will earn from this moment forward. So when a company raises its own forecast, it is not reporting news about the past — it is revising the thing you are actually buying. When results are strong but guidance is merely fine, the report tells you the past was good and says nothing new about the future, and a stock that had already run up on the assumption of a good quarter has no reason to go higher.

The basketball version: no general manager signs a free agent because of last season's scoring average. They sign him for the next three seasons. Last year's numbers are useful only as evidence about what the next three years will look like. If a player puts up a great season and then shows up to camp out of shape and talking about load management, the great season does not save the contract negotiation. That is Jabil. If a player puts up a great season and the training staff says he has added a jump shot over the summer, you pay up. That is Micron.

One caution before you go looking for this pattern. Guidance is the company's own forecast, issued by people who have every incentive for the stock to go up, and it is not audited the way reported results are. Management teams routinely set the bar where they are confident of clearing it. The skill worth building is not "buy when guidance is raised" — it is noticing the gap between what the company says about the future and what the stock price already assumes about the future. That is also exactly what the Citigroup analyst was arguing about Moderna today: he did not dispute that the cancer program is working. He argued that after a 200%-plus run, the price already assumes it works. Same idea, pointed the other way.
