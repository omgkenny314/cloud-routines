# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, November 3, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today the market split in half again, and this time technology was on the winning side of the split. The Nasdaq Composite rose 0.83% and the S&P 500 rose 0.36%, snapping a three-day losing streak, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 0.38% — and almost all of that Dow weakness was one stock, Apple. The single biggest storyline is a bounce in chipmakers: the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, which tracks the chip industry, had fallen roughly 11.6% over the three prior sessions as investors panicked about how much money these companies are spending on artificial intelligence, and today buyers came back in. Nvidia rose nearly 3% and was the single strongest upward force on the S&P 500. But notice what this was *not* — it was not a broad, confident rally. It was today's Election Day for the midterm congressional elections, and volume and conviction were light while the country voted. The S&P 500 spent the whole day in a narrow band around a quarter to a half percent higher, and small companies went nowhere. Underneath it all sits the fact that makes this market genuinely unusual: investors now think the Federal Reserve's next move is a rate *increase*, not a cut, and they put the odds somewhere between 71% and 85% for the December meeting. So the day was a technology rebound on a quiet election holiday, with a hawkish central bank and an uncounted election both sitting in the background.

## Headlines that matter

- **Today was the midterm congressional election, and the results are not in yet.** Republicans went into today holding the Senate roughly 53-47 and the House of Representatives by something like 219-214, with sources differing slightly on the exact counts. A party needs 218 seats for a House majority, and Democrats need a net gain of three seats to take the Senate. **Why it matters:** markets do not care much who wins in a partisan sense; they care about what a government can and cannot do. A divided government means tax and spending legislation mostly stops, which markets often shrug at or even like. But it also raises the odds of a debt-ceiling standoff — a fight over whether to let the Treasury keep borrowing to pay bills Congress already approved — and those fights have moved markets badly in the past. One more thing worth knowing about today's trading: prediction markets have become large enough to be a real-time read on this. The Kalshi exchange had about $247 million of notional volume on the House outcome and $177 million on the Senate by late morning.

- **The Federal Reserve's next move is probably a rate hike, and that is the most important fact in this market.** At its October 28 meeting the Fed held its benchmark rate at 3.75% to 4.00%, but the vote was 10-2, and both dissenters — Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack — wanted a *quarter-point increase*. Chair Warsh said politics played "no role" in the timing ahead of the elections. Markets now price the odds of a December hike somewhere between 71% and 85% depending on which venue you check, and the next meeting is December 8-9. **Why it matters:** higher interest rates make safe bonds pay more, which makes risky stocks relatively less attractive, and they raise borrowing costs for every company funding a build-out with debt. For two years the market's question was "how fast will they cut?" The question is now "how far will they hike?" That inverts the logic behind a lot of the positioning investors still hold.

- **The manufacturing economy is running hotter than expected, which feeds the hike case.** The Institute for Supply Management's manufacturing index for October, released yesterday, came in at 55.5, up from 54.5 and above forecasts near 54.7. Any reading above 50 means the sector is expanding, and this was the tenth straight month of expansion, with production at its strongest since November 2021. **Why it matters:** the Fed is being asked to raise rates into an economy that does not look weak. Strong data removes the Fed's excuse not to act. The same report showed its prices-paid index easing to 75.2 from 77.9 — but still showing rising input costs for the 25th consecutive month, which is precisely the inflation stickiness the Fed is worried about.

- **Memory-chip prices have become the new pressure point of the entire AI trade.** Apple's chief executive, John Ternus, called current memory pricing "unprecedented" and said costs will keep rising past December. **Why it matters:** this is a cost that travels. AI data centers are consuming an enormous share of the world's memory-chip supply, which bids up the price for everyone else — phone makers, laptop makers, carmakers. Intel said much the same thing last week when it warned that expensive memory is making computers too pricey for consumers. So the AI build-out is no longer just a story about who profits from AI; it is now a story about which ordinary companies get squeezed by it.

- **The war with Iran is pulling oil in two directions at once.** Crude fell for a second straight session on hopes of a diplomatic settlement, after yesterday's 7%-plus collapse on Qatari-led talks. But prices jumped intraday on a report of U.S. strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island before giving it back. **Why it matters:** oil is the fastest-moving input to inflation, and inflation is what decides whether the Fed hikes in December. Every headline out of those talks is, indirectly, a headline about your mortgage rate.

## Today's lesson: gross margin, or why Apple beat and still dragged the Dow down

Today's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning we study the business itself, its sales and costs and profits, rather than the behavior of its stock chart. And Apple handed us the cleanest example available of a number most people never look at, even though professionals look at it first.

Start with the puzzle. Apple's quarter was good. Revenue rose 12% to $114.5 billion, iPhone brought in $57.6 billion, Mac sales grew 24%, and the results beat what Wall Street expected. The stock fell more than 2% and was the single biggest drag on the Dow Jones Industrial Average. The thing that did the damage was one forecast: Apple said it expects **gross margin** of 45% to 46% for the holiday quarter, down from the 47.9% it just reported.

So what is gross margin? Take every dollar of sales, subtract only the direct cost of making and delivering the product — the parts, the assembly, the shipping — and gross margin is the percentage left over. It is the money available to pay for everything else: research, advertising, salaries, taxes, and finally profit. At 47.9%, Apple keeps about 48 cents of every dollar. At 45.5%, it keeps about 45.5 cents. That sounds like a rounding error. It is not. On roughly $140 billion of holiday-quarter revenue, two and a half cents on the dollar is billions of dollars that simply vanish from the profit line.

Here is why professionals treat margin as more important than the revenue beat. Revenue growth tells you whether people want what you sell. Gross margin tells you whether you have the power to sell it profitably — and it is far harder to fix. Apple can design a better iPhone, but it cannot decide what memory chips cost, and memory is what broke this forecast. Apple's chief executive, John Ternus, called memory pricing "unprecedented" and said it keeps rising past December. The reason is the AI build-out: data centers are hoovering up the world's memory supply, so Apple is now bidding against Nvidia's customers for the same components. A company can grow revenue 12% and still make less money than last year, if margin falls far enough. That is the possibility the market priced today.

One more detail, because it teaches you to read these numbers skeptically. About one percentage point of that reported 47.9% margin came from **tariff refunds** — a one-time recovery of import duties, not a sign the business got better at making money. Strip it out and the underlying margin was closer to 47%. A good analyst asks of every flattering number: is this repeatable, or did it happen once?

If a basketball analogy helps: revenue is shots taken, gross margin is field-goal percentage, and points are profit. Apple just told everyone it is going to take more shots than ever this quarter — and that its shooting percentage is about to drop. More shots at a worse percentage can easily mean fewer points. That is what a margin warning is, and it is why a company can beat on every headline number and still lose ground.
