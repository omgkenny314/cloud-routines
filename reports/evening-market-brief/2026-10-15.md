# Market Close Brief — Thursday, October 15, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a comeback. Overnight, the selling in artificial-intelligence stocks that started here yesterday went global and got much worse: South Korea's Kospi index fell 7.57%, tripped a circuit breaker that halted trading for twenty minutes, and closed in a bear market — down about 21% from its September high. Samsung Electronics fell 9.5%, SK Hynix fell 11.1%, and Japan's Nikkei dropped about 5%. American stock futures followed them down, and then at 7:30 this morning Central time the September wholesale inflation report landed and was dramatically hotter than anyone expected — the biggest monthly jump in producer prices since March 2022. Every ingredient for an ugly day was in place. And then the day went the other way. The Nasdaq Composite fell hard in the morning, climbed roughly 1.5% off its session low, and finished a hair higher. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed up 1.08%. Smaller companies rose. Here is the single biggest storyline, and it is a genuinely counterintuitive one: inflation came in hot, and the market's odds of a Federal Reserve rate increase in two weeks *went down* — from roughly 44% yesterday to somewhere around 27% to 34% today. Bond yields fell by more than a tenth of a percentage point. Why? Because nearly half the inflation came from gasoline, which the Fed tends to look past, and because the same morning's retail sales report showed that once you strip out gas stations, Americans barely increased their spending at all. That combination — prices up, real spending flat — made the Fed look *less* likely to tighten, not more. Money then rotated out of expensive AI names and into banks, insurers, consumer staples and software. Today's lesson is built on the shape of the day itself, so hold onto the fact that the Nasdaq spent most of the session red and finished green.

## Headlines that matter

**September wholesale inflation came in far hotter than expected — and the market shrugged it off.** The Producer Price Index, or PPI, measures prices at the wholesale level: what producers receive, rather than what you pay at the register. It rose 1.3% in September from August, the largest monthly increase since March 2022, and 6.3% from a year earlier, the biggest annual gain since December 2022. Forecasts had been for roughly 0.5% to 0.6% monthly. Even the **core** reading — that is PPI with food and energy stripped out, because those swing wildly for reasons unrelated to the underlying trend — rose 0.7% on the month against about 0.3% expected, and 5.0% from a year ago. On its face this is alarming. Here is the detail that defused it: the Bureau of Labor Statistics said about half the monthly increase traced to gasoline prices, which jumped 20.3%, driven largely by Middle East conflict curtailing oil supply. Why that matters: a central bank cannot fix a war-driven oil price by raising interest rates, so the Fed tends to look through energy spikes rather than react to them.

**September retail sales looked strong and were not.** Headline retail sales rose 0.8% from August, well above the 0.3% to 0.4% expected, after a revised 1.3% gain in August. But sales at gas stations rose 6.4%, and excluding gas stations the increase was only 0.2%. As an economist at Oxford Economics put it, the large jump in the headline figure was almost entirely due to rising gasoline prices. Why this matters enormously: a dollar spent filling a tank is not a dollar of new demand, it is the same tank costing more. Elsewhere the picture was mixed — department stores up 1.6%, furniture up 1.3%, restaurants up 1.1%, electronics up 0.6%. Read the two reports together and you get prices rising while real spending flattens, which is the uncomfortable combination economists call stagflation. One chief economist used exactly that word today.

**Rate-hike odds fell on a hot inflation day, which is the key mechanical fact of the session.** Odds of a quarter-point increase at the Fed's October 27-28 meeting sat around 27% to 34% today depending on the source — the CME Group's FedWatch tool at 27.1%, another reading at 34%, LSEG data at about 29%. Yesterday after the consumer inflation report those odds were near 44%. So they roughly halved. Context: the Fed raised its target range to 3.75%-4.00% in September, so this is a tightening cycle, and attention has shifted toward December rather than October. New York Fed President John Williams said earlier this week that a further hike may still be appropriate in 2026 but that he sees no reason for more in 2027, and that near-term pressures were tied to energy. Richmond's Tom Barkin and Boston's Susan Collins also pointed to energy costs keeping inflation above target. Chicago's Austan Goolsbee has taken a more hawkish line, saying he is increasingly concerned this is old-fashioned demand overheating. That disagreement is real and is why these odds swing so much day to day.

**The AI selloff went global overnight, and that is the most important market event of the past 24 hours.** South Korea's Kospi fell 7.57% to 5,554.22, dropping as much as 9.29% intraday and triggering a 20-minute trading halt. It is now about 21% below its September high, which puts it in a **bear market** — the conventional label for a decline of 20% or more. Samsung Electronics fell 9.5%; SK Hynix fell 11.1%. Why this matters for you: it shows yesterday's American story was not an American story.

## Today's lesson: the reversal day, and the four prices every bar is made of

Today's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning the study of price and trading behavior itself, rather than the business or economy underneath it. And today handed us a textbook example of a pattern with a name.

Start with something most people never learn. When you hear "the Nasdaq was up 0.08% today," you are hearing one number. But a single day of trading actually produces **four** prices, and traders call them the OHLC: the **open**, the first price of the day; the **high**, the best price reached; the **low**, the worst price reached; and the **close**, the final price. On a chart, one day is drawn as a single bar or candle showing all four at once — the body spanning open to close, with thin lines sticking out to the high and the low. The percent change you see quoted in the news is calculated from only one of those four, the close, measured against yesterday's close. Three quarters of the information is discarded.

Here is why that matters today. The Nasdaq Composite closed up 0.08% — a number so small that a headline could fairly call the day "flat." But the index climbed roughly 1.54% from its session low to get to that close, and about 1.26% from where it opened. The low came in the morning, after Asian markets fell apart overnight and the hot wholesale inflation report hit at 7:30 Central. So the real shape of the day was: open weak, fall further, then grind higher for hours and finish positive. That shape has a name — a **reversal day**, specifically a bullish reversal, meaning a session that makes a new low and then closes near the top of its own range. CoreWeave showed you the same pattern in miniature and far more violently: down as much as 9.5% in the morning, up 3.5% by early afternoon.

Think of it like a box score. The final line says your team won by three. That tells you nothing about being down twelve at halftime, the run in the third quarter, or the fact that the other team's star fouled out. A team that wins by three after trailing by twelve is a different team from one that led comfortably the whole way and won by three — and if you only ever read the final margin, those two games look identical. The open, high and low are the halftime score and the run. The close is just the final margin.

Now, what do traders actually infer from a reversal day? The reasoning is this: on any given day, buyers and sellers are both pushing. A low that gets made and then abandoned marks a price at which enough buyers decided the thing was worth owning that they absorbed everything the sellers had to offer, and then pushed back. The close is the only one of the four prices that represents a settled verdict, because it is where traders were willing to hold a position overnight, with news able to break while they sleep. Finishing near the high of a range after making a new low is therefore read as a day where the sellers tried and failed. Two important cautions, though. First, this is a probabilistic read, not a prediction — plenty of reversal days reverse right back the following morning, which is exactly why I said to watch tomorrow. Second, technicians generally want to see **volume**, the number of shares traded, confirm the pattern; a reversal on heavy volume is considered more meaningful than one on light volume, and I was not able to verify today's volume figures. So take the shape as information, not as a conclusion. The practical habit worth building is simply this: when you hear a daily percent change, ask what the path was. Today the path was the story and the percentage hid it.
