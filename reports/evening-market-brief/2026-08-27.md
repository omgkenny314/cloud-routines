# Market Close Brief — Thursday, August 27, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Yesterday the market held its breath; today it exhaled. Nvidia's earnings report landed after Wednesday's close and the stock initially fell — but overnight, investors read the full report and changed their minds. Chief executive Jensen Huang forecast that revenue would grow about 70% in the company's fiscal 2028, when Wall Street analysts had penciled in 44%. That single number reset the mood on the entire artificial-intelligence trade, and Nvidia closed up 8.7%, adding more than $400 billion in market value in one session. Two other technology companies reported strong results the same night — Salesforce and CrowdStrike — and both jumped roughly 10%. The result was the best day since August 4 for the S&P 500, the Nasdaq Composite, and the technology sector. But here is the wrinkle worth noticing, and the reason today was less broadly cheerful than the headline numbers suggest: for much of the session, ten of the eleven sectors in the S&P 500 were actually *lower*. The entire market advance was being carried by technology. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, which owns relatively little of that group, managed only a 0.2% gain. This was a rally in one neighborhood, not across the whole city — and everyone is still waiting on Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who speaks at Jackson Hole tomorrow morning.

## Headlines that matter

**Jackson Hole opened today.** The Kansas City Fed's annual economic symposium began this morning in Moran, Wyoming, and runs through Saturday. This year's official topic is "Financial Innovation: Implications for Payments and Policy." Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his keynote tomorrow, Friday, at 10:00 a.m. Eastern time. *Why it matters:* this is Warsh's first Jackson Hole speech as chair, and it falls between scheduled Fed policy meetings — one of the few moments when a chair can speak more candidly than a formal policy statement allows. The question every trading desk wants answered is whether the interest-rate hiking cycle is *paused* or *finished*. With core inflation at 3.3% against a 2% target, as Wednesday's report showed, that is a live question, and the answer moves everything from mortgage rates to technology-stock valuations.

**Weekly jobless claims fell to 203,000.** Initial claims for unemployment benefits dropped by 4,000 for the week ending August 22, against economists' expectations of 208,000. The four-week average sits just above 205,000. *Why it matters:* claims measure people newly filing for unemployment, so they are the fastest read on whether layoffs are starting. At roughly 200,000, they are historically very low. Economists have been describing this labor market as "low fire, low hire" — employers aren't cutting, but they aren't adding much either. That combination is awkward for the Fed: a strong job market gives it no reason to cut rates, but a *stalling* one would.

**A major payroll revision lands tomorrow.** The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes its preliminary annual benchmark revision to nonfarm payrolls at 10:00 a.m. Eastern tomorrow, covering the twelve months through March 2026. *Why it matters:* the monthly jobs report is built partly on a survey, which is an estimate. Once a year, the BLS checks that estimate against actual unemployment-insurance tax records — the real thing. Last year's revision was large enough to change the story about how strong the economy had been. If tomorrow's revision shows the U.S. added meaningfully fewer jobs than previously reported, the case for the Fed cutting rates gets stronger, and it lands ninety minutes before Warsh takes the podium.

**The Strait of Hormuz is still effectively closed, and oil keeps falling anyway.** The waterway that carries roughly 20% of the world's daily oil supply and 20% of its liquefied natural gas has been largely shut to commercial shipping since late February, with a brief reopening in June that broke down in July. Traffic ran at about three vessels on August 23, against a normal 85 or so per day. Today's news was that Iran and Oman agreed on how to divide the strait's waters and its revenues — though Tehran cautioned that actually reopening the waterway takes more than a deal with Oman. *Why it matters:* this is the clearest example available of a market pricing *expectations* rather than *conditions*. The physical situation is still bad, and the International Energy Agency has warned that the world is drawing down oil stockpiles quickly. But oil fell for a fourth straight day, because traders are pricing in a path toward reopening. Prices move on the change in the outlook, not on the level of the problem.

## Today's lesson: GAAP versus adjusted earnings, or why one company reported two different profits

Our recent lessons covered operating margin, dilution, support and resistance, index construction, guidance, and the expected move. Today's is a fundamental one about **earnings quality**, and Marvell handed us a genuinely startling example.

Marvell reported its profit for the quarter twice. Under one method, it earned $308.0 million, or **$0.33 per share**. Under the other, it earned $865.9 million, or **$0.94 per share**. Same company, same three months, same set of transactions — and nearly three times the profit under the second method. Neither number is a lie. They are answering slightly different questions.

The first is **GAAP** earnings. GAAP stands for Generally Accepted Accounting Principles, the standardized rulebook that U.S. public companies must follow. It is the legally required accounting, and it counts everything, including costs that don't involve writing a check. The second is **non-GAAP**, also called **adjusted** earnings. This is the company's own version, where management strips out items it considers unrepresentative of the ongoing business. For a technology company like Marvell, the largest exclusions are usually stock-based compensation — shares given to employees instead of cash — and the amortization of intangible assets, which is the accounting cost of acquisitions made years ago being written down over time.

Here is the honest case for each side. Management argues that if you want to understand how the operating business is performing this quarter, an acquisition charge from three years ago just adds noise. That's a fair point. The counterargument is equally fair: stock-based compensation is a *real* cost. The company is handing out ownership, which means your slice of the company shrinks — that's dilution, which we covered a couple of weeks ago. Calling it "non-cash" doesn't make it free. When the gap between the two numbers is small, it rarely matters. When adjusted profit is nearly triple the official one, that gap *is* information about how the company pays its people.

Think of it like a player's stat line. GAAP earnings are the box score: every point, every turnover, every foul, recorded by league rules. Adjusted earnings are the coach saying "ignore the third quarter, he was playing through a sprained ankle." The coach may be right that it tells you more about the player's real level. But you would want to know that he leaves the ankle out of the stat line *every single game* — at which point the injury isn't an exception, it's just who he is.

The practical habit worth building: whenever you read a headline saying a company "earned $0.94 per share and beat estimates," check which number that is. Almost always it's the adjusted one, because that's what analyst consensus estimates are built on. Then look for the GAAP figure and the gap between them. A company where the two numbers track closely is telling you something different about itself than one where they diverge by a factor of three — and you saw both patterns today, since Okta reported a 13% operating margin under GAAP against 28% adjusted.
