# Market Close Brief — Monday, October 12, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a holding pattern with a split personality. Stocks were open — today was Columbus Day and Indigenous Peoples' Day, which closes banks and the bond market but not the stock exchanges — so volume was lighter than usual and nobody wanted to make a big bet the day before earnings season starts. Underneath that calm surface, money moved sideways rather than up: nine of the eleven sectors in the S&P 500 finished higher, led by energy as oil climbed, while technology got hit hard enough to drag the Nasdaq down on its own. The Dow Jones Industrial Average, which holds more old-economy industrial and energy names, rose. The Nasdaq, which is stuffed with chips and software, fell. That is what traders call rotation — the same dollars leaving one neighborhood and showing up in another, with the overall market barely moving. The single biggest storyline was the selloff in memory chips and fiber-optic networking stocks, the two groups that have run hardest on artificial intelligence enthusiasm all year, and the reason matters: a Chinese memory maker said it had made progress on next-generation chip technology, which is a direct shot at the pricing power the whole memory trade has been built on.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: closed at 7,807.24, down about 0.06%.** Essentially unchanged — a decline of roughly four and a half points. The broadest measure of large American companies, and it sat within a few points of its record high all day. Energy strength almost exactly cancelled out technology weakness.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: closed at 51,850.55, up 195.60 points, or about 0.38%.** Thirty large, mostly established companies. It had less chip exposure to drag it down and more energy and industrial exposure to lift it.

- **Nasdaq Composite: closed at 27,245.90, down about 0.44%.** The technology-heavy index. Semiconductors did the damage: the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, the standard chip-sector gauge, fell about 2.7%, with Micron Technology alone down roughly 3.9%.

- **10-year Treasury yield: no close today — the bond market was shut for the holiday.** The last traded level was Friday, October 9, at about 5.24%, with the 2-year note near 4.80%. I want to flag the context, because it is the most important number in the market right now: last Wednesday the 10-year touched roughly 5.32% to 5.36%, its highest since 2002, after the Federal Reserve signaled it is leaning toward another rate increase. Bonds reopen tomorrow morning, and Wednesday's inflation report is what they are waiting on.

- **Crude oil: higher, but I could not verify an exact settlement price.** Reports through the session put West Texas Intermediate — the U.S. benchmark grade — somewhere between about $91.90 and $93.50 a barrel, up roughly 1% to 2.75%, after an early dip near $89. Brent, the international benchmark, was above $106. I am not going to pick one of those numbers and call it the close, because my sources disagreed and none of them was an exchange settlement. The direction is not in doubt: oil rose, and the energy sector exchange-traded fund hit an all-time high. Two forces pulled against each other — Middle East supply risk from Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia and a tanker incident near Oman pushing prices up, against hopes for a Russia-Ukraine energy ceasefire pushing them down. Fresh Russian strikes on Ukrainian energy facilities undercut the ceasefire story, and the upward force won.

- **Bitcoin: roughly $83,000 to $83,600, up modestly, perhaps half a percent to one percent.** Bitcoin trades around the clock, so there is no official close and these are approximate. It has been clawing back from a low near $80,300 to $80,400 late last week. For scale, it remains roughly a third below its record above $126,000 set in October 2025.

## Big movers and why

**The memory chip selloff was the day's main event.** Micron Technology (ticker MU), the big American maker of memory chips, fell about 3.9%. SanDisk (ticker SNDK), which makes flash storage, fell roughly 7%. The trigger was reporting that ChangXin Memory Technologies, a Chinese memory manufacturer, disclosed advances in next-generation DRAM — that is dynamic random access memory, the working memory inside computers and AI servers. More capable Chinese supply means more chips competing for the same buyers, which eventually means lower prices. Two other things amplified the move: these stocks had enormous gains to protect, with SanDisk up roughly 700% from its lows at one point this year, and traders have started openly debating whether memory prices have already peaked for this cycle. Today's lesson is built on this, so hold onto it.

**Fiber-optic networking stocks fell alongside them.** Lumentum (ticker LITE), which makes the laser and optical components that move data between AI servers, dropped roughly 8% to 10% depending on when you measured. Ciena (ticker CIEN), a networking equipment maker, fell roughly 8% to 10%. Applied Optoelectronics (ticker AAOI) fell about 6%. There was no bad news from the companies themselves. One account tied Lumentum's slide to a "sell the news" reaction after a product showcase at the Open Compute Project Global Summit — meaning investors had already bought in anticipation of the announcement and sold once it arrived — plus a recent pattern of insiders selling their own shares. Mostly this looks like profit-taking in a group that has been one of the year's biggest winners.

**Meta Platforms (ticker META) rose roughly 3% to 4%**, the standout gainer among the giants. The company released Muse Wonder, which it calls its first frontier-class artificial intelligence model, in two versions. Meta says the models match the benchmark scores of rival frontier models and adds two features it calls Active Memory, which lets the model remember across conversations, and Active Focus, which lets it keep working on a hard problem and ask for clarification. Worth noting as a matter of discipline: those benchmark claims are self-reported by Meta and have not been independently verified. What moved the stock is the perception that Meta has caught up after a long, expensive, 14-month rebuild of its AI organization.

**eBay (ticker EBAY) rose roughly 3% to 5%** after Morgan Stanley named it a top pick and raised its price target to $132. Nothing changed about eBay's business today; an influential analyst changed their published opinion, and buyers responded.

**IonQ (ticker IONQ) fell about 8% to 9%** after announcing it is acquiring Elisity, a cybersecurity company. When a stock falls on its own acquisition news, investors are usually saying one of two things: you paid too much, or this is not your business. Other quantum computing names fell in sympathy — Rigetti Computing (ticker RGTI) and D-Wave Quantum (ticker QBTS) each down about 5% — which tells you the selling was partly about the whole speculative group, not just IonQ.

**Two upgrades worth noting:** Salesforce (ticker CRM) rose nearly 2% after a Bank of America upgrade, and SLM Corporation (ticker SLM), the student lender better known as Sallie Mae, gained about 4% after a Morgan Stanley upgrade.

## Headlines that matter

**Earnings season starts tomorrow morning, and the bar is extremely high.** Analysts expect S&P 500 companies to report third-quarter profit growth somewhere between 25% and 30% compared with a year ago — my sources gave 25% and 29.6%, so treat it as a range rather than a precise figure. Either way it is a very large number. Why this matters: when expectations are that high, simply meeting them is not enough to move a stock up, and missing them is punished hard. Much of today's caution was investors declining to take new positions into that uncertainty.

**The Federal Reserve is leaning toward tightening, not easing, and that is the backdrop for everything.** At the September meeting, Chair Kevin Warsh led the Fed to raise interest rates for the first time in over three years, framing it as restoring price stability faster. Officials also penciled in another increase before year-end and removed an expected cut from next year's outlook. Why this matters: higher interest rates make safe bonds more attractive compared with stocks, and they raise the hurdle that a company's future profits must clear to justify today's share price. That pressure is the reason the 10-year yield touching a 24-year high last week was front-page news.

**Wednesday's inflation report is the week's real event.** The September Consumer Price Index — the CPI, the government's main measure of what households pay for goods and services — comes out Wednesday at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, 7:30 Central. One forecast looks for headline prices up 0.6% from the prior month versus 0.4% before, and 3.6% versus a year ago versus 3.4% previously. Why this matters: if inflation comes in hot, it strengthens the case for that additional rate hike, and both bonds and stocks will react. The Fed's Beige Book, a survey of business conditions across the Fed's twelve districts, follows at 2:00 p.m. Eastern the same day.

**Oil's rise is a two-sided story for the market.** Energy stocks loved it — the sector hit a record. But rising crude feeds directly into gasoline, shipping and manufacturing costs, which feeds into inflation, which feeds back into the Fed's thinking. That is exactly the circle the market is nervous about this week.

**The holiday itself shaped the tape.** With the bond market and banks closed, there was no Treasury trading to give stocks a signal, and volume was lighter than a normal Monday. Thin volume means individual moves can look more dramatic than the conviction behind them. That is worth remembering before reading too much into any single percentage today.

**One caution worth carrying.** A commentary out today pointed out that while the S&P 500 sits near an all-time high, more than half the stocks in the Russell 3000 — a much broader index of roughly three thousand American companies — are down at least 20% from their recent highs. An index can look healthy while most of its members do not. I have not independently verified that statistic, but the general pattern of a narrow market led by a handful of giants is well documented this year.

## Today's lesson: commodity businesses and the moat they do not have

Today's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning the study of the business itself, what it sells and what it earns, rather than the behavior of its stock price. And the memory chip selloff hands us one of the cleanest examples you will ever get.

Start with the thing that happened. A Chinese company, ChangXin Memory Technologies, said it had made progress on next-generation DRAM. Micron did not report bad earnings today. SanDisk did not lose a customer today. Neither company's revenue changed by a dollar. And yet Micron fell about 4% and SanDisk fell about 7%. Why would a competitor's engineering announcement, on the other side of the world, cost American shareholders billions of dollars in a single afternoon?

Because memory chips are a **commodity**. A commodity is a product where one maker's version is close enough to another's that buyers choose almost entirely on price. A gigabyte of DRAM from Micron does the same job as a gigabyte of DRAM from Samsung or ChangXin. Contrast that with a **moat** — Warren Buffett's term for a durable advantage that protects a business from competition, like a castle moat. A patent nobody can work around is a moat. A network of users that makes a product more valuable the more people use it is a moat. A brand people pay extra for is a moat. Memory makers have scale and manufacturing know-how, which are real, but they do not have the kind of moat that lets them name their price.

Here is why that single fact explains the whole move. In a commodity business, profits are not driven by how good your product is. They are driven by the balance between how much of the stuff exists and how much people want. When demand runs ahead of supply — which is exactly what AI data centers have done to memory for the past two years — prices spike, and because the cost of running a chip factory barely changes, almost every extra dollar of price falls straight to the bottom line. That is why SanDisk was up something like 700% from its lows at one point this year. It is also why the fall is violent. Add new supply, and the same arithmetic runs in reverse just as fast. Investors today were not reacting to ChangXin's chips, which do not exist in volume yet. They were reacting to the possibility of future supply, because in a commodity business the mere expectation of more supply is enough to reprice the stock.

The basketball version: a team built entirely on hot three-point shooting can beat anybody on a good night, and the scoreboard looks spectacular. But shooting percentage is not a moat — it is a condition, and conditions change. A team built on defense and rebounding wins on bad shooting nights too, because those edges show up every single game. When you look at a company, that is the question worth asking: is this business winning because of something durable it owns, or because conditions in its market happen to be favorable right now? Both make money. Only one of them makes money reliably, and only one of them is immune to a press release from a competitor.

One honest caveat, because it would be dishonest to leave it out. This is not a reason to think memory stocks are bad businesses or good ones — that is not the point, and it is not my call to make. Cyclical commodity companies can be tremendously profitable at the right point in the cycle. The point is that knowing *why* a company's profits are high tells you how much confidence to place in them continuing. Price-driven profits in a commodity market are the least durable kind there is. Keep that distinction in your pocket this week, because when the banks report tomorrow you will be looking at the opposite situation: a business whose profits come largely from the gap between the interest they collect and the interest they pay — which is its own kind of cycle, driven by the very interest rates the Fed has been raising.
