# Market Close Brief — Thursday, October 22, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a bad day for technology and a perfectly fine day for everything else, which is a more interesting combination than a plain selloff. The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.6% — its worst session since June — the S&P 500 fell 1.21%, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average finished essentially unchanged, up by less than 12 points. When those three indexes split that far apart, the story is never "stocks went down." The story is that one specific group of stocks went down hard while money moved somewhere else.

The single biggest storyline was artificial-intelligence spending, and specifically a question investors have started asking out loud: who is paying for all of this, and what do they get back? Meta Platforms (ticker META), the parent of Facebook and Instagram, reported strong results after yesterday's close — revenue beat expectations and its guidance was upbeat — and the stock fell about 7% anyway. What spooked people was not the earnings. It was the announcement of a new class of non-voting shares, which the market read as a plan to raise an enormous amount of money to fund AI data centers. Tesla (ticker TSLA) also beat expectations and also fell, roughly 3%, for the opposite reason: it *cut* its planned spending for the year. One company signaled it will spend more, the other signaled it will spend less, and the market sold both. That contradiction is the most useful thing that happened today, and tonight's lesson is built on it.

Two other forces pushed in the same direction. Chinese stocks dropped into a bear market, with Bloomberg reporting $2.4 trillion in value lost since a May peak. And oil kept climbing on attacks in Saudi Arabia. Meanwhile money ran into U.S. government bonds hard enough to push the 10-year Treasury yield below 5% for the first time in a month. That last detail tells you this was a flight to safety, not a panic.

## Big movers and why

- **Meta Platforms (ticker META) fell about 7% and was the biggest drag on the market.** Reuters put the decline at 6.7%. The results themselves were good: third-quarter revenue beat expectations, guidance was upbeat. The problem was a plan to issue a new class of non-voting stock. Non-voting shares let a company sell ownership and raise cash without giving the buyers any say in how the company is run — useful if you are Mark Zuckerberg and want to keep control, and a signal to investors that a very large amount of money is about to be raised to pay for AI infrastructure. There was a second hit on the same day: a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay a $25 billion penalty in a data-privacy case tied to the Cambridge Analytica scandal. More on that below.

- **Tesla (ticker TSLA) fell about 3%, despite beating expectations.** Adjusted earnings came in at $0.48 per share against an estimate of $0.44. The stock fell on guidance: Tesla cut planned capital spending for 2026 from more than $25 billion to roughly $20 billion. Investors read a $5 billion cut as Tesla slowing down its investment in AI, robotics and self-driving — the parts of the story that justify its valuation. One data provider showed the decline nearer 1%, and Tesla's trading was volatile through the day, so take 3% as approximate.

- **Chip stocks fell as a group, and harder than the broad market.** Advanced Micro Devices (ticker AMD) fell more than 8%, Micron Technology (ticker MU) about 6%, Intel (ticker INTC) about 5.3%, and Nvidia (ticker NVDA) about 4%. None of these companies reported today. They fell because they *sell* to the companies doing the AI spending, so any doubt about that spending lands on them immediately. This is the read-across effect: one company's guidance gets applied to its suppliers whether or not those suppliers have said anything.

- **Lockheed Martin (ticker LMT) rose about 9.4% and was one of the day's clearest winners.** The defense contractor reported third-quarter results before the open, raised its full-year sales and profit forecasts, posted a record backlog, and announced an F-35 contract. A *backlog* is work already ordered but not yet delivered and billed — for a defense company it is the most informative single number, because it is revenue that is largely already committed. Rising oil and Middle East tensions did not hurt a defense stock either.

- **SpaceX (ticker SPCX) fell sharply, though my sources conflict badly on how much.** Reuters reported 3.9%; a Yahoo Finance midday snapshot showed 9.14%. The stock had surged earlier this week on its first profitable quarter, and today it gave back some of that. I cannot tell you the exact closing decline with confidence. Other space-related names fell with it, including AST SpaceMobile and Rocket Lab.

- **Blackstone (ticker BX) rose roughly 8%** after reporting results before the open. Blackstone is an asset manager — it invests other people's money for fees — which puts it in the financial sector that money rotated *into* today.

- **International Business Machines (ticker IBM) fell about 1.4% even though it beat expectations and raised its annual software revenue forecast.** A useful small example: on a day when the market has decided it is worried about one theme, good news from a company adjacent to that theme does not necessarily help.

## Headlines that matter

**The AI spending question changed shape today, and that is why the market reacted so strongly.** For most of this year the worry about AI was about valuation — are these stocks too expensive. Today the worry became about *financing*: Meta proposing a new share class to fund its buildout, and Tesla pulling back on its own. Why this matters more than a single bad day: when a company funds growth out of its own profits, that growth costs existing shareholders nothing. When it funds growth by issuing new shares or borrowing, existing shareholders pay for it — either by owning a smaller slice of the company or by sitting behind new lenders. The market spent today repricing that distinction across the entire sector.

**A New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay a $25 billion penalty for misleading users about privacy.** The ruling came from Judge Francis Mathew of Santa Fe State District Court, following a September finding after a two-week trial; 26 of 29 statements by Meta and its leadership were found misleading, amounting to more than 43 million violations of state consumer-protection law. The judge calculated that state law supported a penalty above $219 billion and reduced it to $25 billion. Meta says it is confident it will win on appeal. Why it matters: for comparison, the Federal Trade Commission fined Meta $5 billion over the same scandal in 2019. A single state court producing a number five times larger tells you that regulatory risk for large technology companies is not settled, and it arrived on the same day Meta was asking investors to fund an enormous spending program.

**Weekly jobless claims fell to 194,000, near 57-year lows.** That is down 6,000 for the week ended October 17, and below the roughly 200,000 economists expected. *Initial jobless claims* count people filing for unemployment benefits for the first time, so it is the fastest read we get on layoffs. But look at the second number: continuing claims — people still collecting benefits — rose to a six-week high of 1.728 million. Economists describe this combination as "low-hire, low-fire": companies are not letting people go, but they are not hiring either, so anyone who does lose a job stays unemployed longer. Why it matters: a labor market this firm gives the Federal Reserve no reason to cut interest rates.

**The Federal Reserve meets next Tuesday and Wednesday, October 27 and 28, and officials are in their blackout period.** This is worth getting straight because it may not match your assumptions. The Fed *raised* its benchmark rate by a quarter point last month, to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%. Markets put the odds of no change at next week's meeting around 91.5%. The live debate is not whether the Fed cuts — it is whether the Fed holds through year-end or hikes again in December, and my sources disagree sharply on that. The *blackout period*, running from October 17 to October 29, is a self-imposed rule that bars Fed officials from public comment on policy before a meeting. Practically, it means no Fed speeches will move the market between now and Wednesday.

**Oil kept climbing on attacks in Saudi Arabia.** A drone strike on Saudi Aramco's Shaybah oil field was intercepted, but falling debris killed two people; officials said field operations were unaffected. The attacks are tied to Houthi activity against the backdrop of a wider U.S.-Iran confrontation, and the United States has ordered some diplomatic staff and family members out of the Kingdom. Analysts at MUFG noted that Saudi output and exports have not actually been reduced — what is moving the price is a risk premium, meaning buyers paying extra today for the possibility of a disruption tomorrow. Why it matters to a stock investor: higher energy prices feed into inflation, and inflation is the one thing that could push the Fed toward raising rates again.

**Chinese stocks fell into a bear market.** The CSI 300 index closed down 1.8%, putting it 20% below its June high, which is the conventional definition of a bear market. Bloomberg reported $2.4 trillion in market value lost since a May peak, attributing it to a prolonged consumer slump and doubts about AI profitability. The Shanghai Composite closed at 3,658, down 1.5%. One strategist quoted by CNBC called it "a crisis of confidence right now, not a crisis in the economy." Why it matters: it shows the AI profitability doubt is not an American mood, it is global.

**One more data point worth filing away:** U.S. distillate stockpiles rose by 1.68 million barrels to 104.3 million, when a decline was expected. Distillates are diesel and heating oil. A build in inventories when you expected a draw is mildly bearish for those fuel prices, and it cut against the geopolitical story pushing crude higher.

At 8:45 a.m. Central (9:45 Eastern), S&P Global publishes its flash purchasing managers' indexes for October. A *purchasing managers' index*, or PMI, is a survey of the executives who actually buy supplies for their companies; readings above 50 mean the sector is expanding and below 50 mean it is contracting. ## Today's lesson: capital expenditure, and why the market punished one company for spending more and another for spending less

Today handed us something close to a laboratory experiment, so let me teach you the concept it demonstrates. This is a *fundamental analysis* lesson — meaning we are looking at the business itself rather than the price chart.

The concept is **capital expenditure**, almost always shortened to **capex**. Capex is money a company spends on long-lived physical things: factories, machinery, buildings, data centers, the chips that go in them. The distinction that matters is between capex and ordinary operating expenses like salaries or electricity. An operating expense is consumed this year and subtracted from this year's profit. Capex buys an asset that is supposed to produce income for years, so accounting rules do not subtract it from profit all at once — the company spreads the cost out over the asset's useful life, a process called *depreciation*. That accounting difference is why a company can report a healthy profit and still be bleeding cash, and it is why capex is one of the first numbers experienced investors look for.

The number that cuts through the accounting is **free cash flow**: roughly, the cash a business generates from operations minus the capex it spends. It answers a plain question — after paying for everything needed to keep the business running and growing, how much cash is actually left over? That leftover cash is what funds dividends, buybacks, debt repayment, and acquisitions. When capex climbs faster than operating cash, free cash flow shrinks, and a company that wants to keep spending has to get the money somewhere else: borrow it, or sell new shares.

Now watch today. Meta beat on revenue, guided higher, and fell 7% — because it announced a new class of non-voting shares, which the market read as "we are about to raise a very large amount of money to pay for AI data centers." Tesla also beat, and fell 3% — because it cut 2026 capex from more than $25 billion to about $20 billion, which the market read as "we are pulling back on the AI and robotics projects that justify our valuation." Spend more, get punished. Spend less, get punished. Those two reactions look contradictory until you see what the market was actually pricing in both cases, which was not the amount of spending at all. It was the **return** on the spending. Meta's announcement raised the question of whether its AI investments will ever earn more than they cost, and who absorbs the loss if they do not. Tesla's cut was taken as the company's own admission that the opportunity is smaller than advertised. The market is not asking "how much?" It is asking "is this worth it?" — and today it was not persuaded by either answer.

A basketball comparison makes the asymmetry clear. Capex is the front office signing a max contract. Fans do not boo a big contract and cheer a small one; they judge whether the player is worth the money and what it does to the cap sheet. A max deal for a proven All-Star is applauded. The identical dollar figure for an unproven player is a disaster, because the money is committed for years and it crowds out every other move you might want to make. And a front office that suddenly stops spending is not automatically praised for discipline either — sometimes it reads as the team quietly admitting it is not trying to win now. Same dollars, opposite verdicts, and the verdict is always about expected return rather than the size of the check. When you see a company announce a huge capital program, the question to carry is not whether the number is big. It is what the company expects to earn on it, and whether the money is coming out of its own pocket or yours.
