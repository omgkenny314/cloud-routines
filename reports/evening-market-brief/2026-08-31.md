# Market Close Brief — Monday, August 31, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

August ended the way a lot of this year has gone: a headline out of the Middle East, oil up, bond yields up, and stocks down just enough to notice. Over the weekend U.S. forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island, in the Strait of Hormuz — the first publicly acknowledged American attack on Iranian positions in about a month. U.S. Central Command said the Revolutionary Guard was preparing to fire rockets carrying sea mines into the strait, the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes. Iran answered with a missile and drone attack on two U.S. bases in Jordan. Oil jumped more than 2.5%, and that is where the story turned from geopolitics into a market story. Higher oil means higher inflation, and higher inflation means a Federal Reserve that is already talking about *raising* interest rates has one more reason to do it. The 10-year Treasury yield pushed above 4.75%, its highest since January 2025, and traders nudged the odds of a September rate hike up to roughly 60%. Stocks took it in stride at the index level — the S&P 500 lost a third of a percent, the Nasdaq barely moved — but underneath, the day was violent in places. The single biggest storyline for individual investors had nothing to do with Iran: California's legislature let its session end without the wildfire liability protections that utility investors had been counting on, and two of the state's electric utilities lost roughly a fifth of their value in a single session. Despite today's decline, all three major averages finished August higher.

## Big movers and why

- **Edison International (EIX) fell about 23%,** reportedly its largest single-day decline in more than 25 years, trading around $54 by midday. California's legislative session ended today without passing the wildfire liability protections utility investors had been expecting. The bill that advanced, SB 492, was reported to omit both a $6 billion per-incident cap on liability and a mechanism to refill the state's Wildfire Fund. Governor Gavin Newsom had pushed to shield utilities from being sued by insurers over wildfire damage; lawmakers declined.
- **PG&E Corporation (PCG) fell about 18%,** trading near $13.57 at midday — its worst percentage loss in over six years — for the same reason. Analysts moved fast: BMO cut PG&E to Market Perform, and Mizuho cut both PG&E and Edison to Neutral, lowering price targets on each.
- **Tesla (TSLA) rose roughly 5%, closing around $367.95.** Two pieces of company-specific news: confirmation that its Optimus humanoid robot has entered production at the Fremont, California plant, using existing Model S and Model X assembly lines, and a weekend post from CEO Elon Musk about Tesla and SpaceX each building large-scale solar production capacity. I saw the day's gain reported at figures ranging from about 5.0% to 5.5% depending on the source, so call it "about 5%." Worth noting: Tesla rose on a down day for the broad market and a down day for other electric-vehicle stocks. That is what a company-specific move looks like.
- **Energy was the day's best sector.** Chevron (CVX), Exxon Mobil (XOM), Halliburton (HAL), Valero Energy (VLO), and Occidental Petroleum (OXY) all rose on the oil move. The specific figures I could confirm — Halliburton up more than 2.5%, Chevron up 2%, Valero and Occidental up 2%, Exxon up more than 1.5% — were premarket quotes, so I'd treat those as directional rather than as closing numbers.
- **Alphabet (GOOGL/GOOG), the parent of Google, fell about 2.4% to roughly $334.65.** This is a continuation of a rough stretch rather than a single event. The concerns are a leadership shakeup in its artificial-intelligence division and departures of senior researchers, plus capital spending projected at $180–$190 billion for 2026 with the payoff still uncertain. - **Alibaba Group (BABA) fell about 3.4%,** giving back part of the move that followed its earnings report on Friday.
- **Utilities were the worst sector overall,** and not only because of California. When long-term interest rates rise, utility stocks tend to fall — investors often own them for their dividends, and when a government bond starts paying more, a dividend stock has to compete with it.

## Headlines that matter

**The Larak Island strike and the Strait of Hormuz.** On Sunday, U.S. Central Command said American forces conducted what it called "limited, precise action" against two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island, saying Revolutionary Guard forces were preparing to launch rockets carrying sea mines into the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's Revolutionary Guard responded with a missile and drone operation it named "Punishment of the Aggressor," striking the King Hussein and Al Azraq bases in Jordan; Jordan reported intercepting eight missiles. On Monday the Guard also claimed to have shot down a U.S. MQ-9 drone over the strait. **Why it matters:** mines in the Strait of Hormuz are not a symbolic threat. It is the chokepoint for a large share of the world's oil exports, and it cannot be routed around. Markets do not price the strike itself — they price the probability that oil stops flowing. That is why a limited action against two launchers moved crude more than 2.5%.

**Oil is now a Fed story, not just an energy story.** This is the connection worth internalizing. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said at Jackson Hole on Friday that recent good inflation readings had not convinced him the underlying trend had improved. Then oil jumped. Energy costs flow into nearly every price in the economy — shipping, plastics, food, airfares — so an oil spike makes the inflation problem Warsh described harder, not easier. Fed funds futures now put the odds of a quarter-point hike at the September 15–16 meeting at about 60.4%, up from roughly 56% on Friday, according to the CME's FedWatch tool. Before Warsh spoke, the market's base case was that the Fed would hold.

**California's wildfire bill, and why it hit two stocks so hard.** California operates under a legal doctrine called inverse condemnation, under which a utility can be held liable for damage caused by its equipment even without a finding of negligence. That doctrine is what pushed PG&E into bankruptcy in 2019. Investors had been expecting this legislative session to cap that exposure. It didn't. **Why it matters beyond those two stocks:** it's a reminder that for some companies, the biggest risk isn't competition or a bad quarter. It's a vote in a state capitol. More on this in today's lesson.

**Today's economic data was a genuine split.** The Dallas Fed's manufacturing survey for August came in at 11.6, up sharply from 1.3 in July and the highest reading since January 2025. Production rose to 16.1 from 10.1, new orders to 22.0 from 6.4, shipments to 14.1 from 8.8, and the company outlook index climbed 5.8 points to 19.2. That is a strong report. But it sits directly against Friday's Chicago Business Barometer — often called the Chicago PMI — which collapsed to 47.1 from 57.6, more than ten points below what forecasters expected, and back into contraction. (Any reading below 50 signals contraction; above 50, expansion.) One regional survey says Texas factories are accelerating; another says Midwest business activity fell off a cliff. Both are real data. Neither is the whole picture, and that tension is exactly why the Fed's September decision is a genuine coin flip.

**One clarification on the calendar:** Friday's brief listed the Chicago PMI as a Monday release. It actually came out Friday morning, August 28, alongside the University of Michigan consumer sentiment reading of 51.7. The Dallas Fed survey was today's release.

## Today's lesson: contingent liabilities, or the debt that isn't on the balance sheet yet

A balance sheet is a snapshot of what a company owns and what it owes. Assets on one side, liabilities on the other; subtract the liabilities from the assets and what's left is shareholders' equity — in principle, what the owners actually have. Most liabilities are easy to count. A bank loan is a number. A bond is a number. You can look them up.

But some obligations don't appear as a number, because they only exist if something else happens first. These are called **contingent liabilities** — contingent meaning "it depends." A lawsuit that hasn't been decided. A product warranty nobody has claimed yet. Or, for a California electric utility, a wildfire that hasn't started yet, from a power line that hasn't fallen yet. These generally live in the footnotes of a company's financial filings rather than in the totals, which is exactly why casual investors miss them.

For PG&E and Edison International, that footnote *is* the investment case. California's inverse condemnation doctrine means a utility can be on the hook for wildfire damage even when it did nothing wrong. There is no ceiling on the number. PG&E filed for bankruptcy in 2019 because of it. So when investors expected this legislative session to cap liability at $6 billion per incident, what they were really hoping for was not that the risk would disappear — it was that the risk would become *countable*. You can build a model of a company whose worst case is $6 billion. You cannot build a model of a company whose worst case is "however much the next fire costs."

Here's the basketball version. Think of the difference between a team that's six million dollars over the salary cap — genuinely bad, but you know the number, you can plan the trade, you can budget the tax — and a team sitting under an open league investigation that might cost them three first-round picks, or might cost them nothing, or might cost them the franchise. Same roster on the floor either way. But you'd value those two teams completely differently, and the second one is nearly impossible to price at all.

Now notice what did *not* change today. Edison International sold the same electricity to the same customers this morning as it did Friday. Its revenue didn't change. Its profit didn't change. Its power plants and wires are all still there. What changed was the *range of possible outcomes*, and the market marked down nearly a quarter of the company's value on that alone. That's the takeaway worth keeping: for some companies, the most important number isn't in the earnings report at all. It's a maybe, buried in the footnotes — and sometimes it gets decided by a legislature instead of by the business.
