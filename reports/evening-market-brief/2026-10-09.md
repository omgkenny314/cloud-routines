# Market Close Brief — Friday, October 9, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

A quiet-looking rally sitting on top of a genuinely strange day underneath. All three major indexes finished higher, capping a week in which both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq set record closes early on, sold off hard in the middle, and clawed most of it back by Friday. The two things that had been scaring the market all week — long-term interest rates at levels not seen since 2002, and oil above $90 a barrel — both eased slightly today, and that was enough to let technology shares rebound. But the single biggest storyline was not about the indexes at all. It was a $8 billion spectrum deal that turned the American wireless industry upside down in one session: SpaceX agreed to buy a nationwide block of airwaves, and Verizon had its worst day since 2002, T-Mobile fell more than 13%, and AT&T fell nearly 10% — while the companies that own the cell towers those carriers rent space on jumped double digits. Alongside that, Humana soared on new Medicare quality ratings, Delta Air Lines slashed its profit outlook because jet fuel has gotten so expensive, and a consumer sentiment reading came in at its lowest current-conditions level on record. A rally on the scoreboard; a lot of churn in the box score.

A note on sourcing before we go further: my ability to load individual web pages directly was broken today, so everything below comes from search-engine summaries of multiple outlets rather than from me reading the pages myself. Index closes are well corroborated. Where sources disagreed, or where I could only find one, I say so instead of picking a number.

## Big movers and why

The telecom story first, because it was the day.

- **T-Mobile US (TMUS), down 13.27%.** The hardest hit. T-Mobile's competitive advantage has rested heavily on owning the best mid-band and low-band spectrum in the country, and the market decided today that advantage just got less exclusive.
- **Verizon Communications (VZ), down 8.75%.** Its worst single day since July 2002, per CNBC. Twenty-four years.
- **AT&T (T), down 9.81%.** Same story.
- **The cause:** SpaceX agreed to buy Grain Management's nationwide portfolio of 800 megahertz spectrum — described as up to 14 megahertz of paired spectrum. Terms were not disclosed; The Wall Street Journal reported the price at about $8 billion. It still needs Federal Communications Commission approval. **Spectrum** is simply the radio frequencies a wireless network transmits over — it is the finite, government-licensed raw material of the entire industry, and until now the three big carriers were the only ones who owned meaningful amounts of the good stuff. SpaceX buying a nationwide block is the clearest signal yet that it intends to be a fourth carrier, beaming service from satellites rather than from towers. FCC Chair Brendan Carr called more competition in spectrum "really good news for the American consumers," which is not the comment incumbent carriers were hoping to read.
- **The other side of the same trade: tower companies rose.** American Tower (AMT), Crown Castle (CCI) and SBA Communications (SBAC) gained between roughly 6.7% and 8.3% by CNBC's count, with Crown Castle quoted as high as 12% to 13.6% intraday by other outlets. Towers are landlords — carriers rent space on them. Morgan Stanley called the deal "incrementally constructive" for them, the logic being that a new national carrier eventually needs ground infrastructure too, and that more competition means more network spending. Note how the same headline was read as a threat to the tenants and a gift to the landlords.

And the rest:

- **Humana (HUM), up about 11.6% at the close** (the AP's figure; other outlets reported 11.7%, 12.5%, and 13% intraday, and Bloomberg noted it was up as much as 17% at the open). It touched a 52-week high of $450.23 during the session. The cause: the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services released 2027 Medicare Advantage **star ratings** on Thursday evening, and Humana was the big winner. The company said 95% of its Medicare Advantage members will be in plans rated four stars or better next year, up from 20% this year. JPMorgan had expected 60% to 70%. Star ratings matter in dollars, not just prestige: the government pays bonuses on highly rated plans, and one analyst estimated the improvement could be worth about $4.8 billion in bonus payments in 2028. Baird upgraded the stock to Outperform and raised its target to $596 from $390; Barclays raised its target to $582 from $515.
- **The flip side of those ratings.** JPMorgan estimates UnitedHealth Group's (UNH) share of members in four-star-or-better plans falls to roughly 67% and CVS Health's (CVS) to roughly 70%. Alignment Healthcare (ALHC) was the big loser on the downgrades and has reportedly threatened to sue the government over its rating. I found an aggregator reporting UnitedHealth down about 0.5% and CVS down about 4% in extended trading Thursday, and Alignment down roughly 23% in Thursday's after-hours; I could not verify any of their Friday closes, so treat those as unconfirmed.
- **Apple (AAPL), lower — I could not verify the closing percentage.** Reported down 1.6% premarket at $334.89 and down roughly 2.3% to 2.5% in early trading. Nikkei Asia reported that Apple asked suppliers to cut October component orders for the iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max by 15% to 20% versus its original plan, after higher memory-chip costs pushed prices up and demand came in soft. One complicating detail worth holding: Apple did not launch a baseline iPhone 18 this fall, holding it for early next year, so part of the shortfall may be a calendar quirk rather than a demand collapse. Analyst Gene Munster noted lead times down 31% on the Pro but up 1% on the Pro Max — a mixed picture, not a clean one.
- **Delta Air Lines (DAL), down about 2%** (reported down 2.1% premarket at $80.41; I could not verify the close). Revenue of $20.19 billion beat expectations badly — up 16% year over year — but adjusted earnings came in at $1.72 a share against estimates that ranged from $1.75 to $1.96 depending on whose consensus you use. CNBC noted it was Delta's first miss in two years. The real news was the guidance cut: full-year adjusted earnings now $5.10 to $5.60 a share, down from July's $6.50 to $7.50. Delta absorbed $1.6 billion of higher fuel costs in the quarter alone and expects roughly $6 billion more on its annual fuel bill. This is the oil story arriving in a corporate income statement.

## Headlines that matter

**The Federal Reserve is raising rates again, and that is the backdrop for everything.** In September the Fed raised its target range by a quarter point to 3.75%–4%, its first increase since July 2023 after five straight meetings of no change. The minutes of that meeting, released Wednesday, showed all 19 officials backed the hike and most thought another one "would likely be appropriate by year end." Why it matters: for roughly two years the market's working assumption was that rates were done going up and would eventually come down. That assumption is now dead, and every stock valuation built on it has to be redone. Traders currently put only a 20% to 25% chance on a hike at the October meeting, so the fight is over timing, not direction.

**Long-term Treasury yields hit 24-year highs this week.** The 10-year reached roughly 5.32% to 5.36% on Wednesday, the highest since 2002, and the 30-year got near 5.70%. The Fed's own minutes blamed a mix of causes: geopolitics, the Treasury's bond buyback program, and — this one is new and worth filing away — competition for capital from the enormous amount of private debt being issued to build artificial intelligence data centers. Why it matters: the yield on a government bond is the risk-free alternative to owning stocks, and it is also the rate off which corporate borrowing is priced. When it rises, stocks have to work harder to justify themselves. When it eased slightly today, they rallied. That is not a coincidence; it is the mechanism.

**Consumer sentiment fell to a five-month low.** The University of Michigan's preliminary October reading came in at 46.3, down from 48.1 in September and below the roughly 47.6 consensus. Inside it, the current-conditions component dropped to 44.7 from 50.9 — described as the lowest on record — while expectations actually ticked up to 47.3. Year-ahead inflation expectations rose to 4.7% from 4.6%, and five-year expectations to 3.5% from 3.4%. Coverage attributed the gloom to gasoline prices, borrowing costs and slowing hiring. Why it matters: consumer spending is roughly two-thirds of the American economy, so sentiment is a rough early read on demand. But it matters doubly right now because of those inflation expectations. The Fed watches them closely on the theory that if people expect high inflation, they behave in ways that produce it. Rising expectations make it harder for the Fed to stop hiking.

**Oil, war and an election.** Oil is high because of a war with Iran that began in February, and it fell today partly because President Trump said the United States would not attack Iran before the November 3 midterm elections. Why it matters: a large slice of the current oil price is not supply and demand, it is a premium for the risk of a disruption in the Strait of Hormuz. Statements that lower that perceived risk lower the price without a single extra barrel being pumped.

**Hurricane Isaias, the first hurricane of the 2026 Atlantic season, hit Gulf energy infrastructure.** Roughly 1.3 million barrels per day of Gulf oil output — about 63% of the region's total — was shut in, along with about 57% of its natural gas, with crews evacuated from more than 120 platforms. The storm track appeared to veer away from the dense Louisiana refining corridor; the exposed refineries are Chevron's Pascagoula, Mississippi plant and Vertex Energy's Saraland, Alabama plant, together about 2.4% of United States refining capacity. Chevron said Pascagoula remains operational. Why it matters: shut-in production usually comes back within days, but a damaged refinery takes one to two weeks to restart, and Gulf Coast refineries are already running at 95% utilization with diesel inventories at their lowest level for this time of year since records began in 1982. Jefferies warned of a possible "fuel supply event." Reported storm strength varied between Category 2 and Category 3 across sources, so treat the specifics as preliminary.

## Today's lesson: timeframes, or why the same week looks calm on one chart and wild on another

Today's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning the study of price and trading behavior itself, rather than the business or the economy underneath it. And this week handed us the cleanest example of this idea I have seen, in the form of a near-perfect coincidence in the Nasdaq Composite.

Here is the coincidence. The Nasdaq closed today at 27,366.17, up 0.64% on the day. Last Friday, October 2, it closed at 27,190.86. So for the whole week, Monday through Friday, the Nasdaq gained 175.31 points, which is also 0.64%. The entire week's advance happened today. Put differently: from last Friday's close to Thursday's close, the Nasdaq moved from 27,190.86 to 27,193.34 — a gain of two and a half points, or nine thousandths of one percent. Four full trading days of effectively nothing.

Except those four days were not nothing. The Nasdaq rose to a record close of 27,477.31 on Monday, set another record at 27,599.79 on Tuesday, slipped Wednesday, then fell 345 points — 1.25% — on Thursday as the AI trade got hit. It went up 1.5% and then down 1.5%. Somebody who bought Tuesday afternoon and sold Thursday afternoon lost real money. Somebody who only checked the Nasdaq last Friday and again tonight would conclude the week was placid and slightly positive. Both of those people are looking at correct data. They are looking at different **timeframes** — the span of time each price bar on a chart represents. A daily chart draws one bar per session. A weekly chart draws one bar per week. This week, the daily chart shows a roller coaster and the weekly chart shows one small, dull, green bar.

Neither chart is lying, and neither is more "real." But they answer different questions, and the mistake to avoid is answering one with the other. The weekly bar is better at the question *what is the larger direction here* — and the answer it gives is that technology stocks are drifting slightly upward near record highs, which is true and useful. The daily bars are better at *how much disagreement was there along the way* — and the answer is a great deal, which is also true and also useful, because the size of the swings tells you how fragile the consensus is. A market that ends a week unchanged after two records and a 1.25% drop is not the same market as one that ends unchanged by sitting still for five days, even though the weekly chart draws them identically.

The basketball version: two teams both win by two points. In the first game, the lead never exceeded four and the final score was 98 to 96. In the second, the home team was up 22 at halftime, blew the entire lead, and hit a buzzer-beater to win 98 to 96. The standings record both as one win, and the standings are not wrong — a win is a win and that is the number that decides the season. But if you are trying to figure out what either team will do tomorrow night, the standings have thrown away the only information you actually needed.

So the usable habit is this: before you form an opinion from a chart or a percentage, ask what span of time it covers, and then deliberately look at one longer and one shorter. Today's 0.64% and this week's 0.64% are the same number carrying two different meanings. The S&P 500 makes the same point more quietly — it gained 1.15% for the week, and it is still nine hundredths of a percent below the record it set on Tuesday — and the Dow is blunter still: 423 of its 478 points of weekly gain, about 88% of them, arrived in this single session. One number, several timeframes, several stories. Knowing which one you are being told is most of the skill.
