# Market Close Brief — Friday, October 23, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Yesterday the technology stocks fell and everything else held up. Today the market did the exact opposite, and that reversal is the whole story. The Nasdaq Composite — the index stuffed with technology companies — fell only 0.21% and essentially steadied itself after Thursday's rout. Meanwhile the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 652.82 points, or 1.23%, and the S&P 500 fell 0.52%. Two days in a row, the three major indexes have split far apart from one another, just in opposite directions. When that happens, "the market was down" tells you almost nothing. What actually happened is that the selling moved out of technology and into nearly everything else.

The single biggest storyline was oil, and behind oil, Iran. U.S. crude settled above $100 a barrel for the first time in months after reports that the United States and Israel have drawn up plans for renewed strikes on Iran following the midterm elections. West Texas Intermediate crude settled at $103.27 and Brent, the international benchmark, settled at $116.72 — its highest close since April 29. Energy stocks loved it; the energy sector exchange-traded fund hit a record, per Benzinga. Almost nothing else did. Consumer staples, utilities, healthcare and industrials all finished lower. Higher oil is a tax on everyone who is not selling oil, and today the market paid it.

There was a second, genuinely interesting storyline running alongside: Bloomberg reported that SpaceX (ticker SPCX) is in talks to lease access to roughly 80,000 U.S. cell sites to build its own nationwide wireless network. That single report sent the companies that *own* cell towers sharply higher and the companies that *are* phone carriers sharply lower. It is one of the cleanest examples you will ever see of the market repricing an entire industry's competitive landscape in a single session, on news where nothing has actually been signed yet.

## Headlines that matter

**Oil settled above $100 and that is now a macroeconomic problem, not just an energy-sector story.** The trigger was reporting that the U.S. and Israel have plans for renewed strikes on Iran after the midterm elections, plus Iranian claims of attacks on vessels near the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes. Why it matters more than the usual geopolitical headline: the Federal Reserve is currently *raising* interest rates to fight inflation, and energy prices feed directly into inflation. Every dollar oil climbs makes the Fed's job harder and makes another rate increase more likely. That is the chain of logic that turned an oil story into a down day for consumer staples and industrials.

**The October flash purchasing managers' indexes came out this morning, and the headline number was fine while the detail underneath was not.** A *purchasing managers' index*, or PMI, is a monthly survey of business managers; any reading above 50 means activity is expanding, below 50 means contracting. Manufacturing came in at 56.0, up from 55.9 and above the 55.5 expected. Services fell to 57.9 from 58.8. The composite slipped to 57.6 from 58.4, a two-month low but still firmly in expansion. Hiring stayed strong, at the second-fastest pace in over four years. Now the part that matters: **selling prices rose at their sharpest rate since August 2022.** That is companies telling a surveyor they are raising prices faster than at any point in more than four years. With oil above $100 and the Fed meeting in four days, that is the single most consequential data point of the day, and it got almost no attention.

**American Express is cutting about 5% of its workforce explicitly to prepare for AI, and it is not alone.** Chief Executive Stephen Squeri announced the cuts on the earnings call; the company is taking a $336 million pretax restructuring charge. Reported headcount figures vary from roughly 3,500 to nearly 5,000 — Amex had about 77,000 employees at the end of 2025 — and the company has not said which departments are affected. Barclays announced about 5,000 job cuts a day earlier. Why this matters beyond one company: for two years the AI story in the stock market has been about who *sells* AI — chips, data centers, software. This is the other side arriving, companies that merely *use* AI reducing headcount because of it. It is a different economic event, and it cuts against the strong-hiring message in this morning's PMI survey.

**The Federal Reserve meets Tuesday and Wednesday, October 27 and 28, with the decision at 2:00 p.m. Eastern on Wednesday.** Get the direction straight, because it may not match your assumptions: the Fed *raised* its benchmark rate by a quarter point in September, to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%, and signaled at least one more increase. The debate now is whether it holds next week and raises in December, or moves sooner. Most forecasters expect a hold on Wednesday. Fed officials remain in their *blackout period* — a self-imposed rule barring public comment on policy before a meeting — through October 29, so no Fed speech will move the market between now and then. Today's hot selling-price data and $100 oil both push in the hawkish direction, meaning toward higher rates.

**Context worth carrying into next week:** September's consumer price index, released October 14, showed headline inflation at 3.7% year over year, up from 3.4% in August, with core inflation at 2.6%. Inflation is not falling. That is the backdrop against which everything above should be read.

## Today's lesson: how an index is weighted, and why the Dow fell six times harder than the Nasdaq

Today's lesson sits on the technical side — not chart patterns this time, but something more basic that almost nobody is taught: *what the numbers you hear quoted actually measure*. Because today produced a puzzle. The Dow fell 1.23%. The Nasdaq fell 0.21%. The Dow fell almost six times as hard. And when you look inside the Dow for the disaster that caused it, there isn't one — the worst performer was American Express at about 3.7%, and the rest of the top decliners were ordinary 2%-to-3% drops in Home Depot, Coca-Cola, Nike and Caterpillar. No crash. So where did 652 points come from?

The answer is **weighting** — the rule an index uses to decide how much each company counts. There are two rules in common use, and they could hardly be more different.

The S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite are **capitalization-weighted**. *Market capitalization* is a company's share price multiplied by its number of shares — the total market value of the whole business. In a cap-weighted index, a company's influence matches its size. A $3 trillion company moves the index roughly thirty times as much as a $100 billion company, which is intuitively what most people assume an index does.

The Dow is **price-weighted**, and this is the part that surprises people: it counts only the *share price*, with no regard whatsoever for company size. A stock trading at $689 has seven times the influence of a stock trading at $93 — even if the $93 company is the larger business. The index was designed in 1896, when adding up prices was something you could do by hand, and it has never been changed.

Now today's arithmetic does the explaining. Caterpillar closed around $689 and fell 2.13%, which is about $14.68 per share. Coca-Cola closed at $92.88 and fell 2.59%, about $2.47 per share. Coca-Cola had the *larger percentage decline* — it was the worse day for anyone who owned it. But Caterpillar's dollar drop was roughly six times bigger, so Caterpillar did roughly six times as much damage to the Dow. The index does not care which company is worth more or which fell further in percentage terms. It only adds up dollars per share. Pile up five high-priced industrial and consumer names each having a mediocre 2% day and you get 652 points, with no catastrophe anywhere.

Here is the basketball version. Imagine ranking a team's performance by adding up each player's points and dividing by five — that is cap-weighting, roughly: everyone's contribution counts in proportion to what they actually produced. Now imagine instead ranking the team by adding up each player's points *multiplied by his height*. The seven-footer having an average night swings your rating more than the point guard having a brilliant one. Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic; it is just measuring something other than what you assumed. That is the Dow. It has been measuring it since 1896, and it is still the number read aloud on the evening news.

What to do with this: when the three indexes disagree sharply, as they have now for two straight sessions in opposite directions, the disagreement itself is the information. Ask which index was dragged by its *construction* and which by a genuine shift in what investors want to own. Today the Nasdaq's calm tells you technology stopped falling. The S&P 500's modest 0.52% decline tells you the real breadth of the selling — wide but shallow. And the Dow's 1.23% is partly a true signal that money left consumer and industrial names, and partly an accident of a 130-year-old weighting rule meeting a few expensive share prices on a bad day. Three numbers, three different things. Knowing which is which is most of the skill.
