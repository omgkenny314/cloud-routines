# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, September 2, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today the market caught its breath. After three straight losing days driven by a rising oil price and a bond market in revolt, all three major indexes finished higher — modestly, but broadly. The single reason was the bond market: the 10-year Treasury yield spiked early to its highest level since November 2023, and then, instead of pushing higher, it slipped back. That was enough. When yields stopped climbing, stocks stopped falling. Underneath that, the news was actually soft rather than strong — private employers added just 38,000 jobs in August, the slowest month since January, and the Federal Reserve's own survey of business conditions described an economy growing only "modestly." In an ordinary month, weak jobs data is bad news for stocks. Right now it is being read as a reason the Fed may cut interest rates, which would relieve exactly the yield pressure that has been squeezing the market. So the storyline of the day is a market that rallied on relief, not on strength — with the real test arriving after the closing bell, when Broadcom reported the AI results that everyone had been waiting on.

## Headlines that matter

**Private payrolls came in weak.** The ADP National Employment Report showed private employers added just 38,000 jobs in August, below the roughly 48,000 economists expected and down from a revised 46,000 in July. It was the slowest month of hiring since January. Pay growth also cooled: base pay rose 3.2% year over year across all private-sector workers, with people who stayed in their jobs seeing 3.0% and people who changed jobs seeing 4.7%.

*Why it matters:* ADP is a privately produced estimate and it frequently misses the official government figure, so nobody trades it as gospel. But it is the last major labor reading before Friday's official August jobs report, and it points the same direction the Fed has been watching — a labor market that is cooling rather than cracking. A cooling job market is the main argument for the Fed cutting interest rates in September, and lower rates would take pressure off the bond yields that have been the market's problem all week. That is why soft jobs data helped stocks today instead of hurting them.

**The Fed released its Beige Book.** This is the qualitative survey of business conditions gathered from all twelve regional Federal Reserve banks ahead of each policy meeting — anecdotes and interviews, not statistics. It described economic activity as having increased "modestly" since early July, with a positive outlook but mixed sentiment across sectors. Employment rose only very slightly: three districts reported modest job gains, four reported slight gains, and five reported no change at all. On prices, eight districts saw moderate increases, two modest, one slight, and one robust.

*Why it matters:* The Beige Book has no number you can trade. What it gives you is texture — the story behind the statistics that Fed officials will actually be discussing in the room. Read alongside the ADP figure, it describes an economy that is still growing but has stopped adding many workers, with inflation pressure present but not accelerating. That is roughly the profile that lets the Fed cut rates without looking reckless.

**The Middle East escalated again, and oil went with it.** The UAE announced it was cutting all economic ties with Iran, accusing Tehran of firing ballistic missiles at UAE territory. The U.S. military struck Iranian targets around the Strait of Hormuz, which President Trump described as retaliation for Iranian attempts to mine the waterway and for an earlier attack on a U.S. base, along with a threat of a much larger response to any further retaliation. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait, and Iraq are still successfully shipping oil.

*Why it matters:* This is the engine driving everything else this week. The Strait of Hormuz is the chokepoint through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes. Threats to it push oil prices up; higher oil prices push expected inflation up; higher expected inflation pushes bond yields up, because lenders demand more compensation; and higher yields pull stock prices down, both by making bonds a more attractive alternative and by raising the discount applied to companies' future profits. The chain runs oil to inflation to yields to stocks, and it explains why an energy story on the other side of the world moved your technology stocks this week.

## Today's lesson: the 200-day moving average, and what "extended" means

Our recent lessons have covered guidance, the expected move, GAAP versus adjusted earnings, the overnight gap, contingent liabilities, and gross margin. Today's is a technical one, and it starts from a single line in a pre-earnings write-up on Snowflake: heading into today, the stock was trading **51.7% above its 200-day moving average.**

Start with the term. A **moving average** is simply the average closing price over some number of recent trading days, recalculated every day. The 200-day moving average is the average of the last 200 closes — roughly ten months of trading. Each new day, the oldest price drops out and today's price comes in, so the line "moves" forward. Because it blends 200 separate days together, a single dramatic session barely nudges it. That is the entire point: it strips out the noise and shows you the trend the stock has actually been in, as opposed to how it felt this afternoon.

Traders use it two ways. First, as a **trend marker**: a stock persistently above a rising 200-day average is in an uptrend; one below a falling average is in a downtrend. Second — and this is the Snowflake case — as a **measure of extension**. When a stock sits far above its own 200-day average, it has climbed much faster than its own recent history, and the gap tells you how much optimism is already baked into the price. Snowflake at nearly 52% above its 200-day average, up about 52% for the year, was not a stock that needed a good quarter. It was a stock that needed a *great* one, because a good one was already in the price. The market gave you the preview during regular hours today: shares fell about 4% just waiting for the report.

Here is why this connects to the pattern that has dominated this whole week. Credo lost roughly 18% because its gross margin came in three-tenths of a percentage point light. MongoDB dropped about 12% on a beat with raised guidance. Palo Alto Networks fell sharply after beating. None of that is irrational. Those stocks had all run far ahead of their own trend lines, and a stock stretched well above its 200-day average has, in effect, pre-spent its good news. What looks like an overreaction to a tiny miss is usually just the price catching down to reality after running ahead of it.

The basketball version: a career 42% three-point shooter goes on a run and shoots 61% over a month. Nobody thinks he suddenly became a different player — you assume he'll drift back toward 42%, because that 200-game average is who he actually is. The hot month didn't change the truth; it just got ahead of it. A stock 52% above its 200-day average is having that hot month. It may absolutely deserve it — Snowflake's product revenue really is accelerating for the third straight quarter, and sometimes a player genuinely does level up. But the distance from the average tells you exactly how much has to go right just to justify where the price already is. That is the number worth knowing before an earnings report, not after.

One honest limit on this tool: a moving average tells you where a price has been, never where it is going. A stock can stay extended above its 200-day for a year, and a stock below its 200-day can keep falling. It measures stretch, not destiny.
