# Market Close Brief — Thursday, September 10, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Four down days in a row now, and the story has not changed — it has just gotten louder. This morning the government reported that wholesale inflation ran at 5.4% over the past year, the hottest reading of 2026 and a bit above what economists expected. Then oil, which had already been climbing all week on the U.S.-Iran conflict, went vertical: U.S. crude jumped nearly 7% to close above $102 a barrel, its first close over $100 in this cycle. Those two facts point the same direction, and the bond market reacted immediately — the 10-year Treasury yield pushed to roughly 4.90%, the highest since 2023. Traders now put the odds of the Federal Reserve *raising* interest rates next Tuesday and Wednesday at around 60%, up from a coin flip yesterday. The stock damage was again moderate rather than dramatic: the S&P 500 lost 0.58%, and all three major indexes fell between half and two-thirds of a percent. But the fear gauge ticked up, gold and silver got hit hard, and the losses were led by technology and materials. The single storyline: **an oil shock is turning into an inflation problem, and an inflation problem is turning into a Federal Reserve problem — with the August consumer inflation report landing tomorrow morning at 7:30 a.m. Central, one business day before the Fed decides.**

- **Gold and silver both fell hard, which is the day's most interesting cross-check.** Spot gold dropped roughly 1% to somewhere near $4,342–$4,362 an ounce; silver fell about 3.3% to roughly $64.92. **These are mid-session quotes from single sources, not confirmed settles.** More on why falling gold on an inflation day is not a contradiction, in the headlines section.

## Big movers and why

**AeroVironment (ticker AVAV) was the day's standout winner, up about 9.9%** — and it traded as much as 13% higher at the open. AeroVironment makes military drones and unmanned systems. It reported record fiscal first-quarter revenue of $480.5 million, up 6% from a year ago and about $24.5 million above what analysts expected, with adjusted earnings of 59 cents per share against roughly 25 cents expected — a beat of more than 100%. The Autonomous Systems division did the heavy lifting, with sales up 21% to $346 million. Management also told investors that laser weapons could eventually become a flagship business line comparable in size to its biggest existing ones. The number I want you to hold onto, though, is this: **funded backlog rose 37% from a year ago to a record $1.5 billion.** That is today's lesson.

**CooperCompanies (ticker COO) was the day's worst large-cap loser, down somewhere between 14% and 16%.** Cooper makes contact lenses and women's-health products. I previewed this report in last night's brief; here is how the market judged it. Revenue of $1.07 billion came in below the roughly $1.1 billion analysts expected. Adjusted earnings of $1.15 per share actually *beat* the $1.12 expected. The stock still lost roughly a sixth of its value, and the reason was the forecast: for the current quarter Cooper guided to adjusted earnings of $1.05 to $1.09 per share, against a $1.19 consensus — a miss of roughly 10% on the midpoint — and trimmed the full year to $4.51–$4.55 versus $4.63 expected. Separately, Cooper concluded its strategic review and announced it will *keep* CooperSurgical, saying the offers it received were not in shareholders' interest. Investors had been hoping for a sale. Piper Sandler and Baird both downgraded the stock. **Sources put the decline at 13.9%, 14.08%, 15%, 15.9% and 16.8%. I could not pin down one closing figure; "down roughly 15%" is the honest answer.**

**NuScale Power (ticker SMR) jumped somewhere between 11% and 15%,** trading as high as about $11.30 and adding more than half a billion dollars of market value in a session where the broad market fell. NuScale builds small modular nuclear reactors. What makes this notable is that reporters could find **no company-specific news at all** — no press release, no analyst action, no contract. It moved on sector momentum: the ongoing story that artificial-intelligence data centers need enormous amounts of round-the-clock electricity, plus bipartisan political support for advanced nuclear. Worth filing away: a stock that moves 13% on nothing is a stock where the price is being set by a story rather than by new information.

**Semiconductors were the weak spot in big technology.** Nvidia (ticker NVDA) fell about 1.4% to close at $218.25. Marvell Technology (ticker MRVL), Intel (ticker INTC) and Lam Research (ticker LRCX) each fell about 3%. The reason is not chip demand — it is the bond market. Building AI data centers is being financed with enormous amounts of borrowed money, and when long-term interest rates rise, that borrowing gets more expensive and the future profits it is meant to produce get discounted more heavily.

**Oracle (ticker ORCL) fell into its own earnings report, down roughly 4% to about $155.** **Sources conflict here — one mid-morning report had it down only 1.5%, while another had it at $155.22, down 3.97% from a $161.63 prior close. The second one is internally consistent, so I lean on it, but flag it as unconfirmed.** What happened after the bell is in the next section, and it was a very different story.

**Elsewhere:** Apple (ticker AAPL) inched higher, recovering a little of yesterday's post-event decline. Qualcomm (ticker QCOM) closed at $176.88, up 0.27%, after a big run earlier in the week on its custom-silicon partnership with Amazon Web Services — **one source put today's gain at 3.17%, which does not match the closing quote, so treat the smaller figure as the likelier one.** In the premarket, gold miner Franco-Nevada (ticker FNV) fell 3.6% and FedEx (ticker FDX) fell 3.1%, hit directly by the diesel line in the inflation report; **those are premarket prints, not closes.** Energy companies were broadly the day's shelter, as they have been all week.

## Headlines that matter

**Wholesale inflation ran hotter than expected, and the details were worse than the headline.** The Producer Price Index — which measures what producers *receive* for goods and services, one step upstream from what you and I pay — rose 0.4% in August, exactly in line with forecasts. But the year-over-year rate accelerated to 5.4%, up from 4.7% in July and above the roughly 5.1%–5.3% economists expected. That is the highest annual reading of 2026. Core PPI, which strips out food and energy, ran 4.6% against 4.5% expected. The damage came from goods: energy prices rose 4.2% in the month and **diesel fuel rose 24.1%.** *Why this matters:* the producer index is a leading indicator for the consumer index. Companies that pay more for diesel eventually charge more for shipping, and that shows up in the price of everything shipped. A 24% jump in diesel in a single month is the oil war arriving in the domestic price data. That is why FedEx fell on an inflation report.

**Jobless claims were a non-event, which is itself information.** Initial claims for unemployment benefits came in at 206,000 for the week ended September 5, matching both the forecast and the prior week exactly. *Why it matters:* the Federal Reserve has two jobs — keep prices stable and keep employment high. When the labor market is visibly weakening, the Fed has a reason to go easy on inflation. A flat, healthy claims number removes that excuse. It gives the Fed room to be hawkish.

**Oil closed above $100 for the first time in this cycle as attacks on shipping intensified.** The U.S.-Iran conflict is now in its seventh month. Goldman Sachs warned this week that continued escalation raises the risk of oil above $120 a barrel. *Why it matters:* oil is an input to nearly every price in the economy. There is a chain here worth memorizing, because it has driven the market for two straight weeks: tanker attacks in the Persian Gulf → higher crude → higher diesel and gasoline → higher consumer prices → a Fed more likely to raise interest rates → lower stock prices and higher bond yields. Every link in that chain fired today.

**The odds of a Fed rate hike next week jumped to about 60%.** The Federal Open Market Committee meets Tuesday and Wednesday, September 15 and 16. As recently as a few weeks ago the market thought the Fed was done raising rates; after Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech, a strong jobs report, and now this inflation print, futures markets put the chance of a quarter-point increase at roughly 60%. Fed officials are in their pre-meeting blackout and cannot comment. *Why it matters:* rate *hikes* in 2026 were not the base case for most investors this year. A market repricing that possibility in real time is a market where nearly every asset has to be re-valued at once — which is exactly what you are seeing in stocks, bonds, gold and bitcoin all moving together.

**Gold and silver fell on a hot inflation day, and that seems backwards.** Gold is supposed to be the inflation hedge. It dropped about 1%; silver fell more than three times as much. *Why it matters, and why it is not a contradiction:* gold pays you nothing — no dividend, no interest. Its main competitor is a Treasury bond, which does pay interest. When the inflation report pushes expected interest rates *up*, holding gold gets more expensive in terms of the income you give up, and gold falls even though inflation is the thing gold is supposed to protect against. Silver fell harder because roughly half of silver demand is industrial, so silver is part precious metal and part economic bet — and a Fed that might raise rates is bad for the economic-bet half.

## After the bell

It is a little over half an hour past the close. The two big reports are already out, and both were good.

**Oracle (ticker ORCL) rose about 7% in extended trading after a clear beat.** Adjusted earnings were $1.92 per share against roughly $1.74 expected, on revenue of $19.35 billion versus about $19.14 billion expected. Revenue grew 30% from a year ago in the fiscal first quarter, which ended August 31. Cloud revenue rose 62% to $11.6 billion, with cloud infrastructure specifically up 121%. For fiscal 2027 Oracle guided to $8.10 in adjusted earnings per share on at least $90 billion of revenue, slightly above the roughly $8.07 and $89.76 billion analysts were modeling. What that number means is the subject of tonight's lesson.

**Adobe (ticker ADBE) rose about 3.0% to roughly $361.06 in extended trading.** Adjusted earnings were $6.13 per share against about $6.09 expected, on revenue of $6.76 billion against roughly $6.70 billion expected — the fifth straight quarter of beating the earnings estimate. Adobe raised its full-year revenue target to a range of $26.58 billion to $26.63 billion and lifted its full-year earnings target as well. The growth driver was artificial intelligence: what Adobe calls "AI-first annualized recurring revenue" grew more than 150% from a year ago, and the company said it has crossed one billion monthly active users across its creative and productivity products. Context worth knowing: options traders had been pricing in a move of roughly 8% in either direction, so a 3% gain is a positive result that came in well *under* what the market was braced for.

**One follow-up from last night:** American Eagle Outfitters (ticker AEO) reported Wednesday evening. Revenue rose 6.5% to $1.37 billion and the company beat on earnings, helped by tariff refunds — but earnings per share still fell more than 50% from a year ago, and management pointed to roughly $20 million of incremental tariff costs and a 150-to-200 basis point hit to gross margin. ## What to watch tomorrow

**The August Consumer Price Index at 7:30 a.m. Central (8:30 a.m. Eastern) is the whole day.** This is the consumer-level inflation report, and it lands one business day before the Fed's meeting begins. Consensus is roughly 3.3% to 3.4% year over year. If it comes in at 3.3%, that would be the third straight monthly decline in the annual rate — which would ease the pressure considerably. If it comes in hot, after today's 5.4% producer print, expect the rate-hike odds to move again and expect the reaction in stocks and bonds to be immediate. Note that oil's biggest surge happened *this week*, so it will show up in the September report, not this one.

**University of Michigan consumer sentiment, preliminary September reading, at 9:00 a.m. Central.** Economists expect a decline to 51.0 from 51.7. That is a historically depressed level. The part professionals actually read is the survey's inflation-expectations questions — the Fed watches whether ordinary people *expect* higher prices, because expectations tend to become self-fulfilling.

**Earnings before the opening bell:** Kroger (ticker KR), the supermarket chain, is the notable one — a clean read on grocery inflation and how much of it consumers are absorbing. Hooker Furnishings (ticker HOFT) also reports, another tariff-exposed furniture name.

## Today's lesson: backlog, or the revenue a company has already won but not yet earned

Every lesson so far has been about money a company *has* made — margins, earnings quality, operating income — or about what a chart says about price. Today let's look at a number that sits in between: **backlog**, the work a company has already been hired to do but has not done yet.

Here is the plainest version. Suppose you run a painting business. In August you painted eleven houses and collected the money. That is revenue — it is in the past, it is settled, and it is what shows up in your income statement. But you have also signed contracts for thirty more houses over the next two years, at agreed prices. You have not painted them and you have not been paid. That thirty-house pipeline does not appear as revenue anywhere. It is your **backlog**, and it is arguably the most useful thing anyone could know about your business, because it tells them what next year looks like before next year happens.

Public companies report this. AeroVironment calls it **funded backlog** — orders where the customer has actually appropriated the money, as opposed to intentions. Software and cloud companies use a mouthful of an accounting term, **remaining performance obligations**, usually shortened to RPO: the total dollar value of contracts signed but not yet delivered. Different names, same idea.

Both of today's big winners were backlog stories, and it is worth seeing how differently the two numbers behaved. AeroVironment's revenue grew 6% — respectable, not thrilling. But its funded backlog grew 37%, to a record $1.5 billion. The gap between those two numbers is the whole reason the stock rose 10%: the business you can see today is growing slowly, and the business already contracted for tomorrow is growing six times faster. Oracle's version was more dramatic. Revenue grew 30%, which is remarkable for a company nearly fifty years old. But remaining performance obligations came in at $664 billion — up $209 billion in a single year, with more than $30 billion of new AI cloud contracts signed in the last three months alone. Oracle's *entire annual revenue* is running around $90 billion. It has signed contracts worth more than seven years of current revenue.

Here is the basketball version. Revenue is points scored this season. Backlog is your recruiting class — the players who have signed letters of intent but have not suited up yet. A team can have a mediocre record and a top-ranked incoming class, and the smart evaluators will be far more excited about that team than the record suggests. That is what a 37% backlog gain on 6% revenue growth is telling you.

Now the discipline, because backlog is the number most easily oversold. Three questions to ask every time. First: **when does it convert?** A $664 billion figure spread over ten years is a very different thing from the same figure over three. Second: **can it actually be delivered?** Oracle has to build the data centers, buy the chips and secure the electricity to fulfill those contracts, and that costs enormous money up front — which is precisely why Oracle's own borrowing is part of the story that pushed bond yields up today. Signing the contract is the easy half. Third: **can it be cancelled?** "Funded" backlog, like AeroVironment's, is stronger than unfunded, because the customer has already set aside the cash. Contracts vary. A signed order is not a completed sale, and companies that lean hard on backlog in their press releases are sometimes drawing attention away from a present that is less impressive than the future they are describing.

Which is the useful takeaway: backlog is one of the few genuinely forward-looking numbers in a financial report — most of the rest is history — but it is a promise, not a payment.
