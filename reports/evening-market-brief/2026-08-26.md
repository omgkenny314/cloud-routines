# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, August 26, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a waiting room. The three major indexes barely moved — the S&P 500 finished within two points of where it started, which is about as close to perfectly flat as a full trading day gets — because the entire market was holding its breath for one event that happened after the closing bell: Nvidia's quarterly earnings report. The morning did bring real news. The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge showed prices still running well above the Fed's 2% target, which normally would have knocked stocks down, and Meta Platforms agreed to a roughly $17–18 billion settlement with dozens of state attorneys general over harm to teenagers, one of the largest corporate settlements in recent memory. Neither storyline could pull the market in a direction, because the number that mattered wasn't due until 4:20 in the afternoon Eastern time. When it landed, Nvidia beat expectations on both revenue and profit — and the stock still fell. That is the day's real story, and it's the subject of today's lesson.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: 7,675.70, down about 0.02%** — a decline of roughly 1.6 points. Essentially unchanged. Investors were unwilling to make a big bet in either direction ahead of Nvidia.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 53,463.88, down 113.52 points, or 0.21%.** The weakest of the three, snapping a four-day winning streak. The Dow holds fewer big technology names, so it got less of the modest tech support that held the other two indexes up.
- **Nasdaq Composite: 26,130.20, down about 0.08%** — roughly 21 points. Software stocks were the drag: Intuit's disappointing outlook, released last night, pulled down a whole group of its peers.
- **10-year Treasury yield: about 4.643%, up roughly 2.4 basis points.** A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so that is a rise of about 0.024%. Yields barely budged despite a hot inflation report — a falling oil price and last week's Treasury bond-buyback program kept a lid on them. The 2-year yield eased to about 4.176% and the 30-year to about 5.166%.
- **Crude oil (West Texas Intermediate): about $80.78 a barrel, down roughly 1.9%.** A second straight sharp drop, on worries about weakening global demand for fuel. Cheaper oil feeds through to cheaper gasoline and shipping, which is mildly good news for inflation.
- **Bitcoin: roughly $78,500–78,700 during U.S. morning hours**, after touching a three-month high above $81,000 yesterday. I could not verify a precise 4:00 p.m. Eastern price today, so treat that range as a morning snapshot rather than a close. The recent rally has been driven by heavy inflows into bitcoin exchange-traded funds and a softer dollar.

## Big movers and why

- **Abercrombie & Fitch (ANF) — up somewhere in the range of 37% to 40%**, one of the largest single-day moves any established retailer has posted this year. The company earned $4.17 per share for the quarter against its own prior guidance of $1.80 to $2.00, on revenue of $1.27 billion, up 5%. The reason for the blowout was unusual: roughly $100 million in refunded tariffs, money the company had previously paid to the U.S. government on imported goods and got back. Management raised its full-year profit forecast to $13.10–13.60 per share from $10.20–11.00, and lifted its expected operating profit margin to 14.5–15% from 12–12.5%. Different outlets reported today's gain anywhere from 12% to 40% depending on when they snapped the price; I could not confirm a single official closing percentage.
- **Intuit (INTU) — down about 9.2%.** The TurboTax and QuickBooks maker beat earnings estimates last night by roughly 13% but forecast fiscal 2027 revenue growth of only 9–10%, a slowdown from the nearly 14% it just delivered. Yesterday's lesson covered exactly this — guidance about the future usually matters more than results from the past.
- **The Intuit ripple effect.** Intuit's cautious outlook was read as a warning about business software spending generally. **ServiceNow (NOW)** and **Workday (WDAY)** each fell about 2%, and **Salesforce (CRM)** slipped about 1% during the regular session — before its own report tonight went a very different direction (see "After the bell").
- **Meta Platforms (META) — up about 3%** at midday, after agreeing to settle the multistate teen-safety case. One source reported the gain as closer to 1%, so treat the exact figure as unconfirmed; the direction is not in doubt. Investors treated a large, known, one-time cost as better than an open-ended trial with an unknown outcome.
- **Zoom Communications (ZM) — down about 7%.** Zoom beat on revenue last night but guided to third-quarter earnings of $1.46–1.48 per share against the $1.50 analysts expected. A two-cent shortfall on the forecast cost the stock 7%.
- **Kohl's (KSS) — up about 2%**, after raising its full-year outlook — helped, like Abercrombie, by about $150 million in tariff refunds — and announcing it would restart share buybacks of up to $100 million this year.
- **Nvidia (NVDA) — down more than 1%** during the regular session, as traders trimmed positions ahead of the report rather than hold through it.

## Headlines that matter

**The Fed's preferred inflation gauge came in hot.** The July personal consumption expenditures price index — the PCE index, the specific inflation measure the Federal Reserve watches most closely — showed core prices up 3.3% from a year earlier. "Core" means excluding food and energy, which bounce around too much month to month to reveal the underlying trend. The headline figure, including food and energy, was about 3.7%, slightly above the 3.6% economists expected; sources differed on whether that was an uptick or unchanged from June. Both measures rose 0.2% on the month. Personal income rose 0.4% and spending 0.2%, both a bit stronger than forecast. *Why it matters:* the Fed's target is 2%. At 3.3% core, inflation is not merely above target, it is stuck well above target — and the market is currently handicapping a roughly one-in-three chance the Fed's next move is a rate *hike*, not a cut. Every inflation report between now and the September meeting is a vote on that question.

**Meta settled the teen social-media case for roughly $17–18 billion.** Meta reached a settlement with 48 state attorneys general, ending a landmark federal trial over claims the company knowingly designed Facebook and Instagram to be addictive to minors. Beyond the money, Meta agreed to product changes including a default two-hour daily time limit for users under 18. Reported figures ranged from $17 billion to $18 billion; a notable wrinkle is that Meta pays only about 70% of the total unless TikTok and YouTube agree to comparable one-hour limits and their own payments of roughly $5.3 billion each. *Why it matters:* markets hate open-ended legal risk more than they hate large bills. A number you can write down goes into the financial model; a trial that could go anywhere does not. That's why the stock rose on an enormous payout. It also puts pressure on Alphabet, YouTube's owner, and on TikTok.

**Jackson Hole begins tomorrow.** The Kansas City Fed's annual economic symposium runs Thursday through Saturday, with new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivering his first keynote in that role Friday morning at 8:00 a.m. Eastern. *Why it matters:* a new chair's first major speech is how he tells the market what he cares about. With inflation at 3.3% and a rate hike genuinely on the table, every sentence will be parsed. Today's flat market is partly about Nvidia and partly about not wanting to be positioned ahead of Friday.

**Oil kept falling.** Crude has now dropped sharply two sessions running, on cuts to 2026 global demand forecasts. *Why it matters:* oil is an input cost for nearly every physical business, so a sustained decline eases inflation pressure from the supply side — which is one reason bond yields didn't spike today even after a hot inflation print. It's also bad news for energy company profits.

## After the bell

**Nvidia (NVDA)** reported second-quarter results for its fiscal 2027 year, and the numbers were strong. Revenue was $96.22 billion against roughly $92.17 billion expected — a beat of about 4% — and adjusted earnings of $2.22 per share against $2.10 expected. Revenue and profit both roughly doubled from a year ago. Gross margin came in near 75%.

The stock fell anyway, trading down roughly 2% to 4% in early after-hours action. Two things appear to be behind it. First, management guided third-quarter gross margin down to about 74% from 75% — a one-point decline, but the direction is what matters to investors watching for signs that memory-chip and manufacturing costs are catching up with Nvidia. Second, and more fundamentally, expectations were extraordinarily high going in; the options market had been pricing a swing of hundreds of billions of dollars in market value in either direction. I saw the third-quarter revenue guidance reported at about $108 billion against a consensus near $104 billion, and a new share-repurchase authorization reported at both $50 billion and $80 billion by different outlets. I could not verify either figure against a second reliable source, so treat the guidance number and the buyback size as unconfirmed. After-hours prices move quickly, so the percentage decline above is a snapshot, not a settled figure.

**Salesforce (CRM)**, the customer-relationship-management software company, was tonight's clear winner: shares rose about 14% in extended trading. Revenue was $11.35 billion against $11.32 billion expected, up 11% from a year ago. Net income was $3.53 billion, or $4.29 per share, up 87% — though a large piece of that came from a $2.6 billion gain on its stake in the AI company Anthropic, which is an investment gain rather than money earned from selling software. Guidance was the real driver: third-quarter adjusted earnings of $3.42–3.44 per share on $11.42–11.50 billion in revenue, both above what analysts expected, and a full-year outlook of $16.67–16.71 per share on $46.1–46.4 billion in revenue.

**CrowdStrike (CRWD)**, the cybersecurity company, was scheduled to release results at 4:05 p.m. Eastern with a conference call at 5:00 p.m. The key figure to watch is net new annual recurring revenue — the amount of new yearly subscription revenue added in the quarter — which management had guided to $284–286 million. Analysts expected about $0.29 per share on $1.44 billion in revenue. **HP Inc. (HPQ)** also reported this afternoon, with expectations near $0.66–0.72 per share on about $14.34 billion in revenue, and the central question being whether rising memory and processor costs are squeezing its profit margins. At the hour I'm writing this, I could not verify actual results for either company, so I won't guess at them.

## Today's lesson: the expected move, or why beating the number isn't the same as winning

Our recent lessons covered operating margin, dilution, support and resistance, index construction, and guidance. Today's is a technical and market-structure concept, and Nvidia handed us a textbook case.

Here is the puzzle. Nvidia earned more than Wall Street expected. It sold more than Wall Street expected. Revenue roughly doubled from a year ago. And the stock went *down*. If a stock price simply reflected whether a company did well, that would be impossible.

The resolution is a concept called the **expected move**. Before a big scheduled event — an earnings report, a Fed decision — traders in the options market place bets on how much a stock will move, in either direction. Those bets can be converted into a single number: the size of the swing the market is collectively bracing for. That number is derived from **implied volatility**, which is just the options market's forecast of how bumpy a stock is about to get. Ahead of tonight's report, coverage described the options market as pricing a potential swing of hundreds of billions of dollars in Nvidia's market value.

That expected move is the real bar. Not the analyst estimate — the *price already contains* the analyst estimate, plus the market's guess about how much the company will beat it by. Nvidia had beaten its own guidance thirteen quarters running, and everyone knew it. So a 4% revenue beat wasn't news; something like a 4% beat was already the assumption baked into the price. When the actual result merely matched what was already assumed, and management simultaneously signaled that gross margin — the share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of making the product — would tick down from 75% to 74%, there was nothing left to push the stock higher and one small thing pushing it lower.

Here is the basketball version, and it's exact. Your team is favored by 14 points. They win by 9. Did they play well? Yes — they won. Did anyone betting on them make money? No. The **point spread** is the market's expectation, and the whole game is played against the spread, not against zero. Wall Street works the same way: analyst estimates are the opening line, the options market's expected move is the spread, and a company has to beat the *expectations about the beat* to move the stock up.

The practical takeaway for reading the news: when you see "Company X beat earnings but the stock fell," you are not looking at an irrational market. You are looking at a company that won the game by 9 when it was favored by 14. The next question worth asking is always what was already priced in — and the harder that question is to answer, the more humble it's worth being about predicting reactions to earnings at all.
