# Market Close Brief — Thursday, October 1, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

October opened with a fight in the bond market, and stocks spent the day watching it. Early on, the yield on the 10-year Treasury note pushed up to 5.344% — the highest reading since 2002, twenty-four years ago — and stocks sold off on it. Then, around late morning, the bond market turned. Yields came back down, the 10-year finished lower on the day, and stocks climbed out of the hole to close slightly higher. The major indexes ended up with tiny gains that badly undersell how much movement happened underneath them. The single biggest storyline was that reversal in yields, and the reason it happened: two Federal Reserve officials, Vice Chair Philip Jefferson and New York Fed President John Williams, both signaled they are in no rush to raise interest rates again after September's increase. That cooled the fear that has been driving bond yields to multi-decade highs. The day's other headline was a single stock — Accenture, which had what looks like the best day in its history after an earnings report, and which quietly tells you a lot about how this market is pricing artificial intelligence.

## Big movers and why

- **Accenture (ACN), up about 18.8%, closing at $217.86 from $183.37.** The consulting and technology services giant reported fiscal fourth-quarter earnings of $3.29 a share on revenue of $18.68 billion, against expectations of roughly $3.18 and $18.03 billion. Revenue grew 6.2% from a year earlier. But the number that moved the stock was bookings — the value of work clients have signed up for but that has not yet been delivered. Full-year bookings hit a record $84.5 billion, and the company logged a quarterly record of 141 individual client bookings worth at least $100 million each. Operating margin in the quarter jumped to 15.3% from 11.6% a year earlier, and operating income rose 40% to $2.86 billion. Accenture also returned a record $11.5 billion to shareholders over the year, up 38%, split between $7.5 billion of share repurchases and $4 billion of dividends, and announced a partnership with Anthropic on artificial intelligence safety. A note on the number, because it matters: the stock traded as high as $227.63 intraday, which would have been a gain of about 24%, and closed up about 18.8%. Different sources reported the day's gain anywhere from 15.6% to 22%. The figure best supported is the one you can compute from the two closing prices: roughly 18.8%.
- **Corteva (CTVA), down somewhere between 82% and 84%, to about $13.96 — and this is not a disaster.** This is worth understanding because the headline number is terrifying and almost meaningless. Corteva completed a **spin-off**: it separated its seed and genetics business into a brand-new independent public company called Vylor, trading as VYLR, and gave every Corteva shareholder one Vylor share for each Corteva share they held. So a Corteva holder did not lose 82% of their money. They now own a smaller Corteva plus a share of Vylor. The stock price fell because the company it represents is now much smaller. Vylor itself rose about 3.76% in its first day of trading. When you see a stock down 80% and no bad news attached, look for a spin-off or a stock split before you look for a catastrophe.
- **Micron Technology (MU), roughly flat to slightly lower, from a prior close of $1,065.11.** This one is a lesson in itself. Micron reported a blowout quarter after the bell on Wednesday — adjusted earnings of $33.42 a share against $31.61 expected, revenue of $54.23 billion against $51.07 billion expected, and guidance for about $61.5 billion of revenue in the current quarter, far above what analysts had modeled. The stock jumped sharply in after-hours trading on that news. And then, by Thursday's close, it had given essentially all of it back. Reporting varied on the exact finish — one source described the stock as having "barely budged," another put it down about 1.46% — so call it flat to modestly lower. Either way, a beat that large producing no gain tells you the good news was already in the price, and that investors are now weighing Micron's rising capital spending and costs against its rising revenue.
- **Other notable gainers.** Synopsys, McKesson, and IBM were all higher by at least 5.9% on the session. I was not able to independently verify the specific catalyst behind each of those moves today, so I am naming them without explaining them rather than guessing.

## Headlines that matter

**The Fed signaled patience, and that is why the bond market turned.** Vice Chair Philip Jefferson said policymakers may need more time to determine whether additional rate increases are necessary. New York Fed President John Williams said there was no urgency to consider another increase following the Fed's rate rise in September. Why this matters: the Fed is in a tightening cycle — it is raising rates to fight inflation, not cutting them — and the bond market has been pricing in the risk of more increases to come. That expectation is what drove the 10-year yield to a 24-year high this morning. When two senior officials say "not so fast," that pricing unwinds, yields fall, and stocks get relief. Higher yields hurt stocks in two ways at once: they make safe bonds a more attractive alternative to stocks, and they raise the borrowing costs that squeeze corporate profits.

**ISM manufacturing: growth fine, prices alarming.** The Institute for Supply Management's manufacturing index came in at 54.5 for September, a hair below the 55.0 expected and a touch under August's 54.6. Any reading above 50 means the sector is expanding, so this is the ninth straight month of growth — the longest stretch since 2022. New orders improved to 55.3 from 53.7, and employment improved to 52.7 from 51.2. Both are encouraging. But the Prices Paid index — what manufacturers are paying for raw materials — jumped to 77.9 from 71.1, a leap of 6.8 points, marking the 24th consecutive month of rising input costs. Why this matters: that is the inflation the Fed is worried about, showing up in the pipeline before it shows up in consumer prices. It is the single most hawkish number released today, and it pulls in the opposite direction from Jefferson and Williams.

**Jobless claims fell to 197,000.** Initial claims for unemployment benefits came in at 197,000 against a forecast of 201,000, down from a revised 198,000 the prior week, and the lowest since mid-July. The four-week average slipped 2,500 to 200,000. Why this matters: this is the cleanest weekly read on whether employers are laying people off, and the answer is plainly no. A tight labor market is good news for workers and awkward news for a Fed trying to cool inflation, because it supports the wage growth that feeds into prices.

**Oil and the Strait of Hormuz, still.** Crude stayed elevated near $90 because Middle East exports remain constrained, with the U.S. blockade on Iranian oil exports and sanctions keeping supply tight. Why this matters: energy is an input to nearly everything, so oil near $90 feeds directly into the inflation data that drives the bond market that drives stocks. This is the chain that connects a shipping lane to your portfolio.

**A side note with real consequences: France.** Part of today's demand for U.S. Treasuries came from worry about France's fiscal outlook, which pushed money toward safe-haven assets. There was also an unwinding of crowded positions — meaning a lot of traders were betting the same way on yields going higher, and when the move reversed, they had to buy bonds back in a hurry, which accelerated the drop in yields. Crowded trades reverse violently; that is part of why today's bond move was as sharp as it was.

## Today's lesson: support and resistance, and what a failed breakout tells you

Today's lesson is a technical one — technical analysis meaning the study of price behavior itself, rather than the business or the economy underneath it. And today the clearest example was not a stock at all. It was the 10-year Treasury yield.

Here is the idea. **Resistance** is a price level where selling has repeatedly overwhelmed buying, so the price keeps failing to get above it. **Support** is the mirror image: a level where buying has repeatedly overwhelmed selling, so the price keeps refusing to fall below. These are not magic lines. They are just places where a lot of people have decided something, which is why they tend to matter again the next time price arrives there. Think of it as a spot on the floor where the defense always sets up — not because the hardwood is special, but because that is where the defenders have learned to stand.

Now watch what happened today. The 10-year yield climbed all morning and reached 5.344%, a level it had not seen since 2002. That is about as significant a ceiling as exists: a twenty-four-year high. Getting above it would have been what traders call a **breakout** — price pushing through resistance into territory with no recent history, where there are no old buyers or sellers waiting and moves can run fast. Instead, the yield touched that level, could not hold it, and reversed hard enough to finish the day *lower* than where it started. That is a **failed breakout**, and it is one of the more informative things a chart can show you. It means the move arrived at the critical level with no one left to carry it further.

Why does a failed breakout matter more than an ordinary down day? Because of who gets hurt. Traders who bought into the breakout — in this case, betting yields would keep climbing — are now sitting on losing positions, and they have to get out. Their exiting pushes the move further in the opposite direction. That is precisely what today's reports described as "an unwinding of crowded positions." A lot of people were leaning the same way, the level held, and the scramble to unwind accelerated the reversal. The stock market's recovery from an early loss to a small gain was the downstream effect.

The discipline this teaches is about sequencing. The news — Jefferson and Williams talking down the urgency of more rate increases — is the *reason* the level held. The chart is what showed you that it did, and showed it in real time, before anyone had written the explanation. A good analyst uses both: the level tells you where the fight will happen, and the fundamentals tell you who is likely to win it. Neither one alone would have told you much today. One more thing worth noting, and it is the honest limitation of the tool: a failed breakout tells you the ceiling held *this time*. It does not tell you the ceiling will hold tomorrow.
