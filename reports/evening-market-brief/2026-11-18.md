## The day in one paragraph

This was a split day, and the split is the story. The Nasdaq finished higher while the Dow fell more than 500 points — the kind of day where "the market" did two different things at once and the average is useless. The single biggest storyline was Nvidia, which reported genuinely enormous results last night and then could not hold the gains. The company beat on revenue, beat on profit, guided the next quarter above expectations and raised its outlook for next year, and the stock still spent the day in the red for its fifth straight losing session. That is worth sitting with, because it tells you something important: when a market has stopped trusting a company's leadership and a securities regulator is reportedly asking questions, good numbers stop being enough. Underneath that, two other forces pushed in the same direction. The Federal Reserve released the minutes of its October meeting and they were hawkish — almost every official there expects to keep *raising* interest rates, not cutting them. And UnitedHealth fell hard on a proposed government rule, dragging the Dow down with it almost single-handedly. So: technology clawed back a little, health care and energy were hit, and the bond market kept its yields above 5%.

## Big movers and why

**UnitedHealth Group (ticker UNH) — down somewhere between 6% and 9%, and the reason the Dow looks worse than the market.** Sources genuinely disagree on the size: one post-close report said 6%, another said 8%, and a mid-afternoon reading had it at 9.01%. The trigger was a proposed federal rule from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, awkwardly named CRUSH — Comprehensive Regulations to Uncover Suspicious Healthcare — which would expand audits of health insurers and give the agency more power to claw back improper payments. Senator Elizabeth Warren separately called for breaking up vertically integrated health care conglomerates and named UnitedHealth among them. One analysis I read made an honest admission worth repeating: there was no *new* agency action today, so the reason this second-day decline was larger than the first is not fully settled. **Humana (ticker HUM) fell about 6%** and **Cigna (ticker CI) fell over 2%** on the same news.

**Nvidia (ticker NVDA) — down about 3%, its fifth consecutive losing session.** The numbers were not the problem. More on them below. The problem is that co-founder and chief executive Jensen Huang left abruptly last week, the board has ruled out his return, the search for a permanent replacement is ongoing with Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress serving as interim chief executive, and the Wall Street Journal reported yesterday that the Securities and Exchange Commission has opened a civil investigation into whether the company's disclosures misled investors, reportedly connected to chip sales in Asia. The stock opened higher on the earnings and gave it all back. Sources put the intraday decline anywhere from 1.4% to 3.5%; the nearest-to-close reading I found was about 3.09%. I could not verify an official closing price.

**TJX Companies (ticker TJX) — up roughly 9% to 10%, the day's standout gainer.** TJX is the off-price retailer behind T.J. Maxx, Marshalls and HomeGoods. ## Headlines that matter

**The Fed's October minutes landed, and they were hawkish.** The Federal Open Market Committee met on October 27 and 28 and held its target range at 3.75% to 4.00%. Today's minutes revealed that "almost all" participants thought further rate *increases* would likely be appropriate to get inflation back to the 2% target. A couple of officials went further and said a hike of *more than* a quarter point could warrant consideration at the December 8–9 meeting if incoming inflation data disappoints. Governor Christopher Waller has separately said a half-point increase should be considered. San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly argued today for hikes but with a "measured approach." Markets are now pricing a quarter-point December hike as close to certain, with futures implying further increases into January. *Why it matters:* this is the single biggest force acting on your portfolio right now, and it runs opposite to the direction most people assume the Fed moves. When the rate available on perfectly safe government debt goes up, the price any rational investor will pay for a company's uncertain future profits goes down. That arithmetic punishes expensive, long-dated growth stories hardest — which happens to describe the technology companies that have led this market for years.

**The Fed is now openly worried about AI debt.** Buried in the same minutes: almost all participants discussed financial stability risks that could emerge if the returns on AI investment disappoint investors, with specific unease about "opaque" debt financing in the sector. *Why it matters:* this is a central bank saying out loud that it sees the AI buildout as a potential systemic risk, not just an exciting growth story. The money for data centers increasingly comes from borrowing, and the Fed is signalling it does not have clear visibility into who owes what to whom. That is the quiet background to every AI-related selloff you have watched this month.

**Nvidia's actual results were extraordinary.** Revenue of $115.84 billion against about $109.10 billion expected. Adjusted earnings of $2.68 per share against roughly $2.48 expected. Data center revenue of $107.6 billion, up 110% from a year ago and up 21% from the prior quarter. Gross margin of 74.9% on an adjusted basis. Net income of $65.3 billion for a single quarter. Guidance of about $130 billion for the current quarter, give or take 3%, which assumes *no* data center compute revenue from China at all, and the company raised its revenue growth outlook for the next fiscal year to roughly 75%. Its new Vera Rubin systems are running at CoreWeave, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, Oracle and Nebius. *Why it matters:* you now have a clean separation between a business and a stock. The business is compounding at a rate that has almost no precedent at this scale. The stock fell anyway, because governance, leadership and regulatory risk are real risks that no quarterly beat can answer. Hold that distinction — it is one of the most useful things you can learn this year.

**The consumer is holding up, but the earnings are flattered.** Target posted its best sales growth in five years, Lowe's beat, TJX beat and raised. On the surface, a healthy American shopper. Underneath, tariff refunds added $0.50 per share at Target and $0.34 at Lowe's. *Why it matters:* for the Fed, a resilient consumer makes it easier to keep raising rates, which is bad for stocks. And for you as an analyst, a refund is cash that arrives once and does not come back. That is tonight's lesson.

**Oil fell on surplus expectations, taking energy shares with it.** *Why it matters:* cheaper oil is disinflationary, which in theory helps the Fed's problem. But it arrives through lower energy company profits, which is why a development that is good for the economy showed up today as a 3% loss across the biggest names in the sector. Good macro news and good stock news are not the same thing.

## Today's lesson: earnings quality, or the difference between points you scored and points you can score again

Tonight's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning we read the company's actual financial statements rather than the behavior of its stock price. The concept is **earnings quality**, and today handed us a textbook case.

Start with the jargon, defined once. **EPS** means earnings per share: the company's profit divided by the number of shares, so you can compare a huge company to a small one. When you hear "Target earned $2.65," that is EPS. A **one-time item**, sometimes called non-recurring, is a gain or a cost that hit the quarter but has no reason to repeat — a legal settlement, selling a building, a tax refund. **Earnings quality** is the question of how much of a reported profit comes from the company actually doing its job well, repeatedly, versus from things that happened to it once.

Now today. Target reported $2.65 per share and beat expectations. But $0.50 of that came from tariff refunds — money returned to the company for import duties it had previously paid. That is roughly 19% of the reported profit, and it is not going to arrive again next quarter. Lowe's is the sharper example. It reported $2.98 per share, of which $0.34 was tariff refunds. Take the refunds out and the underlying number is around $2.64 — *less* than the $2.88 it earned in the same quarter a year ago. So Lowe's "beat expectations" and, on the part of the business that actually repeats, went backwards. Both statements are true at the same time. That is why earnings quality is a question you have to ask separately from "did they beat?"

Here is the basketball version. Imagine scouting a guard who averaged 28 points a game. Impressive — until you look closer and find that one night he scored 60 against a team missing both its starting bigs, and his other games were in the low twenties. The 28 average is not a lie; the arithmetic is correct. It is simply not *predictive*, because you cannot schedule a depleted opponent every night. What you actually want to know, if you are deciding whether to build around him, is what he does on a normal Tuesday against a healthy defense. The tariff refund is the 60-point night. The recurring business is the normal Tuesday.

The practical habit to build is small and it will serve you for years: whenever you hear that a company beat expectations, ask *what the profit was made of* before you decide what the beat means. Companies disclose these items, usually in the earnings release and almost always on the conference call, precisely because the rules require it. And notice that the market sometimes agrees with you and sometimes does not — Lowe's barely moved today despite a flattered beat, while TJX rose about 10% on a quarter where adjusted profit merely matched and the revenue outlook was a shade light. The market was paying for TJX's sales momentum and raised guidance, not for the profit line. Reading which part of a report the market actually responded to is the skill that grows out of this one.
