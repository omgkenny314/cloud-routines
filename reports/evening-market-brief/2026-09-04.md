# Market Close Brief — Friday, September 4, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Good economic news turned out to be bad news for stocks. The August jobs report landed at 8:30 this morning and it was a blowout: the economy added 162,000 jobs when forecasters expected around 53,000, roughly three times the estimate and the strongest month since March. On top of that, June and July were both revised upward by a combined 55,000. Normally a strong labor market is something to celebrate. Today it wasn't, because it hands the Federal Reserve room to *raise* interest rates at its meeting on September 15 and 16 — and just yesterday the market had convinced itself the Fed would hold steady. Bond yields jumped, the odds of a September rate hike moved up to roughly 58 to 60 percent from about half the day before, and all three major stock indexes finished lower. The declines were mild, though — none of the three fell even six tenths of one percent. This was a market recalculating, not panicking. Underneath the surface it was a far more dramatic day for individual companies: Lululemon collapsed to an eight-year low, Fair Isaac lost roughly a fifth of its value after a regulator ended its decades-long grip on mortgage credit scores, and memory-chip stocks climbed right through the selling.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: 7,718.60, down 0.38%** (a loss of about 29 points). A broad, orderly pullback as higher expected interest rates make future company profits worth slightly less today.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 53,414.25, down 0.51%** (a loss of 271.86 points). The biggest percentage loser of the three, which is unusual — the Dow's thirty older, established companies normally hold up better on rate-scare days.
- **Nasdaq Composite: 26,506.99, down 0.29%** (a loss of about 77 points). The best of a bad bunch, cushioned by strength in semiconductor stocks.
- **10-year Treasury yield: about 4.79% to 4.80%, up roughly 3 basis points.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so that is a move of about three hundredths. My sources report 4.79% and 4.80%, so treat the last decimal as approximate. The bigger story was the 2-year yield, which tracks what traders expect the Fed to do over the next couple of years: it jumped about 7 basis points to roughly 4.40%, its highest level since January 2025. When the short end moves more than the long end, the bond market is telling you the news changed its mind about *the Fed*, not about the economy's long-run path.
- **Crude oil: around $91 a barrel for U.S. West Texas Intermediate, roughly flat to slightly lower on the day**, with Brent, the international benchmark, near $95. The day was quiet but the week was not — WTI finished up somewhere between 8% and 9%, its strongest week since mid-July, as fighting between the United States and Iran resumed and traffic through the Strait of Hormuz thinned out. I could not pin down an exact official settlement price from two independent sources, so treat the $91 figure as approximate.
- **Bitcoin: fell back below $80,000, down about 2%**, with an intraday low near $78,649. It had actually touched $82,240 early Friday morning — its highest since May — before the jobs report hit. Bitcoin pays no interest, so when bonds suddenly look like they'll pay more, money rotates out of it. Roughly $295 million of leveraged bullish crypto bets were force-closed. One source I found quoted a materially lower late-day price than the others, so I could not verify a precise closing print; the direction and the sub-$80,000 level are well corroborated.

For the week as a whole, using last Friday's closing levels from my August 28 brief: the S&P 500 finished essentially flat (up less than a tenth of a percent), the Dow slipped about a quarter of a percent, and the Nasdaq gained roughly four tenths of a percent. The 10-year yield, though, climbed from about 4.67% to about 4.80% over those five sessions. The stock market moved sideways this week; the bond market did not.

## Big movers and why

**Lululemon Athletica (ticker LULU) fell about 18%, to roughly $100 a share, an eight-year low.** The athletic-apparel company reported Thursday evening and it was ugly in every place that counts. Revenue *fell* 4% from a year ago to $2.4 billion, missing the roughly $2.46 billion Wall Street expected. Comparable sales — sales at stores open at least a year, the cleanest read on whether the actual business is working — dropped 9% to 10% globally and 12% in North America, with core women's leggings down about 20%. The reported profit of $2.92 per share technically beat, but it included a one-time tariff refund, so it flattered the picture. Then management cut full-year guidance for the third time this year, to $10.35 to $10.5 billion in revenue, a decline of 5% to 7%. Their forecast for this quarter was the real shock: 93 to 98 cents of earnings per share against the roughly $2.40 analysts had penciled in. A new chief executive arrives in days and now inherits all of this.

**Fair Isaac (ticker FICO) plunged somewhere between 16% and 21% depending on when you measured it during the session.** I could not confirm the exact closing percentage — reputable sources ranged across that span — but the direction and severity are not in doubt. Fair Isaac builds the FICO credit score. Bill Pulte, director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, announced that "effective immediately" he is instructing Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to approve all lenders to use VantageScore, a competing score. That ends a rule that effectively required government-backed mortgages to use FICO. Pulte accused the company of raising the per-person cost of a credit score by 1,800% since 2020; VantageScore's owners advertise 99 cents against Fair Isaac's $10 or more. Mortgage originations are reported to make up about 71% of Fair Isaac's business-to-business scores revenue, so this is a direct hit to the profitable core. Credit bureaus fell alongside it.

**Tesla (ticker TSLA) dropped about 6%, closing near $374.64.** The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration opened an investigation into Tesla's Cybercab — the two-seat robotaxi with no steering wheel and no pedals — one day after the company began giving rides to members of the public in limited areas of Austin. The stock had surged nearly 7% Thursday on excitement ahead of that launch, so today gave most of it back. That sequence, rally into an event and sell off on the follow-through, is worth filing away.

**SanDisk (ticker SNDK) and Micron Technology (ticker MU) rose while everything else fell** — SanDisk up somewhere between 3% and 8% depending on the source, to around $1,686 a share, and Micron up between 2% and 5%. (The spread across sources here is wide; both clearly finished higher, but I could not verify a single closing figure for either.) Memory chip prices are in a steep upcycle: industry-wide DRAM revenue rose 57% and NAND flash revenue rose 70% quarter over quarter in the most recent period, driven by artificial-intelligence demand. That is today's lesson, below.

**Guidewire Software (ticker GWRE) fell about 22%.** The insurance-software company beat on both revenue and profit for its finished quarter, then forecast weaker-than-expected revenue for the quarter ahead. Beat the past, miss the future, get punished — the market pays for what's coming.

**Zscaler (ticker ZS) fell about 4%, to roughly $170.25.** The cybersecurity firm also beat, with $1.19 in adjusted earnings per share against $1.09 expected and $898 million in revenue against $877 million. But its guidance implies about 17% revenue growth next fiscal year, down from 25% this year. Investors in fast-growing software companies pay for the growth rate, not the beat.

**DocuSign (ticker DOCU)** reported strong results and raised its full-year revenue outlook. I could not verify where the stock actually closed, so I'm noting the news without the number.

## Headlines that matter

**The August jobs report was the whole day.** Payrolls grew 162,000 against about 53,000 expected. The unemployment rate held at 4.1%, with 7.0 million people unemployed. Revisions added 55,000 jobs to June and July combined — July went from a reported loss of 23,000 jobs to a gain of 21,000, which meaningfully rewrites the summer's story. The labor force participation rate ticked up to 61.6%. *Why it matters:* the Fed has two legal jobs, maximum employment and stable prices. When employment is clearly fine, the Fed is freed to focus entirely on inflation — and right now that means it can consider tightening rather than easing. Every asset that benefits from cheap money reprices when that becomes more likely.

**One detail inside the report cuts the other way: wages are still losing to inflation.** Average hourly earnings rose 10 cents, or 0.3%, to $37.75, which is up 3.1% over the past year. Headline inflation was recently running at 3.7%. *Why it matters:* the typical worker's paycheck is buying slightly less than it did a year ago. A hot jobs number and a squeezed consumer can coexist, and if you want to understand why a company like Lululemon is watching its customers trade down, this is the number to look at.

**Rate-hike odds jumped, and the September Fed meeting is now genuinely live.** Futures traders moved to roughly a 58% to 60% probability of a quarter-point hike on September 16, up from about 49% to 52% the day before. Context: the Fed has held rates steady at five straight meetings this year. On Thursday, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said he would support holding rates if disinflation continues, but added that "it may not take much acceleration in inflation to nudge me into supporting tighter policy," and said his decision would be "heavily influenced" by next week's inflation data. Inflation is meaningfully above the Fed's 2% target — 3.7% headline and 3.3% core in July — though the Fed's preferred three-month measure has fallen from 4.76% in February to about 3.05% now. *Why it matters:* a rate *hike* in 2026 would be a real regime change after a long stretch of markets assuming the next move was down. That is why a 162,000 jobs print moved bond yields as much as it did.

**The United States and Iran resumed striking each other this week, and energy markets noticed.** Vice President JD Vance said Thursday that Washington will not hold talks with Tehran unless Iran stops attacking commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz. Transits through the Strait fell to six vessels Wednesday from eleven Tuesday, against a ten-day average of nearly thirteen. Diesel prices hit a record high. *Why it matters:* roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes through that waterway. Oil at $91 feeds directly into inflation, which feeds directly into the Fed decision described above. Today's two big stories are not separate stories.

## After the bell

It is quiet, and honestly it was always going to be. Friday evenings are the emptiest slot on the earnings calendar — companies almost never report into a weekend, because there is no trading session to absorb the news. **I could not verify any post-close earnings releases or meaningful after-hours moves as of roughly half an hour after the bell, and I found no major companies scheduled to report tonight.** If something does cross later this evening, it will be an outlier rather than a scheduled event.

Two things worth knowing about the pause ahead: the Federal Reserve's communications blackout period begins Saturday, September 5 and runs through Thursday, September 17, so no more Fed speeches to move markets until after the decision. And Monday, September 7 is Labor Day — U.S. stock and bond markets are closed. The next trading session is Tuesday.

## Today's lesson: relative strength, or what it means when a stock goes up on a down day

Today the whole market fell. The S&P 500 lost 0.38%, and the news of the day — higher interest rates coming — is specifically the kind of news that hurts fast-growing technology companies most, because their value depends on profits far in the future, and higher rates shrink what future money is worth today. Semiconductor stocks should have been among the worst hit.

SanDisk and Micron went up anyway. **Relative strength** is exactly that: a stock rising when its group and the broader market are falling, or falling less than everything around it. It is a technical concept — meaning it comes from reading price behavior rather than from reading financial statements — and it is one of the few technical signals with an obvious, common-sense meaning. When a stock climbs into a headwind, buyers are showing up who care more about something specific to that company than they do about the thing scaring everyone else.

Here the specific thing is the memory-chip pricing cycle. Industry DRAM revenue rose 57% in a single quarter and NAND flash revenue rose 70%, because artificial-intelligence systems consume enormous amounts of memory. Buyers today decided that a supply shortage driving prices up 60 or 70 percent a quarter matters more to SanDisk's next two years than a quarter-point move in the federal funds rate. They may be right or wrong. But the *behavior* is information, and you got it for free just by noticing which direction the stock went on a red day.

The basketball version: your team is down fifteen and getting outplayed everywhere, but one guy is 8-for-10 and drawing double teams. The scoreboard says the team is losing. That player's line says something different is happening in his individual matchup, and it's worth watching whether it holds up when the game turns. Same logic. Two cautions, though. One down day proves nothing — relative strength is only meaningful when it persists across multiple sessions, which is why analysts track it over weeks rather than hours. And it works in reverse, which is arguably the more useful direction: a stock that *falls* on a strong market day is flashing the same signal in red. Lululemon has now spent months underperforming a rising market, and today it hit an eight-year low. That weakness was visible long before this week's earnings report explained it.
