# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, September 22, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a split-personality market, the kind that looks boring on the surface and is anything but underneath. The S&P 500 finished essentially unchanged — down six one-hundredths of a point, which is as close to perfectly flat as a day gets — while the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about a third of a percent and the Nasdaq Composite rose about half a percent to a second straight record close. Underneath that flat headline, money kept moving in one direction: out of oil-linked and health-care names, into chips and artificial-intelligence-related technology. The single biggest storyline was oil. Crude fell for a fifth straight day after President Trump said U.S. officials met with an Iranian delegation for three hours on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly in New York, and after reports that Saudi Arabia is restarting its East-West pipeline. Cheaper oil is a gift to the Nasdaq and a problem for the energy companies that make up a meaningful chunk of the Dow, so the two indexes went opposite directions on the same piece of news.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: 7,764.64, down 0.06 points — call it flat (0.00%).** A rare dead-even close. Gains in technology were offset almost exactly by losses in energy and health care.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 51,863.69, down 185.14 points (-0.36%).** The Dow holds big oil companies and large health-care names, and both groups were on the wrong side of today's news.

- **Nasdaq Composite: 27,244.28, up 122.18 points (+0.45%).** A record closing high, its second in a row, on continued strength in semiconductor and AI-related stocks. It also set a record intraday high of 27,250.92.

- **10-year Treasury yield: around 4.95%, down roughly 1 basis point.** (A basis point is one one-hundredth of a percentage point.) Yields drifted slightly lower as traders waited on Fed speakers and jobs data. I could only confirm this from one source and as an intraday reading, not a verified closing level.

- **Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate settled at $94.59, down 1.2%.** Brent, the international benchmark, settled at $99.25, down about 1%. Fifth consecutive daily decline, on U.S.-Iran talks and the Saudi pipeline restart. Even after the slide, crude is still up more than 9% for the month.

- **Bitcoin: holding around $86,000.** It opened Tuesday near $86,600 and traded in the mid-$86,000s through the morning, near its highest levels since January. I could not verify a precise price as of the 4:00 p.m. Eastern stock market close, so treat that $86,000 figure as "about where it was all day," not a confirmed closing print.

## Big movers and why

**Viking Therapeutics (VKTX)** was the day's standout, closing near $40.90 after gaining roughly 31%. The company released maintenance-study results for VK2735, its experimental obesity drug. The headline number was 22% placebo-adjusted weight loss at 33 weeks on the 17.5 milligram dose. The more interesting number for investors was what happened when patients stepped down their dosing: those who moved from weekly to every-other-week injections kept up to 97% of the weight they had lost, and monthly dosers kept up to 90%. One analyst at Truist called it a "best-case scenario." Less frequent dosing is a commercial question as much as a medical one — it changes how much drug a company has to manufacture and how likely patients are to stay on it.

**Labcorp Holdings (LH)** and **Quest Diagnostics (DGX)** both fell sharply. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, the federal agency that sets Medicare payment rates, published preliminary rates for laboratory services in 2027 that come in about 15% below 2026 levels. CMS argued, using data from more than 6,400 labs, that Medicare has been overpaying labs by roughly 16% versus what private insurers pay, and put the taxpayer savings at about $1 billion a year. In premarket trading Labcorp was down about 5.9% and Quest about 6.9%; I was able to confirm those premarket figures but not the exact closing percentages. Labs have roughly 30 days to comment, with final rates due in November — so this hangs over both names for a while.

**Shopify (SHOP)** rose roughly 7% to 8% on a pair of analyst calls: Piper Sandler raised its price target to $180 with an Overweight rating, and Bernstein started coverage at Outperform with a $160 target. Both arguments rest on AI tools making it cheaper and easier for new merchants to open a store, which widens the pool of customers Shopify can sell to. One report also tied the move to an AI partnership with Meta Platforms (META); I could not independently confirm the terms of that deal.

**SoFi Technologies (SOFI)** gained about 4.6% after becoming the first national bank to go live with stablecoin settlement across Mastercard's global payments network, migrating its full $25 billion card program to blockchain-based settlement.

**GameStop (GME)** added about 4% after a regulatory filing showed chief executive Ryan Cohen bought 1,150,680 shares on Monday for roughly $26.4 million, pushing his personal stake past 40 million shares.

**AutoZone (AZO)** rose after its fiscal fourth-quarter report — more on that below, because it is today's lesson.

**New York Times Company (NYT)** was one of the day's worst performers, trading as low as $63.52 and now down more than 24% from its high for the year. There was no single fresh catalyst I could confirm; the pressure appears to be a continuation of concern about slowing digital subscriber additions — 280,000 net adds in the second quarter, down from 310,000 in the first and well below the 450,000-plus quarterly pace of late 2025. I saw one count putting today's drop near 8.6%, but I could not confirm that figure against a second source.

**Energy stocks** were broadly weak across the day as crude slid, which is the mechanical reason the Dow lagged. I could not verify individual closing percentages for the big oil names today.

## Headlines that matter

**The U.S. met with Iran for three hours.** President Trump said American officials sat down with an Iranian delegation on the sidelines of the U.N. General Assembly. Why it matters: this year's conflict and the disruption around the Strait of Hormuz are the reason oil has been elevated all month. Every signal that diplomacy is live takes some of the "something terrible might happen" premium out of the oil price. Lower oil flows into lower inflation expectations, which flows into lower bond yields, which supports the stocks investors value most on far-off future profits — technology. That is the chain reaction you watched today.

**Saudi Arabia is restarting its East-West pipeline**, with Riyadh aiming for meaningful flows by Saturday. Why it matters: that pipeline lets Saudi crude reach the Red Sea without passing through the Strait of Hormuz. It is a physical workaround to the chokepoint that has been scaring the market, and physical supply relief is more durable than a headline about talks.

**The Federal Reserve is in a hiking cycle, and the market is still handicapping the next move.** The Fed raised its target range by a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00% at its September meeting — its first increase since 2023 — under Chair Kevin Warsh, citing inflation that remains above the 2% target. The updated projections showed 16 of 18 officials seeing at least one more quarter-point increase this year, with four penciling in two. Today brought a run of Fed commentary: New York Fed President John Williams, Governor Philip Jefferson, and Richmond Fed President Thomas Barkin all spoke. Why it matters: when the central bank is tightening rather than easing, every piece of inflation and jobs data gets read as a vote for or against the next hike. That is why yields barely moved today — nobody heard anything decisive.

**ADP's weekly employment pulse showed the four-week average of private job gains rising to about 20,000 a week**, up from roughly 16,750, with hiring accelerating for a third straight week. Why it matters: with the Fed worried about inflation, a labor market that is firming rather than cracking makes it easier for the hawks on the committee to argue for another increase.

**CMS proposed cutting lab reimbursement 15% for 2027.** Why it matters beyond Labcorp and Quest: it is a reminder that for large parts of health care, the government is not a regulator off to the side — it is the customer, and it sets the price. A company whose revenue depends on a federal fee schedule has a risk that no amount of good management removes.

**U.S.-China summit preparation continued** ahead of President Trump's Washington meeting with President Xi, with trade, Taiwan, artificial intelligence and rare earth minerals on the table. Why it matters: semiconductors sit at the center of that negotiation, and the chip trade has been the engine of this Nasdaq rally.

## After the bell

It is early — the closing bell rang about half an hour ago — so here is what is out so far.

**KB Home (KBH)**, the homebuilder, reported its fiscal third quarter (ended August 31) at about 4:10 p.m. Eastern. Earnings came in at $1.05 per diluted share against analyst expectations near $0.90, on revenue of $1.30 billion. But the beat sits on top of a genuinely weak quarter: revenue fell 20%, homes delivered dropped 19% to 2,732, and net income fell to $65.3 million from $109.8 million a year ago. Average selling price was roughly flat at about $473,000. The company repurchased $50 million of stock and said plainly that "the housing market continues to be challenging, with conditions weakening since our June earnings report," pointing to higher mortgage rates pressuring affordability. Shares were up about 0.3% at $47.99 in early after-hours trade — a beat that the market is treating as no great news, which tells you something about how investors read the guidance. The conference call began at 5:00 p.m. Eastern, so the stock's reaction may look different by tonight.

**Worthington Enterprises (WOR)** was also scheduled to report its fiscal first quarter after the close, with analysts looking for about $0.75 per share on roughly $331 million in revenue. Its conference call is set for tomorrow, September 23. I could not verify actual results as of this writing.

**Spire Global (SPIR)** was on the after-hours watch list as well. No results confirmed yet.

**AutoZone (AZO)** reported this morning rather than tonight, but it is the most substantive report of the day and it is worth carrying into the evening: fiscal fourth-quarter earnings of $56.05 per share versus the $53.98 analysts expected, on revenue of $6.595 billion, up 5.6% but slightly short of the roughly $6.68 billion Wall Street wanted. Same-store sales rose 2.7% overall — domestic up 1.6%, international up 10.7%. Gross margin expanded 182 basis points to 53.3%.

Then the bigger one: S&P Global's flash purchasing managers' indexes at 9:45 a.m. Eastern. A PMI is a monthly survey of purchasing managers; readings above 50 mean the sector is expanding, below 50 means contracting. Expectations are around 56.8 for services and 53.2 for manufacturing. The flash version is an early estimate released before the final number, which is why it moves markets.

## Today's lesson: earnings quality, or asking where the beat came from

Here is a number from today: AutoZone earned $56.05 per share when Wall Street expected $53.98. That is a beat of about $2 a share, roughly 4%. If that is all you read, you would conclude the company had a strong quarter.

Now here is the second number. AutoZone's gross margin — the share of each sales dollar left after paying for the merchandise itself, which we covered on September 18 — expanded by 182 basis points to 53.3%. Of that 182 basis points of improvement, about 145 came from tariff refunds. Money the company had previously paid in import duties and got back. That is real cash and it is perfectly legitimate, but it is not something that happens every quarter, and it has nothing to do with selling more brake pads. Strip it out and the underlying margin improvement was closer to 37 basis points. Meanwhile revenue came in *below* expectations, and domestic same-store sales — sales at locations open at least a year, which strips out the boost from simply opening new stores — grew just 1.6%.

That gap is what analysts mean by **earnings quality**. It is not a question of whether the numbers are honest; AutoZone disclosed the tariff refund plainly. It is a question of whether the profit is *repeatable*. High-quality earnings come from the core business doing what it does, over and over. Low-quality earnings come from one-time items: a tax settlement, an asset sale, an insurance payout, a legal win, a tariff refund. A company can beat estimates on those for a quarter, but you cannot build a forecast on them, because next quarter they are gone.

Think of it as a box score. A player drops 32 points and everyone sees the headline. Then you look closer: 18 of those came from the free-throw line in a game the referees called unusually tight. He is still a good player, and the 32 points still went on the board — but if you are projecting what he does next game, you should be paying attention to the 14 he scored in the run of play, not the 18 the whistle handed him. Earnings quality is the habit of always finding the free throws.

The practical version, for any earnings report you read: don't stop at "beat" or "miss." Ask three questions. Did revenue grow, or only profit? If only profit, what made margins better — is it something the company *did*, like pricing power or cost discipline, or something that *happened to it*? And is the same-store or organic number — growth excluding new locations and acquisitions — holding up? AutoZone's answers today were: revenue slightly short, margin mostly from a refund, same-store growth modest at home and strong abroad. The stock rose anyway. Both of those things can be true at the same time, and noticing the difference is the whole skill.
