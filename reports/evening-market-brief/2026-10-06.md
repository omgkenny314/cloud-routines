# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, October 6, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a quietly strong day that ended with a milestone: the S&P 500 closed at a record high for the first time since August, and it did so without any drama. Two pressures that have been leaning on this market all autumn — high bond yields and expensive oil — both eased at the same time, and that gave stocks room to climb. But the single biggest storyline was a deal. Alphabet, the parent company of Google, signed a 20-year agreement to buy nuclear power from Constellation Energy, and that one contract sent Constellation's stock up roughly 13% and lifted essentially every nuclear-power company in the market with it. This is the clearest example yet of a theme that has been building for two years: the artificial-intelligence boom is no longer only a technology story. Data centers need enormous amounts of electricity, that electricity has to come from somewhere, and the companies that can supply it are now being valued as AI companies in their own right. Chip stocks did their part too, with Nvidia closing at another record. The result was a broad, orderly advance with all three major indexes higher.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: 7,818.93, up 44.98 points, or 0.58%.** A record close and the index's first close ever above 7,800, edging past the previous record of roughly 7,816.70 set in August. The drivers were falling Treasury yields and strength in large technology names.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 51,521.28, up 253.38 points, or 0.49%.** A solid gain, though the Dow holds only 30 companies and carries less technology than the other two indexes, so it tends to lag on days like this.
- **Nasdaq Composite: 27,599.79, up 122.48 points, or 0.45%.** Also a record close — its second in a row. Nvidia and Broadcom did much of the lifting.
- **10-year Treasury yield: about 5.27%, down roughly 5 basis points.** A **basis point** is one hundredth of a percentage point, so this is a drop of about 0.05%. The yield had reached about 5.33% yesterday, its highest since 2002. Today's retreat came as traders continued to scale back bets on another Federal Reserve rate increase. Falling yields are the quiet reason stocks could rally.
- **Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate steadied near $89 a barrel; Brent, the international benchmark, finished near $100.** I want to be straight with you here: my sources disagreed on the exact settlement, ranging from about $87.99 to $89.62 for WTI, and even disagreed on whether the day finished slightly up or slightly down. What is clear and consistent across all of them is that oil had just dropped about 3.7% over the prior two sessions and essentially stopped falling today. The cause was a steep price cut by Saudi Arabia (more below).
- **Bitcoin: roughly $85,500 to $86,200, close to flat on the day.** It pushed above $86,000 twice and reached about $86,450 before fading back. Bitcoin trades 24 hours a day, so there is no official closing price — treat these as approximate. More than $172 million in leveraged positions were liquidated over 24 hours and U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs saw net outflows, which capped the rallies.
- **Gold: about $4,121 an ounce, down roughly 0.44%.** A mild pullback on a day when investors were comfortable owning stocks.

## Big movers and why

**Constellation Energy (CEG), up roughly 13%.** The day's headline move. Alphabet (GOOGL), Google's parent, signed a 20-year power purchase agreement with Constellation to bring 890 megawatts of nuclear capacity onto the PJM grid, which serves the mid-Atlantic and parts of the Midwest. Reported at about $4.3 billion, the money funds upgrades to *existing* reactors — modernized turbines, steam generators and digital control systems — rather than new construction. That distinction matters: the capacity arrives years sooner and at far lower risk than building a plant from scratch. One honest caveat on the number: my sources put the gain at 12%, 13.5% and 15%, so call it "about 13%" rather than a precise figure. Sources also differed on whether the upgrades cover six plants or eleven reactor units; those can both be true, since one plant can hold several reactors, but I could not confirm which framing is right.

**The rest of the nuclear sector went along for the ride.** Vistra (VST) rose about 8%, Talen Energy (TLN) about 7%, Oklo (OKLO) about 8%, NANO Nuclear Energy (NNE) about 8%, and NuScale Power (SMR) about 7%. None of these companies signed anything today. They rose because one large, credit-worthy technology buyer just demonstrated it will sign a 20-year contract for nuclear electricity, and investors reasoned that if Google will do it, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta might too.

**Nvidia (NVDA), up 2.06% to a record close.** The chipmaker's market value reached roughly $5.7 trillion, within about $300 billion of $6 trillion, and the stock is up about 28% this year. Broadcom (AVGO) rose 2.09% alongside it, and Advanced Micro Devices (AMD) gained close to 3%, also at an all-time high.

**But the chip sector was split, not uniformly strong.** The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index — the main index tracking chip stocks — rose only 0.27%, far less than Nvidia alone. Intel (INTC) fell 2.60% and Qualcomm (QCOM) fell 2.31%. That gap is worth noticing: money is flowing toward the companies selling into AI data centers and away from those tied to traditional computers and phones. "Chip stocks rose" would be a misleading summary of today.

**Illumina (ILMN), down about 5.9%, and Moderna (MRNA), down about 6.1%.** Both fell after hitting fresh 52-week highs in recent sessions — Illumina on an earnings recovery and analyst target increases, Moderna on cancer-vaccine optimism and news it will rejoin the Nasdaq-100 index before the open on Friday, October 9. Neither drop appears to be driven by bad news. This is what traders call profit-taking: after a stock runs hard, some holders sell simply to lock in the gain, and that selling alone can push the price down for a day or two.

**Constellation Brands (STZ), up 2.43% to $116.06.** The beer and wine company (no relation to Constellation Energy, despite the shared name) rose into the close ahead of its earnings report after the bell. See below.

## Headlines that matter

**The Federal Reserve is the backdrop behind everything in this brief.** On September 16 the Fed raised its target interest rate a quarter point to a range of 3.75% to 4.00% — a unanimous 12–0 vote and its first increase since 2023. *Why it matters:* the Fed is in a tightening cycle, raising rates to fight inflation rather than cutting them to support growth. Higher interest rates hurt stocks two ways at once. They make safe government bonds a more attractive alternative to owning stocks, and they raise the borrowing costs that squeeze company profits. Every day this autumn, the market has essentially been asking one question: is another hike coming?

**Today the answer kept moving toward "probably not," and that is why yields fell.** Traders now put the odds of a rate increase at the October meeting at roughly 17% to 18%, down from about 36% a week ago. Two data points caused that shift: the personal consumption expenditures price index — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — showed core prices up 3% in August, meaningfully cooler than the 3.3% expected; and the September jobs report showed the economy added only 29,000 jobs against expectations of more than 80,000. *Why it matters:* cooler inflation removes the reason to hike, and weak hiring adds a reason not to. When the market lowers its expectation of future rates, bond yields fall, and stocks rise on the relief.

**Saudi Arabia cut its oil prices sharply, and that is bullish for stocks even though it sounds like energy news.** Saudi Aramco lowered the price of Arab Light crude for Asian buyers to $5 a barrel below the regional benchmark for November — a six-year low, compared with a $2 discount this month. Separately, G7 countries agreed to release 100 million barrels of diesel and crude from emergency reserves, and OPEC+ left November production targets unchanged. *Why it matters:* a producer cutting prices that aggressively is competing for market share, which signals that physical oil supply is becoming more plentiful. Cheaper energy feeds into lower inflation, lower inflation means less pressure on the Fed, and less pressure on the Fed means lower rates. That is the chain that connects a Saudi price list to a record close on the S&P 500.

**The counterweight: attacks linked to Yemen's Houthi forces kept traders cautious about Gulf shipping.** *Why it matters:* a large share of the world's seaborne oil moves through the Strait of Hormuz. As long as that route carries risk, there is a premium built into the oil price that can return quickly. Today's calm in oil is real but not guaranteed to last.

## After the bell

It is early in the after-hours session. Here is what has landed so far.

**Constellation Brands (STZ) reported fiscal second-quarter results, and they are more interesting than the headline suggests.** On the surface it was a clear beat: revenue of $2.63 billion against about $2.54 billion expected, and adjusted earnings of $3.74 a share against roughly $3.55 expected. The company reaffirmed its full-year forecast of $11.20 to $11.90 in adjusted earnings per share, and its board declared a quarterly dividend of $1.03 a share payable November 13.

Underneath, though, is a number worth learning to look for. Beer revenue rose 5% to $2.47 billion and **shipments** — beer sold by Constellation to its distributors — jumped 5.5%. But **depletions** — beer actually sold by those distributors to bars, restaurants and stores — *fell* 0.6%. Modelo Especial depletions fell 2% and Corona Extra fell 5%, and those are the company's two flagship brands. Beer operating margin contracted 160 basis points to 39.0%. In plain terms: the company sold more beer into the pipeline than came out the other end. I was not able to verify the stock's after-hours price reaction at the time of writing, so I am not going to guess at it.

**Also scheduled to report after today's close:** Penguin Solutions (PENG), Neogen (NEOG), Worthington Steel (WS), Saratoga Investment (SAR) and AXIL Brands (AXIL). Results for those had not crossed when I wrote this. Constellation Brands holds its earnings call tomorrow morning rather than tonight, which is unusual and means management has not yet been questioned about the depletions number.

Minutes are the detailed written account of what officials actually argued about, published three weeks after each meeting. September was one of the four meetings a year that includes the "dot plot" — each official's private forecast for where rates are headed — so these minutes should reveal how much disagreement sat behind that unanimous 12–0 vote. ## Today's lesson: contracted revenue, or why a 20-year signature is worth 13%

Today's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning the study of the business itself rather than the behavior of its stock price. And Constellation Energy handed us a nearly perfect example.

Here is the puzzle worth sitting with. Constellation did not invent anything today. It did not discover a new power source, acquire a company, or report better profits. Its reactors will produce exactly the same electricity tomorrow that they produced yesterday. All that happened is that Google agreed, in writing, to buy some of that electricity for the next twenty years. And the market decided the company was worth about 13% more than it was the day before. Why should a signature be worth that much?

The answer is **revenue visibility** — how confidently you can predict what a company will earn in the future. Most of a power company's electricity is sold into what is called the **merchant market**: it goes onto the grid at whatever the going price happens to be that hour, and that price swings with weather, fuel costs and demand. Merchant revenue is real revenue, but it is unpredictable revenue. A **power purchase agreement**, or PPA, is the opposite: a specific buyer commits to a specific volume at a specific price for a specific number of years. Today's deal converts a slice of Constellation's output from "whatever the market pays" into "890 megawatts, this price, until 2046, signed by one of the most creditworthy companies on earth."

That matters because of how stocks get valued. The value of a business is the cash it will generate in the future, discounted back to what that cash is worth today — and the word *discounted* is doing real work. Uncertain future cash is worth less than certain future cash, so investors apply a bigger haircut to it. Make the cash more certain and you shrink the haircut, which raises the value, even though the number of dollars never changed. This is why investors will pay a higher multiple of earnings for a subscription software company than for a company selling the same amount of goods one order at a time. Same revenue, different reliability. It also explains why the entire nuclear sector rose today: the deal did not just help Constellation, it proved that this kind of contract is available to anyone who owns a reactor.

The basketball version: imagine two players who both averaged 20 points a game last season. One has a guaranteed four-year contract. The other is on a non-guaranteed one-year deal and has to re-earn his spot every October. The on-court production is identical. The financial certainty is not even close, and any front office building a payroll around them would treat them completely differently. Twenty years of guaranteed revenue is the guaranteed contract.

One caution before you go hunting for this pattern. Contracted revenue cuts both ways. Locking in a price for twenty years means you are protected if electricity prices collapse — and you have given away the upside if they soar. A long-term contract removes risk in both directions, and whether that is a good trade depends entirely on the price that was locked in, which outside investors rarely get to see in full. The skill worth building is not "contracts are good." It is learning to ask, every time you look at a company's revenue, how much of it is promised and how much of it has to be won again next quarter.
