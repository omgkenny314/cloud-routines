# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, September 29, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

A very quiet day at the index level hiding a genuinely violent day inside it. All three major indexes finished slightly lower — the S&P 500 down just 0.16%, the Nasdaq down less than a tenth of a percent — and all three closed well above their worst levels of the session. That is the second straight losing day, but barely. Underneath that calm surface, one company lost more than a quarter of its value in a single session: Fair Isaac (FICO), the company that makes the credit score nearly every American mortgage is priced off of, fell about 27% after a federal regulator ended its decades-long lock on mortgage credit scoring. That is the single biggest storyline of the day, and it is a rare thing — not an earnings miss, not a bad forecast, but a government decision that changed what a business is in one morning. The day's second story is the one that has run all month: the bond market. The 30-year Treasury yield pushed above 5.6% intraday, its highest since June 2002. And the third is a genuinely worrying data point — consumer confidence fell to its lowest level in more than twelve years, with Americans pointing directly at prices and gas costs. Stocks shrugged all of that off to close nearly flat, which tells you something in itself.

## Big movers and why

- **Fair Isaac (FICO), down 27.07% to close at $613.26.** This is the day. Fair Isaac makes the FICO score. The Federal Housing Finance Agency, which oversees the mortgage giants Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, directed them to accept VantageScore 4.0 alongside FICO Classic — and, critically, to price mortgages off a *single* grid that treats both scores equally. The price difference is the whole story: a VantageScore costs roughly $0.99, a FICO score roughly $10. For years FICO was the only score Fannie and Freddie would price off of, which is why Fair Isaac could roughly double its prices since 2020 and make it stick. That is over. The stock opened at $668.08 after closing Monday at $840.89 — meaning it never traded between those two prices — and it was the worst performer in the S&P 500 on its steepest one-day percentage decline in more than six years. It is now down roughly 65% from its 2025 peak.
- **Equifax (EFX), down about 3.95%; TransUnion (TRU), down about 2.49%; Experian, down roughly 1.5% to 2% in its U.S.-listed shares.** Here is the wrinkle worth noticing: these three credit bureaus jointly *own* VantageScore. They just won. And they fell anyway. The reason is that FHFA Director Bill Pulte paired the announcement with sharp public criticism of the bureaus and said the agency is "seriously considering" moving to bi-merge credit reporting — using two credit reports instead of three on a mortgage application. If lenders buy two reports instead of three, one bureau gets cut out of every loan. The market decided that threat outweighed the VantageScore win.
- **Carnival (CCL), up about 11.4%.** The cruise operator reported fiscal third-quarter adjusted earnings of $1.43 a share on record revenue of $8.43 billion, and raised its full-year adjusted earnings forecast to $2.24 a share. It absorbed $131 million in adverse fuel and currency effects and still beat. The forward-looking numbers were the stronger part: 2027 booked occupancy and pricing at record levels, and record third-quarter customer deposits up nearly 7% on flat capacity. Shares were up as much as 14% intraday, the most since April 8.
- **CarMax (KMX), up more than 6%.** The used-car retailer earned $1.16 a share in its fiscal second quarter against expectations near $0.73, on revenue of about $7.9 billion versus roughly $7.1 billion expected. Comparable used-vehicle sales grew more than anyone expected — that is the number that matters for a retailer, because it strips out the effect of simply having more stores.
- **Summit Therapeutics, up about 18%.** AstraZeneca is investing $2 billion in the biotech to collaborate on a cancer drug that could be paired with AstraZeneca's other treatments. For a company at Summit's stage, a large partner writing a check that size is validation from someone who did the scientific due diligence.

## Headlines that matter

**Consumer confidence fell to a more-than-12-year low.** The Conference Board's index dropped 6.7 points to 81.9, badly missing the 89.2 that forecasters expected. Both halves fell: the Present Situation Index, which asks how things are right now, fell 7.9 points to 109.3, and the Expectations Index, which asks about the next six months, fell 5.9 points to 63.6 — its third consecutive monthly decline. Net views of current business conditions turned negative for the first time since September 2024, and when asked what was on their minds, respondents pointed to prices, the cost of goods and services, and gas prices in particular. *Why it matters:* consumer spending is roughly two-thirds of the U.S. economy. Confidence surveys are imperfect predictors of actual spending — people often complain and shop anyway — but the composition here is the concerning part. An Expectations reading in the low 60s has historically been associated with recession risk. And note the mechanism: this is September's fuel-price spike showing up in household sentiment. The oil story and the consumer story are now the same story.

**The 30-year Treasury yield hit its highest level since June 2002.** It traded above 5.6% intraday before settling near 5.56%. The 10-year, meanwhile, barely moved. *Why it matters:* when long-term yields rise faster than short-term ones, that tells you the bond market's concern is not about the next Federal Reserve meeting — it is about the next decade. Investors demand more compensation to lend for 30 years when they fear persistent inflation and a very heavy volume of new government borrowing. The 30-year also sets the tone for mortgage rates. A household shopping for a home loan feels this number before it feels anything the Fed does this month.

**The FHFA ended FICO's mortgage-scoring monopoly.** Covered above as a stock move, but it matters beyond Fair Isaac's shareholders. *Why it matters:* two reasons. First, for the housing market — cheaper scoring and a second accepted scorecard could modestly lower closing costs and may qualify some borrowers who score better under VantageScore's methodology. Second, as an investing lesson: when a company's profits depend on a rule, the rule is part of the business. Regulatory permission can be the most valuable asset a company owns and the only one that can disappear overnight without warning.

**U.S.–Iran diplomacy stalled again.** The two sides held separate talks with mediators, Iran publicly denied direct talks were happening, and Iranian officials signaled pessimism about any deal before the U.S. midterm elections. The seven-month conflict continues to keep the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes through — in question. *Why it matters:* this is the chain that connects a Middle East headline to your grocery bill. Hormuz uncertainty keeps a risk premium in the oil price, expensive fuel feeds inflation, inflation keeps the Fed leaning toward tightening, and higher rates pressure stocks. Every link in that chain was visible in today's data.

**Fed context.** For background on why all of the above lands harder than it would have a year ago: on September 16 the Fed raised its target rate a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00% in a unanimous 12–0 vote, its first increase since 2023. Chair Kevin Warsh has said underlying inflation is not slowing, and 16 of 18 officials see at least one more quarter-point hike this year. *Why it matters:* the Fed is already inclined to tighten. Weak consumer confidence would normally argue for easier policy — but not when the reason confidence is weak is that prices are too high.

## After the bell

It is early in the after-hours session. Here is what has landed so far.

**Concentrix (CNXC), the customer-experience and business-services company, reported fiscal third-quarter results for the quarter ended August 31.** On the adjusted numbers it beat: non-GAAP diluted earnings of $2.92 a share, up 5.0% from $2.78 a year ago and ahead of the roughly $2.81 analysts expected. Adjusted EBITDA — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough proxy for operating cash generation — was $363.0 million, a 14.8% margin versus 14.5% last year. The company generated record third-quarter free cash flow of $268 million from operations, $218 million on an adjusted basis, and raised its quarterly dividend. Revenue, though, declined 1.2% to $2,453.7 million from $2,483.3 million.

Now the number that will get attention: on a GAAP basis — that is, the official accounting standard rather than the company's adjusted presentation — Concentrix reported a **loss** of $16.24 a share, against a $1.34 profit a year earlier. The cause is a $1.05 billion non-cash goodwill impairment charge. Goodwill is the premium a company paid above the tangible value of businesses it acquired; when the acquired business is no longer judged to be worth what was paid, accounting rules require writing that premium down. What makes this one striking is the stated trigger: the company cited its own stock price range and market capitalization as the primary reason for the writedown. In other words, the market's low opinion of the company became the accounting evidence that the company was worth less. No cash left the building, but that is a $1.05 billion admission.

## Today's lesson: moving average crossovers, and the "death cross"

Today's lesson is a technical one — technical analysis meaning the study of price and trading behavior itself, rather than the business underneath. Fair Isaac handed us the example, and it builds directly on something we covered earlier this month.

We talked before about the **200-day moving average**: the average closing price over the last 200 trading days, recalculated daily, which smooths out noise and shows the trend a stock has actually been in. Today's idea is what happens when you put *two* moving averages on the same chart — a fast one and a slow one — and watch where they cross.

The standard pair is the 50-day and the 200-day. The 50-day average covers about ten weeks and reacts fairly quickly; the 200-day covers about ten months and reacts slowly. When a stock has been rising, the fast average sits above the slow one, because recent prices are higher than older prices. When a stock has been falling for a sustained stretch, the fast average eventually drops *below* the slow one. That downward crossing is called a **death cross**. The opposite — the 50-day rising up through the 200-day — is a **golden cross**. The names are melodramatic; the arithmetic is not.

Here is the part worth actually understanding. A death cross is not a prediction. It is a piece of arithmetic that can only happen *after* a decline has already lasted long enough to drag ten weeks of average price below ten months of average price. It is confirmation, not warning. Fair Isaac is the clean illustration: the stock is down roughly 65% from its 2025 high, and today it closed at $613.26 after breaking decisively below roughly $876 — a level that had held as the low back in April. Both its 50-day and 200-day averages now sit hundreds of dollars above where the stock trades. Analysts flagged the crossover today, though I could not verify the exact average levels; sources I reached gave conflicting figures, so take the concept from this and not the decimals. What the crossover tells you is simply this: this is no longer a stock having a bad stretch inside an uptrend. The trend itself has changed.

The basketball version: a death cross is like a team's ten-game record finally dropping below its season-long record. That crossing does not cause anything — by the time it happens, everyone watching already knew the team was struggling. But it does mark the moment the slump stopped being a slump and became who the team is this year. The useful question a death cross raises is never "what happens next?" It is "has something structural changed, or is this noise?" For Fair Isaac, today answered it: a regulator removed the exclusive arrangement the company's pricing power rested on. The chart is not telling you the future. It is telling you that the story the price had been following for years is over, and a different one has started.

One caution, because this is where people get hurt with technical signals. Moving average crossovers are slow by construction, and they generate plenty of false alarms in choppy markets — a stock can cross down, cross back up, and cross down again over a few months while going nowhere. They are a way of describing a trend, not a trigger. Anyone who tells you a death cross by itself means something specific is going to happen next is selling you certainty that the tool does not contain.
