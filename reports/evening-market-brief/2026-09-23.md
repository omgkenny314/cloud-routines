# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, September 23, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a day when good news about the economy was bad news for stocks. A survey of U.S. business activity came in far hotter than anyone expected — the strongest reading in five years — and instead of cheering, the market sold off. The reason is the Federal Reserve. The Fed raised interest rates last week for the first time since 2023, and a booming economy makes another hike more likely, not less. Bond yields took off: the 10-year Treasury yield punched through 5% and closed at its highest level since July 2007, a 19-year high, in the biggest one-day move for that yield in about a year and a half. Stocks followed bonds down, with the Nasdaq giving back the record it had just set on Tuesday. Oil added to the inflation worry by snapping a five-day losing streak after Iran's president told the United Nations his country would not surrender. The single biggest storyline is the bond market: when money earns 5% risk-free for ten years, everything else has to justify itself against that.

## The numbers

**S&P 500** closed at 7,706.03, down 0.75%. Higher bond yields make future company profits worth less today, and the broad market repriced accordingly.

**Dow Jones Industrial Average** closed at 51,511.59, down 352.10 points or 0.68%. The Dow fell least of the three, helped by its tilt toward older industrial and value names over expensive tech.

**Nasdaq Composite** closed at 26,936.04, down 1.13%. Tech fell hardest — it had closed at a record 27,244 on Tuesday, and high-growth stocks are the most sensitive to rising rates.

**10-year Treasury yield** rose about 13 basis points to roughly 5.10%, the highest since July 2007. (A basis point is one one-hundredth of a percentage point.) Reports put the intraday peak as high as 5.135%. The strong business-activity survey, hawkish Fed commentary, and weak demand at a government bond auction all pushed it up, and the move accelerated once it broke above the round 5% level. The 5-year yield jumped roughly 19 basis points to 5.031%, above 5% for the first time since 2007, and the 2-year reached 4.93%.

**Crude oil** rose: U.S. West Texas Intermediate settled at $92.16 a barrel, up 1.8%, and Brent settled at $103.08, up 3.9%. This snapped five straight down days after Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian told the U.N. that Iran "cannot be made to surrender." Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has nearly stopped — tanker trackers counted just two commodity-carrying vessels on a waterway that normally sees more than 100 ships a day.

**Bitcoin** traded around $84,300 in the afternoon, down roughly 2.5% on the day, after opening near $86,200. Crypto fell alongside stocks as yields rose — the same logic applies, since an asset paying no interest looks worse when safe bonds pay more.

## Big movers and why

**Worthington Enterprises (WOR)**, an industrial manufacturer, surged about 16% — the day's standout gainer. It beat on both earnings and revenue, but the outlook did the heavy lifting: CEO Joseph Hayek pointed to "rapidly growing demand for our engineered ASME tanks used in liquid cooling systems for data centers." A century-old metal-products company just got repriced as an AI infrastructure supplier.

**Micron Technology (MU)** rose about 5% to roughly $1,090–1,096, one of the few large tech names to climb on a down day. Micron closed above $1,000 for the first time ever on Tuesday, capping a 266% rally driven by a historic memory-chip shortage. Its entire 2026 high-bandwidth memory supply is already sold out to feed Nvidia's AI data centers, and analysts keep raising targets — Citi reaffirmed a Buy with a $1,300 target — ahead of earnings on September 30.

**IonQ (IONQ)**, a quantum computing company, jumped on news that it demonstrated what it calls the industry's first end-to-end real-time quantum error correction decoder, running on a single ordinary CPU. Reports on the size of the move conflict and I could not pin down the exact closing figure: the stock was up more than 11% premarket, spiked intraday to about $46.05, and one source put the close near $42.70, which would be a gain of roughly 5% from Tuesday's $40.74. Treat the direction as solid and the magnitude as unconfirmed.

**Paychex (PAYX)**, the payroll and HR services company, tumbled nearly 7% after revenue in its Management Solutions business — its largest segment — missed expectations.

**Royal Caribbean (RCL)** fell close to 3% to a 52-week low after announcing it would buy a 50% stake in the parent company of Sandals and Beaches Resorts for $3 billion. Investors often mark a buyer's stock down when a large cash deal lands in a rising-rate environment.

**KB Home (KBH)**, the homebuilder, slipped more than 1% after guiding fourth-quarter deliveries below expectations and forecasting housing gross margins of 16% to 16.6% against the 17.2% analysts wanted. Homebuilders are the most rate-sensitive corner of the market, and a 5% 10-year yield goes straight into mortgage rates.

**Alibaba (BABA)** U.S.-listed shares fell about 4% as traders took profits after a four-day winning streak, with the Trump–Xi summit looming.

## Headlines that matter

**The business activity survey ran hot.** S&P Global's flash composite Purchasing Managers' Index — a monthly survey asking companies whether business is getting better or worse, where anything above 50 means expansion — rose to 58.4 in September from 56.0, the highest since July 2021. Services hit 58.7 and manufacturing 56.7, a four-year high. Why it matters: 58.4 is not "fine," it is "booming." A booming economy with inflation already too high is exactly the combination that forces a central bank to keep raising rates, which is why a good number produced a bad day.

**The Fed is hiking again, and the market is bracing for more.** On September 16 the Fed raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point to a target range of 3.75%–4% in a unanimous 12-0 vote — its first increase since 2023 — with Chair Kevin Warsh saying inflation has been "too high for too long." Sixteen of eighteen officials expect at least one more hike this year. After today's data, market-implied odds of another hike at the October 27–28 meeting jumped sharply; sources put the new figure between roughly 66% and 73%, up from around 55% a day earlier and under 10% a month ago. Why it matters: the entire market spent years pricing in rate cuts. Repricing for hikes instead is a change in the weather, not the forecast.

**A weak five-year Treasury auction added fuel.** When the government sells new bonds and buyers show up thin, the government has to offer a higher yield to clear the sale. That is part of why the 5-year yield jumped 19 basis points in a day. Why it matters: it suggests today's yield move was not only about the Fed but also about genuine hesitation among buyers of U.S. government debt.

**Iran, oil, and the Strait of Hormuz.** Iran's president struck a defiant tone at the U.N., and crude reversed higher. Meanwhile Saudi Arabia is preparing to restart exports through its East-West pipeline, which would let it bypass Hormuz entirely. Why it matters: oil is an input cost for nearly everything. Rising crude feeds inflation, which feeds Fed expectations, which feeds bond yields — the same chain that drove today.

**Xi Jinping arrived in Washington.** The Chinese president landed today ahead of tomorrow's one-day summit with President Trump, his first U.S. visit in three years. Why it matters: a tariff truce between the two countries expires November 10, and the U.S. is reportedly holding off on announcing new tariffs until after the meeting. Whatever comes out of it lands directly on the cost structure of a lot of American companies.

## What to watch tomorrow

**The Trump–Xi summit in Washington** is the main event — one day, with an agenda covering tariffs, artificial intelligence governance, critical minerals, Taiwan, and Iran. The market's specific question is whether the tariff truce expiring November 10 gets extended.

**Initial jobless claims** at 8:30 AM Eastern. This is the most timely read on the labor market that exists. Given that the Fed is now hiking into a hot economy, a low claims number reinforces the case for more hikes.

**Current account data and new home sales** also arrive. New home sales carry extra weight with the 10-year yield above 5% and mortgage rates following it up.

**Four Federal Reserve speakers**: Williams, Barkin, Hammack, and Paulson. New York Fed President John Williams is the one to listen for, since his views tend to track the center of the committee.

**Earnings**: Accenture (ACN), the consulting and technology services firm, reports before the open — a useful read on corporate IT spending. Costco Wholesale (COST) reports after the close, one of the better windows into how the American consumer is actually behaving.

**Friday** brings durable goods orders, which track business orders for long-lasting equipment and serve as a proxy for corporate investment.

## Today's lesson: market breadth, or checking whether the whole team showed up

Today's lesson is a technical one — technical analysis meaning the study of price and trading behavior itself, rather than a company's underlying business.

**Breadth** is the measure of how widely a market move is shared. The headline index number tells you the score; breadth tells you how the score was produced. The classic tool is the advance-decline line: count how many stocks rose, subtract how many fell, and track that running total. A market where 400 of the 500 S&P names fall is a genuinely broad decline. A market where the index drops but 300 names actually rose is something else entirely — a few very large stocks dragging the average down while most of the market holds up.

Today gives a clean illustration in the spread between the indexes. The Dow fell 0.68%, the S&P 500 fell 0.75%, and the Nasdaq fell 1.13% — and the Russell 2000, which tracks small companies, sank too. That ordering is not random. It says the selling concentrated in the most rate-sensitive parts of the market: expensive tech names whose value rests on profits far in the future, and smaller companies that borrow at floating rates and feel a 5% Treasury yield immediately. Meanwhile utilities and consumer discretionary stocks led the sector declines, both classic casualties of rising rates. So this was not a panic where everything got thrown overboard at once. It was a targeted repricing around one variable: the cost of money.

Here is the basketball version. A team wins by 12. That tells you almost nothing on its own. If the scoring is spread across eight players, the team is healthy and the win is repeatable. If one star scored 45 and everyone else was cold, the same 12-point margin is fragile — one bad shooting night and the whole thing collapses. Breadth is reading the box score instead of the final score. When an index climbs on a handful of enormous names while most stocks quietly slip, that narrowness is a real fact about the market, even though the headline number looks fine.

One honest caveat: I verified today's index-level and sector spreads, but I could not confirm a specific advance-decline count for the session, so I am not giving you one. The concept holds regardless — and the habit worth building is that whenever you hear "the market fell 0.75%," the next question is *which* stocks fell, and were they most of them or just a few heavy ones.
