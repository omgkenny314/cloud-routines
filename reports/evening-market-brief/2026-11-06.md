# Market Close Brief — Friday, November 6, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a bounce, not an all-clear. Yesterday was the worst day for American stocks since the April 2025 tariff panic, and it pushed the Nasdaq into what Wall Street calls a correction. Today the chip stocks that led the fall led the recovery, and the Nasdaq clawed back a little under a fifth of yesterday's point loss. But notice the split: the Nasdaq rose about 1.1%, the S&P 500 rose only about 0.3%, and the Dow actually *fell* about 0.5%. That is the opposite of the normal pattern, and it tells you this was not broad buying — it was money going back into the specific stocks that had just been hit hardest, while the rest of the market drifted. All three indexes still finished the week lower.

The single biggest storyline is not a number at all; it is a political one. In Tuesday's midterm elections Democrats took both houses of Congress, and separately, voters in communities across ten states were handed 41 local ballot measures about data centers — the enormous warehouses of computers that artificial-intelligence companies are spending hundreds of billions of dollars to build. Voters sided with restricting them in all but one. For two years the market's central assumption has been that AI spending would continue more or less without limit. This week investors started pricing in the possibility that the binding limit is not chips or money but electricity bills and voters — a slower, messier problem than anything a semiconductor company can engineer its way out of. Today's second storyline worked against the first: a much stronger-than-expected jobs report, which is good news for workers and awkward news for stocks, because this Federal Reserve is *raising* interest rates, not cutting them.

## Headlines that matter

**The October jobs report came in nearly three times as strong as expected.** Employers added 165,000 jobs against forecasts clustered between about 59,000 and 65,000. Unemployment *fell* to 4.1% from an expected 4.2%. August and September were revised up by a combined 84,000, with September now showing a 60,000 gain. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4% on the month and 3.1% from a year ago, both above expectations. Professional and business services added 70,000, the largest gain of any sector.

Why it matters, and why stocks did not simply celebrate: this Federal Reserve is fighting inflation, not unemployment. Strong hiring and rising wages are evidence it has not yet done enough. So good news about jobs arrives at the stock market as bad news about interest rates. There was also a softer detail inside the report — the U-6 underemployment rate, a broader measure that counts part-time workers who want full-time work, rose to 7.9% from 7.6%, and labor-force participation was 61.9%. A genuinely strong report, but not uniformly so.

**The Fed is closer to another rate hike.** The federal funds rate sits at 3.75%–4.00% after a quarter-point *increase* in September, and the Fed held steady on October 28 by a 10-2 vote, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both wanting to hike. After today's jobs report, market-implied odds of a quarter-point hike at the December 8–9 meeting moved to roughly 78%, up from about 67% yesterday; I saw readings between 69% and 85% across sources, so treat the exact number as unsettled and the direction as clear. Chair Kevin Warsh has said policymakers will watch "the accumulation of data." Cook said inflation remains too high. New York Fed President John Williams said the effects of the global tariffs are "mostly behind us," while New York Fed researchers published work arguing tariffs can keep pushing consumer prices up for a full year as costs pass through to retailers and shoppers.

Why it matters: every valuation in the stock market is a judgment about future profits measured against the return available on safe government bonds. When that safe return goes up, the same future profits are worth less today. That is the mechanism quietly pressing on stocks underneath all of this week's AI headlines.

**The data-center backlash became a measurable political fact.** Ballotpedia counted 41 local data-center measures across ten states on Tuesday's ballots; voters backed restrictions or rejected pro-development measures in all but one, Harwood, North Dakota. An NBC exit poll found 56% of voters said AI and data centers were a crucial or major factor in their congressional vote. Governors-elect Gina Hinojosa in Texas, Keisha Lance Bottoms in Georgia, and Amy Acton in Ohio all campaigned on regulating data centers. The pressure is bipartisan: Texas Governor Greg Abbott had already paused data-center permitting, and Pennsylvania's Josh Shapiro enacted construction restrictions by executive order. Surveys point to rising household electricity bills as the main driver of the opposition.

Why it matters: the AI build-out has always had physical constraints — chips, power, land. Investors assumed those were engineering problems, which get solved on a schedule you can model. Permitting and local politics are not engineering problems. They are slow, unpredictable, and resistant to capital. That is why a set of local ballot measures moved trillions of dollars of market value this week.

**Earnings season is quietly excellent, which is the week's strangest fact.** With 88% of the S&P 500 reported, FactSet's November 6 update puts third-quarter earnings growth at 31.4%, up from 30.1% a week ago, and revenue growth at 14.3%. All eleven sectors are growing earnings, seven of them at double-digit rates. And the breadth is notable: the Magnificent 7 group of megacap technology companies grew earnings 22.6%, while the *other 493* companies grew 32.6% — the rest of the market is out-earning the giants.

Why it matters: this is the best argument that what happened this week was a repricing of expectations rather than a deterioration in businesses. Profits are rising fast. What changed is what investors are willing to pay for them.

## After the bell

Here is the honest answer, and it is a short one: **nothing of consequence has come out since the close, and nothing significant is scheduled for tonight.**

That is not a gap in my reporting — it is how the calendar works. Friday evening is the deadest slot of the corporate week. Companies release earnings after the close so investors have time to read the numbers before trading resumes, and a Friday release gives the market an entire weekend to think, which is the last thing a management team wants. So companies report Monday through Thursday evenings. A Friday-night release is usually a sign a company is hoping the news gets buried.

## Today's lesson: the 200-day moving average, and how a floor becomes a ceiling

Today's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning we read the behavior of the price itself rather than a company's financial statements. And today left the Nasdaq sitting just below the single most-watched line in the business.

Start with the tool. A **moving average** is the average of an index's closing prices over some number of recent days, recalculated every day. A 200-day moving average takes the last 200 closing prices, averages them, and plots that one number. Tomorrow it drops the oldest price, adds the newest, and plots again. The result is a smooth line that follows the price but lags behind it. Its whole purpose is to strip out noise: any single day can be mad, but 200 days of closes averaged together can only move if something sustained is happening. Two hundred trading days is roughly ten months, so this line is a picture of the long-run trend. (A 50-day average does the same job over about ten weeks, and so reacts much faster.)

Think of a basketball player's shooting percentage. One cold night barely dents a season average — you were 2 for 11, and your season number moves from 47% to 46%. But a month of cold nights bends the average, and once it has bent, everyone talks about you differently. The 200-day moving average is price's season average. Yesterday's 5.5% crash was one brutal game. The 200-day did not move much. What *did* move was where the price sits relative to it.

Here are today's actual numbers. The Nasdaq Composite closed at **24,579.42**. Its 200-day moving average sits at roughly **25,095 to 25,098** — I verified that figure from two separate technical sources, though one widely-used site printed 26,626, which looks stale, so treat it as approximate. So the index closed about **2% below** its 200-day line. Its 50-day average is far above, near 26,500. And the index remains about **10.9% below its record close of 27,599.89**, set on October 6 — which is what "correction" means: a drop of 10% or more from a recent peak.

Now the part that is genuinely useful. For most of this year the Nasdaq was *above* that 200-day line, and on the handful of occasions it fell toward it, buyers appeared and it bounced. A level that repeatedly stops a decline is called **support** — think of it as a floor. Yesterday the index fell straight through it. And here is the thing worth carrying with you: when a floor breaks, it very often becomes a ceiling. The technical term is **resistance**, and the reason is human rather than mathematical. Plenty of people bought near that line on the way down, believing it would hold. They are now losing money. If the index climbs back to where they bought, many of them will sell just to get out even — and that wave of break-even selling is exactly what stops the rally. The same price level that attracted buyers on the way down generates sellers on the way up.

Which is why today's 1.09% gain, encouraging as it looked, did not change the technical picture. The Nasdaq bounced, but it bounced *underneath* the line. Until it closes convincingly back above roughly 25,100, a chart reader would call this a rally within a downtrend rather than the end of one. And two honest caveats: none of this is prophecy. The 200-day average matters largely *because* so many people watch it — it is a widely shared reference point, not a law of physics, and indexes break through it in both directions all the time. It also describes where price has been, never where it is going. The useful habit is not predicting from the line, but knowing which side of it you are standing on, and why the people around you are reacting the way they are.
