# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, October 13, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was the day earnings season started, and the market responded by going almost nowhere — while two very different stories pulled hard in opposite directions underneath. The big banks opened the season with a genuinely mixed set of reports, strong enough in trading and dealmaking to keep the market from falling, weak enough in old-fashioned lending to stop it rising. Meanwhile the memory chip selloff that began yesterday did not just continue, it went global and got much worse overseas: South Korea's main stock index fell more than 7% in a single session, one of its worst days in years, on the same worry about Chinese competition in memory chips that hit Micron yesterday. And yet the S&P 500 finished down less than two points. That gap — a chip panic abroad, a shrug at home — is the single biggest storyline, and it is what today's lesson is built on. One more thing quietly helped: oil stopped climbing and Treasury yields eased back from their 24-year highs, which took a little pressure off everything. Tomorrow morning's inflation report is still the week's main event, and nobody wanted to be too committed before it.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: closed at 7,805.45, down 1.79 points, or about 0.02%.** That is as close to unchanged as a day gets — two one-hundredths of one percent. The broadest gauge of large American companies stayed within a whisker of its record. Bank strength and an Apple record cancelled out the chip selloff almost exactly. I verified this against yesterday's close of 7,807.24 in the repository: the arithmetic lines up precisely.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: closed at 51,670.10, down 180.45 points, or about 0.35%.** Thirty large, established companies, and today's worst performer of the three. Johnson & Johnson was the main culprit — it is a Dow member and it fell hard. One outlet rounded the decline to "170 points"; the 180.45 figure is the one that reconciles exactly with yesterday's 51,850.55 close, so that is the one I am using.

- **Nasdaq Composite: closed at 27,204.38, down 41.52 points, or about 0.15%.** The technology-heavy index. Chips dragged, Apple and Meta Platforms lifted, and the two nearly offset.

- **10-year Treasury yield: roughly 5.19% to 5.22%, down a few basis points.** I could not verify an official closing figure, and I want to be straight with you about that. The bond market reopened today after yesterday's Columbus Day closure, and the readings I found through the session ranged from 5.189% to 5.276%, clustering around 5.19% to 5.22%. For comparison, Friday's close was 5.24%, and the recent peak was about 5.34% to 5.37% last week — the highest since 2002. So: lower, which is the direction that matters, but I am not going to hand you a precise close I could not confirm. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. The reason yields eased is that oil stopped surging.

- **Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate settled at $94.14 a barrel, up 40 cents, or about 0.4%. Brent settled at $105.69, down 96 cents, or about 0.9%.** West Texas Intermediate is the U.S. benchmark grade; Brent is the international one. These two settlements both reconcile exactly with yesterday's closes of $93.74 and $106.65, which is the cleanest confirmation I could get. The split reflects two forces: hopes for a U.S.-brokered deal on Russian diesel and an "energy ceasefire" between Russia and Ukraine pushed prices down, while continued Houthi attacks on Saudi energy facilities pushed them up. The important point is not the direction of either grade but that the relentless climb paused — that is what let bond yields fall.

- **Bitcoin: roughly $83,400 to $83,500 in the afternoon, up about 1.3% over 24 hours.** Bitcoin trades around the clock, so there is no official close. It opened near $81,750, dipped below $81,000 yesterday, and spent today recovering. The recovery was attributed to ordinary spot buyers stepping in rather than exchange-traded fund money, which has been flowing out — U.S. spot bitcoin funds saw roughly $680 million of weekly outflows. For scale, bitcoin remains roughly a third below its record above $126,000 set a year ago.

## Big movers and why

**Bumble (ticker BMBL) was the day's biggest winner, up about 56%.** The dating app company agreed to be taken private by Ithaca Holdings for $6.05 per share in cash, a deal valuing it at roughly $1.3 billion. This is worth understanding as a category: when a company agrees to be bought for cash at a fixed price, its stock jumps almost immediately to just under that price and then mostly stops moving. The uncertainty about the business is replaced by a much narrower question — will the deal close? That is why takeover pops are usually one-day events rather than the start of a trend.

**Johnson & Johnson (ticker JNJ) fell roughly 5% to 6% despite beating expectations.** The healthcare and pharmaceutical giant topped both revenue and adjusted earnings estimates and the stock dropped anyway. Reported declines ranged from about 4.8% to 6.8% depending on when the figure was captured. This is the single most useful thing a new investor can learn from today: a company can report better numbers than Wall Street expected and still fall, because what moves a stock is the gap between results and expectations, not the results themselves — and expectations include things the headline earnings number does not capture, like guidance for coming quarters and the quality of the underlying growth.

**Goldman Sachs (ticker GS) rose about 1% on a very large beat.** The investment bank reported earnings of $17.01 per share against estimates near $13.09, with revenue of $18.68 billion versus an expected $16.71 billion. Stock-trading revenue rose about 72%. Note the mismatch: an enormous earnings beat produced a roughly 1% gain, and the stock faded from premarket gains of more than 4%. When a stock has already run up in anticipation, even great news buys you less than you would think.

**Wells Fargo (ticker WFC) fell about 3%.** Earnings per share of $1.87 edged past estimates, but revenue of $21.87 billion missed by about 2%, and net interest income came in short. Net interest income is the difference between what a bank earns on its loans and what it pays on deposits — the core, boring, bread-and-butter profit engine of a traditional bank. Investors forgave a lot today, but not a miss there.

**JPMorgan Chase (ticker JPM) missed, and the stock barely moved.** My sources disagreed on the exact figures — one reported $5.75 per share excluding a $658 million securities loss against a $5.92 consensus, another reported $5.57 against $5.90 — so take the specific number as unconfirmed. What is consistent across sources is that it was a miss, described as the bank's first quarterly shortfall in close to three years. Reports on the stock reaction also conflicted, ranging from down 3% premarket to roughly flat to up about 1%. I could not verify where it closed.

**Citigroup (ticker C) beat, and the reaction is genuinely unclear.** Earnings per share came in around $2.85 against estimates near $2.65, with revenue of about $23.8 billion. One source reported the stock down 3.4%, another said it edged higher. I could not resolve that conflict, so I am flagging it rather than picking a side.

**Micron Technology (ticker MU) fell roughly 4% to 5%, and the chip selloff broadened.** Micron is the big American memory chip maker. Applied Materials (ticker AMAT), which makes the equipment used to manufacture chips, fell about 6%. Lam Research (ticker LRCX) and Teradyne (ticker TER), also chip equipment makers, each lost more than 3%. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index — the standard chip sector gauge, usually called the SOX — was down about 1.5% in the afternoon and on track for a sixth consecutive decline, though I could not confirm its final close. The cause is the same as yesterday: China's ChangXin Memory Technologies, usually called CXMT, signaled progress on next-generation memory architecture, with its president reportedly saying at a Shanghai industry event that products using it will ship before year-end.

**Apple (ticker AAPL) hit a record high, up about 1%.** The company unveiled HomeView, a smart home hub built for multiple people in a household that uses facial recognition to show each person relevant information. Apple being at a record on the same day chip stocks fell for a sixth straight session is the cleanest illustration of how little "the market" tells you about any individual stock.

**Meta Platforms (ticker META) rose roughly 2% to 3.4%**, a second day of gains on the reception to its Muse Wonder artificial intelligence model. Truist raised its price target to $1,000 from $763.

**Fermi (ticker FRMI) jumped somewhere between 17% and 22%**, the figure depending on when it was measured. The energy and AI infrastructure developer signed a credit support agreement with Advanced Micro Devices (ticker AMD). A credit support agreement means a larger, stronger company is effectively standing behind some of a smaller company's borrowing, which lowers its cost of raising money.

**Domino's Pizza (ticker DPZ) fell about 3.8%** after trimming its full-year sales outlook — a company telling you it now expects to sell less than it previously said.

**UnitedHealth Group (ticker UNH) gave back a big early gain.** The largest private health insurer beat third-quarter expectations and rose more than 4% before the opening bell, then faded to roughly flat, with one report showing it down 0.2%. Premarket moves are made on thin volume by a small number of participants; they are a hint about the day, not a prediction of it.

## Headlines that matter

**South Korea's Kospi index fell more than 7%, and that is the number to remember from today.** The Associated Press reported the index down 7.4% to 5,914.35, its lowest since late July, though other outlets reported somewhere between 5.5% and 6.5% — treat the precise figure as approximate, but the magnitude is not in dispute. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix led the fall, with SK Hynix down roughly 8.8% to 10%. A "sell-side sidecar" — a South Korean circuit breaker that briefly slows down large sell orders — was triggered for the first time in nearly two months. Why this matters: the Kospi is unusually concentrated in memory chip makers, so it functions as a nearly pure bet on the memory cycle. When the same news costs Korea 7% and the S&P 500 two one-hundredths of a percent, you are watching what concentration does.

**Earnings season opened mixed, and the market's own interpretation was telling.** The pattern across the big banks was consistent: trading and investment banking were strong, while traditional lending was softer. Analysts came into this season expecting S&P 500 profit growth of roughly 25% to 30% versus a year ago. Why this matters: when the expectation bar is set that high, beating it is the minimum requirement, not an achievement — which is exactly what Goldman's enormous beat and 1% gain demonstrated.

**Oil's pause took real pressure off the bond market.** The reason the 10-year yield eased today is that energy prices stabilized, and the reason that matters is the chain it sits in: oil feeds gasoline and shipping and manufacturing costs, which feed inflation, which feeds the Federal Reserve's thinking, which sets interest rates, which determine what every stock in the market is worth. The driver was progress on a U.S.-brokered Russia-Ukraine "energy ceasefire" and a possible deal on Russian diesel, though that ceasefire has been fraying since it was announced Sunday — Russia struck Ukrainian energy sites shortly afterward. So this is relief, not resolution. For context on how sharp the energy squeeze has been, the national average diesel price was $6.26 a gallon yesterday, nearly 80% higher than at the start of the year.

**Tomorrow's inflation report is the last one before the Fed decides.** The September Consumer Price Index arrives tomorrow at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, 7:30 Central. Forecasters expect headline inflation of roughly 3.6% to 3.7% compared with a year ago, up from 3.4% in August, with core inflation near 2.4% to 2.5%. The Cleveland Fed's model projected 3.60% headline and 2.39% core. "Core" strips out food and energy, which bounce around too much to show the underlying trend. Why this matters enormously: this is the final CPI reading before the Fed's October 28 decision, markets have fully priced in a quarter-point rate increase by December, and most of the expected rise in the headline figure is coming from energy — exactly the thing that paused today. A hot number strengthens the case for more tightening.

**One sobering data point from the bond side.** A Bank of America survey of fund managers found they would shift meaningfully out of stocks and into bonds at a 10-year Treasury yield of around 6%. We are at roughly 5.2%. That is not a prediction, but it tells you where large professional investors have drawn their own line, and it explains why every move in that yield gets so much attention.

## Today's lesson: relative strength, or why the same news costs Korea 7% and America nothing

Today's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning the study of price and trading behavior itself, rather than the business or the economy underneath it. And today handed us the single cleanest example of this idea I have come across: one piece of news, one day, two markets, and a difference of more than seven percentage points.

**Relative strength** means comparing how one thing performs against something else over the same period, usually a stock or a group against the broad market. It is not a complicated calculation — you are just asking: did this do better or worse than everything else, and by how much? Note that this is a different idea from the Relative Strength Index we covered a few weeks ago. That one measures a stock against its own recent past. This one measures a stock against its neighbors.

Here is today's example. The news was the same everywhere: China's CXMT signaled progress on next-generation memory chips. Against that single piece of news, South Korea's Kospi index fell about 7.4%. The American chip equipment makers fell 3% to 6%. Micron fell 4% to 5%. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell for a sixth straight session. The S&P 500 fell 0.02%. And Apple closed at a record high. Same day, same news, and the outcomes range from catastrophic to triumphant.

What relative strength tells you is where the news actually landed. The Kospi is packed with memory chip makers — Samsung and SK Hynix are an enormous share of it — so a threat to memory pricing is a threat to the whole index. The S&P 500 has eleven sectors and five hundred companies, and memory chips are a slice of one of them. Apple does not sell memory; it buys it, which means cheaper Chinese memory is arguably good news for Apple's costs. So the same headline is an existential problem in Seoul, a sector problem in Philadelphia, and a mild tailwind in Cupertino. The relative moves make the structure of the news visible in a way the headline itself does not.

Think of it like watching a team that loses by one point. The final score says it was close. But if you look at the individual players, you find the star guard shot 2-for-15 while the bench scored 40. The narrow final margin hid both an awful performance and a great one, which cancelled out. Today's two-point decline in the S&P 500 is that one-point loss. Underneath it, the chip complex had an awful game and Apple had a career night. If you only ever read the index, you would conclude nothing happened today. Something very large happened today; it just happened to specific neighborhoods, and the relative moves are how you find them.
