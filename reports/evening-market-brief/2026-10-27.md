# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, October 27, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a split-screen day: a strong one for anything connected to artificial intelligence, and a brutal one for Boeing. The AI trade picked up right where it left off yesterday, and the reason was concrete rather than atmospheric — Broadcom, which designs custom AI chips, is at the center of a $63 billion arrangement to get far more of its hardware into Anthropic's hands, announced late Monday. Chip stocks rallied hard on it; a widely followed semiconductor index rose about 3.6%. That lifted the Nasdaq Composite 1.18% and the S&P 500 about 0.6%. Meanwhile Boeing (ticker BA), the aircraft maker, reported a quarterly loss that was worse than expected and pushed back the timeline on both its 737 MAX production ramp and its cash-flow targets — and the stock fell about 10.7%, to its lowest level since April 2025. Because the Dow Jones Industrial Average gives Boeing enormous influence (a quirk of its construction I taught you on the 23rd), that single stock was almost enough to cancel out everything else: the Dow finished up just 15.99 points. Underneath both stories sits tomorrow, when the Federal Reserve announces its interest-rate decision. Almost nobody expects a move tomorrow, but almost everybody expects a rate *hike* by December — an unusual backdrop for a market sitting near record highs.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: closed at 7,722.60, up about 46 points, or 0.6%.** The broad U.S. stock index rose for a third straight session as AI and chip names did the heavy lifting. It closed Monday at 7,676.77, so the arithmetic ties out. CNBC and the closing recap at Investrade both report this level. One site, StockMarketWatch, showed a 0.05% gain instead — that looks like a quote of the SPY exchange-traded fund captured before the close, not the index, and I would disregard it.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: closed at 52,377.57, up 15.99 points, or 0.03%.** Essentially flat, and the reason is one name: Boeing's roughly 10.7% drop. The Dow is price-weighted, so its highest-priced members swing it hardest, and Boeing is one of them. Strip Boeing out and the day's gain would have looked far more like the S&P 500's.

- **Nasdaq Composite: closed at 26,477.81, up 308.43 points, or 1.18%.** The tech-heavy index led by a wide margin, which is exactly what you would expect on a day whose single storyline was AI chips. Confirmed by CNBC, Investrade and Kiplinger.

- **Russell 2000: closed at about 2,818, up 1.16%.** This is the index of smaller U.S. companies. I mention it because it kept pace with the Nasdaq today, which tells you the buying was not *only* in the AI giants. (Single source — Investrade — so hold it loosely.)

- **10-year Treasury yield: right around 5.00%, essentially unchanged.** The 10-year Treasury yield is what the U.S. government pays to borrow money for ten years, and it is the number that sets the tone for mortgage rates and for how investors value stocks. CNBC had it up less than one basis point at 4.995% during the session, and a service called Rallies had it at 4.997%; one newsletter reported a close of 4.98%. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. I could not verify an exact official close, so treat it as "about 5%, and it barely moved" — the bond market is sitting on its hands until the Fed speaks tomorrow.

- **Crude oil: down about 2%, with West Texas Intermediate settling near $99.90 a barrel and Brent near $113.88.** West Texas Intermediate is the U.S. benchmark grade; Brent is the international one. Oil fell for a second day on diplomacy — Saudi, Turkish and Pakistani officials met Iran's leadership in Tehran, which the market read as lowering the odds of a supply disruption. Note that WTI slipping under $100 is the headline-grabber, but both grades remain near multi-month highs. These settlement figures come from a single closing recap; yesterday's official settles ($101.76 WTI, $115.81 Brent) are consistent with them.

- **Bitcoin: roughly $80,000, close to flat on the day.** Bitcoin trades around the clock, so there is no official close. CoinDesk showed about $79,451 in the morning, and a quote timestamped 18:40 UTC had it near $80,686, up about 0.45%. It dipped below $80,000 earlier as traders de-risked ahead of the Fed. It remains roughly 36% below its all-time high near $126,000.

## Big movers and why

- **Boeing (ticker BA), the commercial aircraft and defense manufacturer, closed down about 10.7% at $168.14 — its worst day since the April 2025 tariff panic and a new 52-week low.** Three things did the damage. First, the loss was bigger than expected: a net loss of $116 million, or 26 cents a share, which works out to a 34-cent loss after adjusting for one-time items, versus the 21-cent loss analysts had penciled in. Second, the company said it does not expect to meaningfully ramp up 737 MAX production until the second half of 2027. Third, and most important to the people who sold today, it laid out free-cash-flow targets of $3 billion in 2027 and $6 billion in 2028, reaching its $10 billion goal in 2029 — about a year later than investors had been assuming. Worth knowing the other side: revenue rose about 4% to roughly $24.19 billion, the loss narrowed enormously from the $5.34 billion loss a year ago, free cash flow improved to $464 million, and the order backlog stands at about $745 billion. The market did not care, because the cash is arriving later than promised. Today's lesson is built on this stock.

- **Broadcom (ticker AVGO), which designs custom AI chips and networking hardware, closed up about 4% and was near the top of the S&P 500.** Late Monday it announced a roughly $63 billion arrangement under which Bank of America, BlackRock, Citigroup and Morgan Stanley finance purchases of Broadcom's AI chips and infrastructure through a special-purpose vehicle that then leases the hardware to Anthropic. Broadcom is neither the lender nor the borrower, but it agreed to provide "residual value support" backstopping about 80% of the deal's value — meaning if the leased equipment is worth less than expected later, Broadcom absorbs much of that shortfall. So the revenue is real and large, and the company has taken on a real obligation to get it. Even after today, the stock sits roughly 30% below its June record, on worries that too much of its business depends on too few customers. (Intraday reports had the gain at 3.3%–3.5%; Kiplinger's close-of-day report says about 4%. I could not pin down the exact closing price.)

- **Advanced Micro Devices (ticker AMD), the chip designer, rose about 4% and was one of the strongest positive forces on the S&P 500 after Broadcom.** No company news of its own — it rose because the Broadcom deal is evidence that AI hardware spending is still accelerating, and AMD sells into that same budget. This is what people mean by a "read-across."

- **General Motors (ticker GM), the automaker, rose about 5% to roughly $80 after announcing it will move its stock listing from the New York Stock Exchange to the Nasdaq.** The ticker stays "GM," shareholders need to do nothing, and trading shifts over on November 17. The reason the stock moved is mechanical rather than operational: a Nasdaq listing makes GM eligible for inclusion in the Nasdaq-100 index, and if that happened, every fund that tracks that index would have to buy the shares. Nothing about how many trucks GM sells changed today. Tesla rose about 3% and Ford edged higher alongside it.

- **CoreWeave (ticker CRWV), which rents out AI computing capacity, rose somewhere between about 3% and 5%** — reports differ depending on the timestamp, and I could not confirm the close. Same driver as AMD: it is a pure bet on AI infrastructure demand.

- **Nvidia (ticker NVDA), the dominant AI chipmaker, rose only about 0.8%.** Notable for being small. On a day when its corner of the market rallied 3.6%, the biggest name in it barely participated — a reminder that "the AI trade" is not one thing moving in lockstep.

- **Coca-Cola (ticker KO), the beverage company, fell about 1.6% to $91.22 despite beating expectations.** Adjusted earnings came in at 91 cents a share against the 88 cents analysts expected, and the company raised its full-year outlook to comparable earnings-per-share growth of at least 11%. What bothered investors was volume in North America, and management's warning that consumers across income levels remain focused on value and that the pressure could persist into 2027. Beating last quarter's number does not help if you tell people next year looks harder.

- **United Parcel Service (ticker UPS), the package delivery company, finished roughly flat** — around $100.41, up 0.08% — even though it beat estimates and raised its full-year revenue guidance to about $92.6 billion against the $91.78 billion expected. A good report that the market had already priced in.

- **Corning (ticker GLW), which makes specialty glass and fiber-optic components, gave up an early gain and traded down about 1% in the afternoon** after beating on earnings with solid optical-communications growth. One analysis noted the stock has now fallen after each of its last three reports despite beating each time.

## Headlines that matter

- **The Federal Reserve's two-day policy meeting began today; the decision comes tomorrow at 2:00 p.m. Eastern (1:00 p.m. Central), with Chair Kevin Warsh's press conference half an hour later.** The federal funds rate — the Fed's main lever, the rate banks charge each other overnight — currently sits in a target range of 3.75% to 4.00%, following a hike in September that was the Fed's first in three years. Why it matters: interest rates are the yardstick against which every stock is measured. When safe government bonds pay about 5%, a stock has to work harder to justify its price, which is why a market near record highs is this sensitive to one announcement.

- **Markets expect no change tomorrow, but a hike by December.** Prediction market Kalshi had roughly a 93% chance of a hold tomorrow with about 6% odds of a quarter-point hike; a separate estimate from interest-rate swaps put hike odds near 11%. For December, Morningstar and Kalshi both cite about a 75% chance of a quarter-point increase, and Kalshi notes that all 100 economists polled by Reuters between October 16 and 20 expect a December hike. Why it matters: this is the opposite of the environment most investors spent the last two years positioned for. Stocks are rallying into a tightening cycle, not an easing one — which is precisely why tomorrow's language, not tomorrow's decision, is the event.

- **Oil fell a second day on Middle East diplomacy.** Saudi, Turkish and Pakistani officials met Iran's leadership in Tehran, and the market reduced the premium it had been paying for the risk of supply disruption. Energy was the worst-performing sector, with the energy sector fund XLE down more than 2%. Why it matters two ways: cheaper oil feeds through to lower inflation, which is helpful with a Fed considering hikes; but it also directly shrinks the earnings of every energy company, which is why that sector fell on a day the market rose. Limiting how far oil can fall: Houthi attacks on Saudi infrastructure and harassment of tankers near the Strait of Hormuz continue.

- **Monday's Anthropic IPO filing is still driving the tape, and the Nasdaq exchange won that listing.** An IPO, or initial public offering, is a private company's first sale of stock to the public. Combined with the GM listing switch, this was a large week for the Nasdaq exchange as a business. Why it matters: the filing told investors that enormous sums are still willing to flow into AI, which is the exact question the market had been losing confidence about through most of October.

- **September durable goods orders were scheduled for release this morning at 7:30 a.m. Central, along with consumer confidence and new home sales.** Durable goods are long-lived manufactured items like aircraft, machinery and appliances, so the orders number is a read on whether businesses are still committing to big purchases. Forecasts ranged from about 1.2% to 1.8% growth. **I could not verify the actual figures that printed today** — none of the sources I reached reported them — so I am not going to guess at numbers. Flagging it as a gap rather than filling it in.

## Today's lesson: the 52-week range, and what a new low actually tells you

Today's lesson is a technical one — meaning we are reading the price chart rather than the financial statements. And it comes straight out of a phrase in the Boeing coverage that is easy to skate past: the stock hit a **new 52-week low**.

Here is the concept. The **52-week range** is simply the highest and lowest price a stock has traded at over the past year. Boeing's range going into today was roughly $176.77 at the bottom, set last November, and $254.35 at the top. A range is not a prediction — it is context. It answers the question "compared to its own recent history, where is this price?" A stock at $200 means nothing to you on its own. A stock at $200 inside a $176-to-$254 range tells you it is sitting in the lower third of where it has been all year.

Today Boeing closed at $168.14. That is below $176.77. The bottom of the range did not hold — it *moved*, and the new bottom is today's price. That is what "a new 52-week low" means, and here is why experienced investors pay attention to it rather than just noting it. Think about who owns the stock. If the price is lower than it has been at any point in a year, then **every single person who bought Boeing in the past twelve months is now sitting on a loss.** Nobody who bought recently has a profit. And people holding losses behave differently from people holding gains — many of them have been waiting to "just get back to even" so they can sell without taking the loss. So when the stock does bounce, it tends to run into a wall of sellers who are relieved to escape. That is the mechanism behind the old trading saying that new lows tend to beget more new lows. It is a tendency, not a law, and plenty of stocks make a new low and turn right around.

Now hold today's two stories side by side, because the contrast is the real lesson. The S&P 500 closed near record highs — the very top of its 52-week range, where essentially everyone who owns it has a gain. Boeing closed at the very bottom of its own, where nobody who bought this year does. Same day, same economy, same Fed meeting tomorrow. The 52-week range is what let you see that those two facts were both true at once, and it is why "the market was up today" tells you almost nothing about what happened to any particular thing you own.

The basketball version: a player scoring 18 points means nothing until you know he averages 9 or he averages 31. The season range is the context that turns a number into information. And a career-low shooting night matters not just because of the box score, but because of what it does to how he takes his next shot — and to how the defense plays him.

A reminder I will keep making: none of the above is a recommendation to buy or sell anything. A new 52-week low is a piece of context about where a price sits, not a signal about what to do.
