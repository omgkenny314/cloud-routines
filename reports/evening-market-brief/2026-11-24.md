## The day in one paragraph

Today the air came back out of the artificial-intelligence trade, but only out of that trade — and that distinction is the whole story. Monday was the best day for U.S. stocks since April 2025, built almost entirely on Nvidia's board reinstating Jensen Huang as chief executive. Today roughly half of the chip sector's Monday gain was handed back, with the damage concentrated in semiconductors and AI-server makers. And yet the Dow Jones Industrial Average *rose* 0.85% and closed back above 50,000, and most of the companies in the S&P 500 finished higher even though the S&P 500 itself finished lower. Three things pushed on the market at once: profit-taking in AI names, a report that the administration is readying a fresh round of tariffs on China, the European Union and more than a dozen other economies, and a consumer-confidence reading that blew past every forecast — which is good news for the economy and bad news for anyone hoping the Federal Reserve will stop raising interest rates. Hold onto that last point, because it is the one most people get backwards right now: this Fed is *hiking*, and the market puts a December increase at roughly 96%. Then, after the closing bell, Dell delivered one of the largest earnings beats you will ever see and raised its full-year revenue forecast to $202 billion — which is the strongest single piece of evidence yet that AI hardware demand is real, arriving on the very day the market decided to doubt it.

## Headlines that matter

**Consumer confidence came in far above every forecast.** The Conference Board's index jumped 11.4 points to 103.3, its highest reading since January 2025, up from an upwardly revised 91.9 in October. Economists expected roughly 92.4. It beat every estimate in Bloomberg's survey of economists. The Conference Board tied the improvement to reduced uncertainty following the midterm elections, while noting consumers still complain about high prices. *Why it matters:* this is the clearest case this month of good economic news being bad market news. A confident consumer keeps spending, spending keeps prices firm, and firm prices give the Fed room to keep raising interest rates. Strong data does not soothe this market; it tightens the screw.

**The administration is readying a new round of tariffs.** Bloomberg reported the U.S. could announce tariff increases as soon as today on goods from China, the European Union and more than a dozen other economies. The legal basis is a Section 301 investigation into industrial subsidies opened on March 11, and the point is to replace tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February. China is expected to face one of the steepest increases; the U.S.-China truce extended after the Shenzhen meeting runs until January. *Why it matters:* tariffs are a tax on imported goods, and they push in exactly the wrong direction for this market. They raise prices, which makes the Fed's inflation problem harder, and they squeeze the profit margins of any company that buys parts or products from abroad — which is most of the hardware industry. Note also that this is reporting based on people familiar with the plans, and the rates are not final. Timing on trade announcements has slipped before.

**The Federal Reserve is still expected to raise rates on December 9.** The Fed's target range sits at 3.75% to 4.00%. Futures markets put the odds of a quarter-point increase at the December 8–9 meeting at roughly 95% to 96%, with the small remainder betting on a half-point move. October's consumer price index rose 0.4% month over month against a 0.3% forecast, and the October meeting minutes showed almost all participants thought further increases would likely be appropriate. Governor Christopher Waller has argued for a half-point move; Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan wants at least two more quarter-point increases. Bill Ackman has argued publicly that continuing to hike risks a recession. *Why it matters:* this is the largest force acting on stock prices right now, and it runs opposite to the direction most people assume. When safe government debt pays you nearly 5%, the price a rational buyer will pay for a company's uncertain future profits falls. That arithmetic punishes expensive, long-dated growth stories hardest — which is a precise description of the AI complex.

**Axios reported that employees at Anthropic and OpenAI fear their unreleased models are too unsafe to release.** The story drew on more than two dozen sources and described an internal debate about whether to stop releasing frontier models publicly at all. Anthropic responded that keeping a model internal does not make it safe and that its responsibility begins in training; OpenAI said it has adopted new safety requirements that could slow releases. *Why it matters:* for markets, the relevant question is narrow and commercial. Enormous sums have been spent building AI infrastructure on the assumption that increasingly capable models will be sold to increasingly many customers. A credible report that the most capable models might be withheld puts a question mark over the revenue end of that chain — which is why it landed on Anthropic's stock and on the AI complex generally rather than being read as a pure safety story. It describes a debate, not a decision, and the companies have not confirmed the fears.

Diluted earnings were $8.82 per share on a GAAP basis, up 287%, and $9.45 per share adjusted, up 265%, against analyst expectations near $6.59. Dell had guided to about $49 billion in revenue, give or take $500 million — it came in nearly $6 billion above its own forecast.

The detail underneath explains it. AI-optimized server revenue hit a record $22.0 billion, up 291% year over year. Traditional servers and networking brought in $15.0 billion, up 235%. Storage was $4.9 billion, up 24%. The whole Infrastructure Solutions Group grew 198% to $42.0 billion. Operating cash flow was a record $4.5 billion for the quarter, and Dell returned a record $4.9 billion to shareholders through buybacks and dividends. The chief financial officer noted adjusted operating expenses fell to 7% of revenue, the lowest in the company's 42-year history. Dell raised full-year guidance to a record $202 billion in revenue, up 78%, from the $192 billion it forecast in September, and guided fourth-quarter revenue to $56.3 billion. One inconsistency to flag honestly: the release's headline bullets give operating income of $7.1 billion, up 308%, while the financial table in the same release shows $7,442 million. I cannot reconcile those two, so I would not lean on either figure.

**Dell's stock reaction was muted, and I could not verify it.** Early after-hours readings disagreed — one showed roughly +2%, another about +1% — and both are unreliable this early. If the modest reaction holds, it is worth understanding why a 103% revenue increase does not produce a 20% pop: the stock already jumped about 20% on Monday, and the market's current worry about AI servers is not whether they sell but what they earn. ## Today's lesson: margins, or why a record quarter can get punished

Tonight's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning we study the business itself, its sales and costs, rather than the behavior of its stock chart. The concept is **margin**, and today handed us a near-perfect pair of examples sitting right next to each other.

Start with the definition. Revenue is what comes in the door. Margin is what you keep out of it, expressed as a percentage. **Gross margin** is revenue minus the direct cost of making the thing, divided by revenue: if you sell a server for $100 and the parts cost $85, your gross margin is 15%. **Operating margin** goes further and also subtracts the cost of running the company — salaries, research, sales teams — so it tells you what the business earns from actually operating. Both are percentages, and that is the point. Revenue tells you how big you are. Margin tells you how much of being big you get to keep.

Here is why that mattered today. Hewlett Packard Enterprise reported a *record* quarter and beat estimates, then said the profit margins on its AI servers would be thinner than expected — and the stock fell somewhere between 12% and 15%. That is not the market being irrational. An AI server is largely a box that holds someone else's very expensive chips. The company assembling it collects a large amount of revenue and keeps a thin slice. So a company can double its revenue selling AI servers and barely add any profit, because almost every extra dollar of sales walks straight out the door to the chipmaker. Investors took HPE's warning as news about the whole category, which is why Dell fell 11% today on a day it had reported nothing at all.

Then, after the close, Dell showed the other side. Revenue up 103%, AI servers up 291% — and the number I would point you to is not either of those. It is the chief financial officer's note that adjusted operating expenses fell to 7% of revenue, the lowest in the company's 42-year history. That is a margin statement. It says Dell more than doubled what it sells without doubling what it costs to run the place, and that is precisely the thing HPE could not promise. Whether Dell's gross margin on those AI servers is genuinely healthy is a question tomorrow's analysis will chew on, and the muted after-hours reaction suggests the market has not decided.

The basketball version: revenue is shots taken, margin is your shooting percentage. A player who takes thirty shots a game is certainly involved, and the box score will look busy. But if he shoots 28%, the team is worse for every extra shot he takes. The question is never only how many possessions a company is using — it is how many points it gets per possession. When you look at any growth company from here on, find the revenue growth, then immediately look for whether the margin went up or down beside it. Growth with expanding margins is a business getting better. Growth with shrinking margins is often just a business getting busier.

One footnote worth keeping, because it was the strangest fact of the day: the S&P 500 fell 0.50% while *most of the companies inside it rose*, and the Dow gained 0.85%. That happens because the S&P 500 weights companies by size, so a handful of giant AI-linked firms can drag the index down while 300-odd smaller members quietly advance. That is a whole lesson of its own — index weighting and market breadth — and I will save it for a day when it is the main event rather than a footnote.
