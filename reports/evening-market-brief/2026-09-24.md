# Market Close Brief — Thursday, September 24, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a day of surface calm sitting on top of real stress. The S&P 500 finished essentially unchanged, the Nasdaq eked out a gain of a hundredth of a percent, and the Dow slipped for a third straight session — numbers so small that the headline reads "quiet." It wasn't. Underneath, the bond market kept coming apart: the 10-year Treasury yield pushed to about 5.14%, its highest since July 2007, and the 30-year hit 5.44%, a level not seen since 2004. A $44 billion auction of 7-year notes drew the weakest demand in decades. Oil jumped again on Middle East headlines. The single biggest storyline is the tug-of-war between those two forces — rising borrowing costs pulling stocks down, and hope that the Strait of Hormuz might reopen pulling them back up. Reuters reported that U.S. and Iranian negotiators, meeting on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly in New York, are discussing a phased deal in which Iran would reopen the strait in exchange for the U.S. lifting its naval blockade. That report is what rescued the afternoon. And one more thing worth noticing: the index was flat while most of the stocks inside it fell. Only three of the eleven S&P 500 sectors finished higher.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: 7,704.13, down 0.02%.** Basically flat. Rising yields pushed it down in the morning; the Hormuz headline pulled it back.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 51,349.98, down 161.61 points, or 0.31%.** A third straight decline. The Dow leans toward industrial and financial companies, the parts of the market that feel high interest rates most directly.
- **Nasdaq Composite: 26,939.37, up 0.01%.** A gain of about three points. Meta's jump on its Connect event roughly offset weakness in Oracle and the AI-power complex.
- **10-year Treasury yield: about 5.14%.** Highest since July 2007. Driven by strong economic data, inflation worry tied to oil, and genuinely weak demand at the government's 7-year note auction. The 30-year reached 5.443%, its highest since 2004.
- **Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate up roughly 2.3% to about $94.30 a barrel; Brent up about 2.5% to $105.69.** Prices spiked higher earlier — Brent touched $108.23 after Houthi missiles were fired at Saudi Arabia — then pulled back on the report of phased Hormuz talks. One data source put WTI slightly lower, near $93.96; the direction is not in doubt, the exact settle differs by source.
- **Bitcoin: roughly $84,300, down about 2%.** It was rejected above $87,000 and slid, with rising rate-hike expectations weighing on risk assets generally.

## Big movers and why

- **MGM Resorts International (MGM), down about 10.4%.** People Inc. — the company formerly called IAC, run by Barry Diller — withdrew its roughly $18 billion proposal to buy the 73% of MGM it does not already own, at $48.30 a share in cash. Diller said "we didn't feel the mix was coming together in the way we had hoped." When a buyout offer disappears, the premium that was priced into the stock disappears with it.
- **Darden Restaurants (DRI), down about 6.6%.** The owner of Olive Garden and LongHorn Steakhouse reported fiscal first-quarter revenue of $3.20 billion against expectations of $3.21 billion. Earnings met forecasts and the company reaffirmed its full-year guidance. A miss of roughly $10 million on $3.2 billion is a rounding error — but the stock was priced for better.
- **Bloom Energy (BE), down about 6%.** Bloom makes fuel cells and has a contract to supply up to 2.45 gigawatts of them to Oracle's New Mexico data center. When Oracle's commitment to that project wobbled, so did Bloom.
- **Oracle (ORCL), down about 4%.** Oracle sent a "force majeure" notice to a unit of Blue Owl Capital, the developer of its New Mexico AI data center, known as Project Jupiter. Force majeure is a contract clause that excuses a party from its obligations when something outside its control gets in the way — here, a 17-mile natural gas pipeline whose permits have been refused by the New Mexico State Land Office, pushing completion from August 2026 to February 2027. Oracle said the project "remains on our planned schedule"; Blue Owl said the notice "does not change the financial commitments to this multi-year project."
- **Everpure (P), up about 6.7%.** At its analyst meeting the company guided to 2028 revenue of $7 to $7.3 billion, well above the roughly $6.19 billion analysts expected.
- **Nebius Group (NBIS), up more than 6%.** Bank of America raised its revenue estimates for 2026, 2027 and 2028.
- **Charles River Laboratories (CRL), up about 5%.** The drug-research services company reaffirmed fiscal 2026 guidance and said revenue and adjusted earnings per share should land at the upper end of its earlier ranges.
- **Meta Platforms (META), up about 3.5% to around $770.** Meta Connect delivered $1,299 VR glasses, a third-generation $449 Ray-Ban Meta, and its Muse personal AI agent pushed into the glasses lineup. Analyst price-target increases landed alongside it. Not everyone was convinced — Gene Munster had expected the stock to be flat to down on a lack of concrete detail about Muse adoption.
- **Dropbox (DBX), lower** on a downgrade from Citi. I could not verify the exact percentage decline, so I am not giving one.

## Headlines that matter

**The bond auction that went badly.** The Treasury sold nearly $44 billion of 7-year notes at a high yield of 5.085% — the highest at a 7-year auction since April 1993. That mattered for three reasons. The yield came in above the 5.078% the market was showing just before the sale, which traders call a "tail" and read as buyers demanding a discount. The bid-to-cover ratio, which measures total bids against the amount offered, was 2.42 versus 2.50 last month. And indirect bidders, a category that includes foreign buyers, took 57.2% versus about 61% previously. Why this matters to a stock investor: the government has to sell this debt regardless. If buyers show up reluctantly, the price falls and the yield rises, and every other interest rate in the economy — mortgages, corporate borrowing, the rate used to value future company profits — gets dragged up with it.

**The Strait of Hormuz.** Roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil normally passes through this narrow waterway between Iran and Oman, and it has been closed for much of a nearly seven-month U.S.-Iran conflict. Reuters reported Thursday that negotiators are discussing a phased arrangement — Iran reopening the strait, the U.S. lifting its blockade — mediated by Qatar, Pakistan and Egypt. Iran has floated a regionwide ceasefire of up to 60 days. The obstacle is obvious: neither side wants to give up its leverage first, and President Trump has said he will not lift the blockade until Iran shows goodwill. For markets this is the highest-leverage story running, because oil at $94 is what is feeding the inflation fear that is feeding the bond selloff.

**Jobless claims near 57-year lows.** Initial claims for unemployment benefits were 197,000 for the week ending September 19, down 1,000, with the four-week average at 202,250. A very low number of people filing for unemployment means employers are not letting workers go. Ordinarily that is unambiguously good news. Right now it is complicated, because a tight labor market gives the Federal Reserve one more reason to worry about inflation rather than about jobs.

**New home sales rebounded.** August new home sales came in at 684,000 at an annual rate, up from 607,000, helped by builders cutting prices. That the number rose at all with mortgage rates tracking a 5.14% 10-year Treasury tells you something about how much price-cutting builders are willing to do to move inventory.

**Fed speakers leaned hawkish.** Four officials spoke: John Williams of New York, Tom Barkin of Richmond, Beth Hammack of Cleveland, and Anna Paulson of Philadelphia. Barkin said economic conditions are firming, with strength extending beyond the artificial-intelligence investment boom, and that inflation risks outweigh risks to maximum employment. "Hawkish" means leaning toward higher interest rates. When several officials say the same thing in one day, the market treats it as the committee's direction of travel, not one person's opinion.

## After the bell

**Costco Wholesale (COST) has reported, and it was a strong quarter.** For the 16-week fourth quarter ended August 30: net sales of $93.9 billion, up 11.2% from $84.4 billion. Net income of $2.998 billion, or $6.75 per diluted share, against $5.87 a year ago and analyst expectations near $6.53. Comparable sales — sales at locations open at least a year — rose 6.7% excluding the effects of gasoline prices and currency, and digital sales grew 19.8%. For the full fiscal year, net sales reached $297.2 billion, up 10.1%, with total revenue of $303.154 billion and net income of $9.226 billion, or $20.76 per share. One important footnote in Costco's own release: this quarter included a one-time benefit of $0.15 per share from IEEPA tariff refunds, partly reinvested in member value. Strip that out and the quarter was $6.60 — still a beat, but a smaller one. The stock reversed its losses from the regular session and was up roughly 1%, near $905, in early after-hours trading.

**BlackBerry (BB) has also reported** fiscal second-quarter results: total revenue of $163 million, up 26% year over year and above the high end of guidance, with adjusted EBITDA nearly doubling to $47 million. QNX, its automotive software unit, set a quarterly revenue record at $80 million, up 27%, and landed what the company called the largest design win in QNX history. Secure Communications revenue was $60.9 million, up 2%. BlackBerry raised its fiscal 2027 QNX revenue guidance to $315–325 million from $295–312 million. I could not verify tonight's after-hours share move from a reliable source, so I am not quoting one.

## What to watch tomorrow

**Friday, September 25 — a light calendar, which means the bond market gets the floor.**

- **Durable goods orders for August**, 8:30 a.m. Eastern, along with the version excluding transportation. Durable goods are items built to last three years or more — machinery, aircraft, appliances. Orders for them are a proxy for whether businesses are investing or hunkering down. The ex-transportation number is the one economists watch, because a single large aircraft order can swing the headline.

Given this week's story, the inflation-expectations figures may matter more than the sentiment number itself. The Fed watches expectations closely, on the theory that if people believe prices will keep rising, they behave in ways that make it true.

## Today's lesson: where the profit actually comes from

Today's lesson is a fundamental one — fundamental analysis meaning the study of the business underneath the stock, rather than the behavior of the price. Costco's report tonight hands us an unusually clean example of a question most people never think to ask: *where does this company's profit actually come from?*

Start with the two numbers side by side. In fiscal 2026 Costco sold $297.2 billion of merchandise. It also collected $5.907 billion in membership fees. Merchandise is 98% of the revenue; memberships are less than 2%. If you stopped there you would conclude memberships are a rounding error.

Now put membership fees next to *profit* instead of revenue. Costco's net income for the year was $9.226 billion. Membership fees were $5.907 billion of that — roughly 64%. And here is the key: membership fees are almost pure profit. The cost of collecting a renewal is close to zero, while every dollar of merchandise revenue has to pay for the merchandise first. Costco deliberately keeps its markup on goods thin — that's the whole promise to the customer — which means the $297 billion of sales throws off a surprisingly small margin. The fees do the heavy lifting.

That gap between the **revenue engine** and the **profit engine** is the concept. They are not always the same thing, and a company's business model is often better described by the second than the first. Costco is not really a retailer that sells memberships; it's a membership business that uses cheap merchandise to make the membership worth renewing. Once you see it that way, you know what to actually watch: the renewal rate. If members stop renewing, no amount of merchandise growth saves the model. (As of the third quarter, the renewal rate was 92.2% in the U.S. and Canada and 89.7% worldwide.)

The basketball version: a team's leading scorer gets the headlines and the highlight reel, and by volume he is the offense. But go look at which lineups actually outscore the opponent, and you sometimes find it's the second unit built around a defender who takes six shots a game. Points are the revenue. Point differential is the profit. When the two disagree, the second one is telling you how the team really wins — and it's the one that tells you which player you genuinely cannot afford to lose.

Try this on other businesses and it gets interesting fast. Amazon's revenue is overwhelmingly retail; a large share of its operating profit has come from cloud computing. The question "where does the profit actually come from?" is one of the fastest ways to understand a company you've never studied.
