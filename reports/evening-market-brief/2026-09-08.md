# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, September 8, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a down day, but a lopsided one, and the lopsidedness is the story. The Dow Jones Industrial Average lost about 1.2% — roughly 627 points — while the Nasdaq Composite lost only about a third of a percent. When two major indexes disagree by that much, it almost always means the damage was concentrated in a handful of specific companies rather than spread across the whole market, and that is exactly what happened. Two separate things were going on. The first was macro: Iran-aligned Houthi fighters attacked Saudi Arabian energy facilities, Brent crude oil pushed close to $100 a barrel, Canada's retaliatory tariffs on American goods took effect just after midnight, and traders spent the day nervous about what Friday's inflation report will say ahead of next week's Federal Reserve meeting. The second was company-specific and had nothing to do with any of that: Novartis, the Swiss drugmaker, had the worst day in its history after a late-stage drug trial failed, and it took a large chunk of the healthcare sector down with it. Healthcare fell more than 2% — its worst single day in over a year — while energy stocks rallied and semiconductors actually gained. If you had looked only at the Dow you would have thought the market fell apart. It didn't. It rotated.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: about 52,786, down roughly 1.18%** (a loss of about 627 points; sources give 628.18 and 626.72). The Dow's worst-in-class day is mostly explained by its heavy weighting in healthcare and financial companies, which were the two weakest groups.

The 3-year yield rose about 2 basis points to roughly 4.48% (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point). Higher oil prices push inflation expectations up, which pushes yields up.
- **Crude oil: U.S. West Texas Intermediate somewhere in the $92.40 to $93.30 range, up roughly 1% to 2%,** with Brent, the international benchmark, hitting a session high of $99.46 — a six-week high. **I could not pin down a single official WTI settlement price from two independent sources, so treat that range as approximate.** The move was entirely driven by the attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure described below.

Bitcoin has been trading like a high-risk technology stock lately — it falls when interest-rate expectations rise, because a thing that pays no interest looks worse when bonds pay more.

## Big movers and why

**Novartis (ticker NVS) fell somewhere between 9% and 11%, its worst day on record.** Novartis is one of the largest pharmaceutical companies in the world. It announced that del-desiran, an experimental treatment for myotonic dystrophy — a rare muscle-wasting disease — failed to meet its goals in a late-stage trial. That was the company's *third* clinical setback in a single week. One source estimated the decline wiped roughly $29.6 billion off the company's market value. Sources ranged from "down 9.4%" to "fell as much as 11%" during the session; I could not verify one exact closing percentage, but the direction and the severity are not in doubt.

**The damage spread to Novartis's partners and to anyone working on similar drugs.** Ionis Pharmaceuticals (ticker IONS) was hit hard — it licensed pelacarsen, a cardiovascular drug, to Novartis, and that drug's Phase 3 study also missed its primary goal. Pelacarsen successfully lowered lipoprotein(a), a blood fat linked to heart disease, but it did not produce a statistically meaningful reduction in the thing that actually matters: heart attacks, strokes, cardiovascular death, and emergency artery procedures. Sarepta Therapeutics (ticker SRPT) and Dyne Therapeutics (ticker DYN), both working on muscular dystrophy treatments, fell sharply as well. Amgen (ticker AMGN), which is in the Dow, declined too. **The specific percentages I found for Ionis (down about 13%), Sarepta (down about 15.5%) and Dyne (down about 30%) were quoted from pre-market or early trading, not the close, so treat them as indicative of scale rather than as closing figures.**

**Energy was the strongest sector in the market.** Oil producers, oilfield-services companies and refiners all rallied as crude hit six-week highs. JP Morgan upgraded Cenovus Energy (ticker CVE) and Magnolia Oil & Gas (ticker MGY) to Overweight, arguing that oil-focused exploration-and-production companies are cheap relative to natural-gas-focused ones at current prices. **I found specific closing percentages for the large oil majors, but could not confirm they were from today's session rather than an earlier one this month, so I am not quoting them.** What is well-corroborated is that energy led the market higher while everything else struggled.

**Semiconductors and optical-networking stocks gained.** Applied Optoelectronics (AAOI), Lumentum (LITE), Coherent (COHR) and Ciena (CIEN) rose on analysis suggesting that OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra model, released September 3, will drive demand for very high-speed 800G and 1.6T optical modules — the components that move data between the computers inside an artificial-intelligence data center. Nuclear and small-modular-reactor names also outperformed, including Oklo (OKLO), NuScale Power (SMR) and Centrus Energy (LEU) — the AI-power-demand trade.

**Financials were the second-weakest group after healthcare,** consistent with a day when the market was worrying about tariffs slowing the economy.

## Headlines that matter

**Houthi fighters attacked Saudi Arabian energy facilities, and oil went to a six-week high.** Iran-aligned Houthi militants launched drones and ballistic missiles at Saudi Aramco installations and other energy infrastructure across southern Saudi Arabia, wounding at least 73 people. Among the targets was the Jazan refinery, which processes about 400,000 barrels a day. Saudi Arabia's Energy Ministry said operations at several oil facilities and utilities were suspended while firefighters worked to contain fires. Saudi Arabia has vowed to retaliate. *Why it matters:* this is the third distinct escalation in the region in about two weeks, layered on top of the ongoing U.S.-Iran exchanges and thinned-out traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. Oil is not just a commodity here — it is an inflation input. Every dollar on the price of crude eventually shows up in gasoline, diesel, freight costs and airfares, which show up in the Consumer Price Index, which is the number the Federal Reserve is staring at right now. Some analysts quoted today warned oil could exceed $120 if the attacks and shipping disruptions persist. There was one offsetting piece of news: Iran said it is close to a deal with Oman to manage traffic through the Strait.

**Canada's retaliatory tariffs on U.S. goods took effect just after midnight.** Prime Minister Mark Carney's counter-tariffs impose duties ranging from 15% to 50% on American products including steel, dairy, agricultural equipment, furniture, clothing, electronics and pulp and paper. **Sources disagree on the size: one figure reported is roughly $20 billion of U.S. goods, another is $27.6 billion — the gap is likely U.S. versus Canadian dollars, but I could not confirm that.** This follows the collapse of trade talks in late August and 50% U.S. tariffs on some Canadian products imposed Saturday. Carney said U.S. demands went too far: "They asked too much and offered too little." *Why it matters:* Canada is one of the largest U.S. trading partners, and a tariff is a tax paid at the border that generally gets passed along in the price of the finished good. Tariffs are, mechanically, inflationary in the short run. That is the second inflation story of the day, arriving on the same morning as the first.

**The Federal Reserve is in its communications blackout, and the meeting is now genuinely live.** The Fed decides on interest rates September 15–16. No Fed official can speak publicly until after that decision, so the only new information between now and then is economic data. Context you need: last Friday's jobs report came in unexpectedly strong, which pushed traders toward pricing a rate *hike* rather than a cut — a real regime change after a long stretch of assuming the next move was down. Inflation has been running well above the Fed's 2% target. *Why it matters:* with no Fed speeches to interpret, Thursday's and Friday's inflation reports carry all the weight. This is why a barrel of oil moved the stock market today.

**The inflation data arrives at the end of this week.** The Producer Price Index for August lands Thursday, September 10 at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, and the Consumer Price Index for August lands Friday, September 11 at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. *Why it matters:* PPI measures prices at the wholesale level — what businesses pay — and often previews where consumer prices head next. CPI is the headline number. These are the last major inputs before the Fed decides.

**A Treasury 3-year note auction was scheduled for 1 p.m. Eastern today.** **I could not verify the auction results — whether demand was strong or weak — from a reliable source, so I am not going to characterize it.** The 3-year yield did finish about 2 basis points higher, which is consistent with an unremarkable day at the short end of the bond market.

## Today's lesson: where the profit came from, or operating income versus net income

Here is a question worth asking about every company you ever look at: *when this business made money, what did it actually do to make it?*

GameStop's report tonight is close to a perfect teaching example, because it has three different profit numbers pointing in three different directions.

Start with **operating income**. That is the profit a company earns from doing the thing it exists to do — revenue from selling stuff, minus the cost of that stuff, minus the cost of running the business (stores, salaries, marketing). It deliberately excludes anything financial: interest, investment gains, one-time items, taxes. GameStop's operating income was $160.2 million, which the company says is the best second quarter in its history. That is a real accomplishment and it has a clear cause. Collectibles — trading cards, figures, that whole category — grew 57% and now account for 45.1% of sales, up from 23.4% a year ago. GameStop is quietly turning into a different company than the one people argue about online.

Now look at **net income**, the bottom line, the number after everything. GameStop pre-announced that it expected somewhere between $290 million and $310 million, up from $168.6 million a year earlier. That looks like enormous growth. But roughly $238 million of it came from gains on its stake in eBay stock and related derivative positions, partly offset by a $75 million loss on digital assets. GameStop is sitting on about 43.4 million eBay shares worth roughly $4.95 billion. In other words, most of the bottom-line "profit" this quarter came from a stock going up, not from selling anything to a customer.

And then the third number, the one that cuts the other way: **revenue fell 18.7%**. The company sold far less than it did a year ago. Store closures and the sale of the French business explain part of that, but the business is unmistakably shrinking on the top line even as the profitable part of it improves.

None of those three numbers is a lie. They are just answers to different questions. Operating income asks "is the actual business working?" Net income asks "did the company end up with more money?" Revenue asks "how big is it?" Analysts use the phrase **earnings quality** to describe how much of a company's profit comes from the repeatable, operational stuff versus from one-time or financial windfalls. High-quality earnings come from operations, because operations tend to happen again next quarter. A gain on a stock holding may not — and can reverse just as fast.

The basketball version: a guy finishes with 28 points and you think, great game. Then you look closer and 18 of those came in the last four minutes of a blowout against the other team's bench. He did score 28. It's on the stat sheet and nobody can take it away. But if you're trying to predict what he does in a close game next week, the 10 points he scored while the starters were on the floor tell you far more than the 18 that came in garbage time. Same box score, two very different pieces of information — and knowing which is which is most of the skill.

Practical takeaway when you read any earnings report: find the income statement and read *down* it rather than jumping to the bottom. Revenue, then gross profit, then operating income, and only then net income. If the bottom line grew much faster than operating income did, something below the operating line did the work — and your next question should be whether that something is likely to happen again.
