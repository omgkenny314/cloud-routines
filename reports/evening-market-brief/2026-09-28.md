# Market Close Brief — Monday, September 28, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

A down day, and a nervous one, but not a violent one. All three major indexes lost ground in a slow bleed rather than a panic, and the storyline was the same one that has driven this market for two weeks: oil and interest rates. Over the weekend President Trump rejected an Iranian offer of a seven-day truce that would have reopened the Strait of Hormuz, and crude jumped at the open. Higher oil revives inflation worries, inflation worries push Treasury yields up, and higher yields pull money away from stocks — that chain reaction is essentially the whole day. The 10-year Treasury yield touched a fresh 19-year high. What makes today interesting is that the oil rally did not hold: reports emerged that Saudi Arabia has restored its East-West pipeline to roughly 3.5 million barrels a day, which routes crude around Hormuz, and oil gave back most of its gains by the close. Stocks did not get the memo, and closed near their lows anyway. The single biggest individual story was separate from all of it — Boeing fell hard after the Federal Aviation Administration said it will not certify the 737 Max 10 until it finishes assessing a newly flagged software problem.

- **Bitcoin: about $83,100, down roughly 1.4% over 24 hours.** A strong dollar, higher bond yields, and risk-off sentiment all work against an asset that pays no interest. The broader crypto market fell harder than bitcoin did.

## Big movers and why

- **Boeing (BA), down sharply.** The FAA said it will not certify the 737 Max 10 — the largest version of Boeing's best-selling narrow-body jet — until it determines whether a recently flagged software problem is a safety-of-flight issue. The glitch involves the vertical navigation system in a specific situation: when a plane aborts a landing approach, goes around, and lines up to land again. The Max 10 was close to final approval with deliveries expected soon, and it is central to Boeing's recovery plan, so a delay of unknown length is a real financial event, not a headline scare. On the size of the drop: reported figures range from about 6.2% to nearly 7%, with one source citing a last trade of $185.85. I could not pin down a single closing figure that every source agreed on, so read it as "down roughly 6% to 7%."
- **GE Aerospace (GE), lower.** GE builds the LEAP engines that power the 737 Max, so it got pulled into the same story. Reported declines ranged from under 1% at midday to about 3%, and I could not verify the closing figure.
- **Nvidia (NVDA), up about 2.3%.** One of the few large gainers. Nvidia's board authorized an additional $150 billion for share repurchases, bringing the remaining authorization to $235 billion — the largest single buyback increase any company has ever announced, surpassing Apple's $110 billion authorization in 2024. Nvidia expects to work through that total by fiscal 2028. This is today's lesson, so hold that thought.
- **Gen Digital (GEN), down roughly 6%.** The consumer cybersecurity company (Norton, Avast, LifeLock) fell on investor unease about its proposed acquisition of GoDaddy — specifically about what the deal does to Gen's balance sheet and strategic focus. I found this in only one source, so treat the exact percentage as unconfirmed.
- **Charter Communications (CHTR)** reportedly traded at its lowest level since April 2013. Also a single-source item, but worth noting: a stock at a 13-year low is a stock the market has fundamentally re-rated, not one having a bad day.
- **Oil tankers.** Companies that ship crude have been among 2026's biggest winners, because disruption at Hormuz forces oil onto longer voyages and drives up the daily rate for a tanker. Frontline (FRO) reported its strongest quarterly earnings in more than two decades earlier this year and paid shareholders a $2.61 regular dividend plus a $0.80 special dividend today. I could not verify Frontline's exact move today, so I am giving you the context rather than a number.

## Headlines that matter

**Trump rejected Iran's seven-day truce offer.** Tehran proposed the halt at last week's UN General Assembly, and it would have included reopening the Strait of Hormuz. Trump turned it down but said he expects U.S. negotiators to hold more talks with Iran this week. *Why it matters:* Hormuz is a narrow waterway that a very large share of the world's seaborne oil passes through. As long as its status is in question, oil carries a "risk premium" — a price cushion for the possibility of supply being cut off. That premium feeds directly into inflation, and inflation is what currently ties the Federal Reserve's hands. This is the pipe through which a geopolitical story reaches your retirement account.

**Saudi Arabia restored its East-West pipeline to about 3.5 million barrels a day.** The pipeline, damaged in a drone attack earlier in September, carries crude across Saudi Arabia to the Red Sea — bypassing Hormuz entirely. *Why it matters:* this is the counterweight to the Iran story, and it is why oil finished the day near flat instead of up 4%. When you see a market reverse hard intraday, there is usually a second piece of news like this one. Learning to look for it is most of what separates reading the tape from reacting to headlines.

**The 10-year Treasury yield hit a 19-year high.** Three forces are pushing it: inflation that is not coming down, a very large volume of new government bonds being issued, and an AI-driven investment boom soaking up capital. *Why it matters:* the 10-year yield is the price of money for the whole economy. It sets mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and — crucially — the rate against which future company profits get discounted back to today's value. It also means a risk-free government bond now pays over 5%, which is real competition for stocks.

**The Federal Reserve's stance.** For context on where this all sits: on September 16 the Fed raised its target rate by a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00%, its first increase since 2023, in a unanimous 12-0 vote. Chair Kevin Warsh has flagged that underlying inflation is not slowing. The Fed's own projections put 2026 headline inflation at 3.7% and core at 3.4% on its preferred PCE measure, and 16 of 18 officials see at least one more quarter-point hike this year. *Why it matters:* it explains why an oil spike is scarier now than it would have been a year ago. The Fed is already leaning toward tightening; expensive oil makes the case for it stronger.

## Today's lesson: buybacks, or what happens when a company shrinks the number of slices

Today's lesson is a fundamental one — fundamental analysis meaning the study of the business underneath the stock, rather than the behavior of the price. Nvidia handed us the largest example in corporate history, so let's use it.

A **share buyback**, also called a repurchase, is a company using its own cash to buy its own stock on the open market and retire it. Those shares stop existing. Nvidia's board just authorized an additional $150 billion of this, bringing its remaining authorization to $235 billion — more than the entire market value of most companies in the S&P 500.

Here is why it matters, and it comes down to division. **Earnings per share**, or EPS, is the single most quoted measure of a company's profitability, and it is just total profit divided by the number of shares outstanding. A company can raise EPS two ways: make the top number bigger, or make the bottom number smaller. Buybacks make the bottom number smaller. If a company earns $10 billion across 10 billion shares, that is $1.00 per share. Retire a billion shares and the same $10 billion of profit becomes $1.11 per share — an 11% "increase" in earnings per share with no improvement whatsoever in the actual business. Think of a pizza: buying back stock does not make the pizza bigger, it means fewer people are at the table, so each remaining slice is larger. Two weeks ago we covered dilution, where a company issues new shares and every existing owner's slice gets thinner. A buyback is that same mechanism running backwards.

This is where the analytical skill comes in, and it cuts both ways. Buybacks are genuinely good for continuing shareholders when a company has more cash than it has good projects to spend it on, and when management believes the stock is cheap relative to what the business is worth — that is exactly how Nvidia framed it, citing cash generation strong enough to both invest in AI technology and return capital. But the same tool can flatter a struggling company. A business whose actual profits are flat can post rising EPS for years by steadily shrinking its share count, and a casual investor reading only the EPS line would never notice. It is like a basketball player whose points-per-game average climbs not because he is scoring more, but because the coach cut the roster and he is simply taking more shots. Same player, better-looking stat.

So the habit to build is this: whenever you see earnings per share growing, ask whether total profit — the raw dollar figure, called net income — is growing too, and at a similar rate. If net income is up 20% and EPS is up 22%, buybacks added a modest boost to real growth. If net income is flat and EPS is up 12%, you are looking at arithmetic, not performance. Both are legitimate things for a company to do with its money. They are not the same thing, and the EPS number alone will not tell you which one you have.
