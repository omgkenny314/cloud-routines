# Market Close Brief — Friday, October 2, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a classic "bad news is good news" session. The September jobs report landed before the open and it was weak — the economy added only 29,000 jobs when forecasters were looking for somewhere in the 80,000 to 90,000 range, and the unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2% from 4.1%. In most years a soft labor market would scare the stock market. Not this year. Remember the situation the Federal Reserve is in: it is *raising* rates right now, not cutting them, because inflation has stayed too high. It hiked in September, and its own projections pointed to one more increase before the end of 2026. A weak jobs report makes that next hike much harder to justify, and traders repriced it fast — the odds of an October hike collapsed to roughly 17% from near 70% on one prediction market just a week ago. Lower expected interest rates are worth the most to the companies whose value sits furthest out in the future, which is why technology led and the Nasdaq nearly doubled the Dow's gain. The single biggest storyline, then, was not the jobs number itself but what the jobs number did to the Fed conversation.

## Headlines that matter

**The September jobs report.** Payrolls rose 29,000 versus forecasts near 84,000 to 90,000, and prior months were revised *down* by about 60,000 — revisions matter because they change the trend, not just one month. The unemployment rate rose to 4.2%. Here is the subtlety: that increase came mostly from *more people entering the labor force*, not mass layoffs. The labor force grew by 485,000 and the participation rate rose to 61.8%, the highest since May. More job seekers with few new jobs to fill still means a cooling market, but it is a different kind of weakness than firings. Average hourly earnings were up about 3.0% from a year ago — below the 3.4% annual inflation rate reported for August, which means the average worker's paycheck is losing ground to prices in real terms.

**Why the Fed angle dominates everything.** The Fed raised its benchmark rate to a target range of 3.75% to 4.00% on September 16 — its first increase since 2023 — to fight inflation driven largely by soaring oil prices. Chair Kevin Warsh said inflation has been "too high for too long," and the committee's projections showed one more hike penciled in for this year. That is the frame for today: the market is not debating how fast the Fed will *cut*, it is debating whether the Fed hikes *again*. After the jobs report, the CME FedWatch tool showed about a 17% chance of a quarter-point October hike, down from roughly 36% a week earlier, and the Kalshi prediction market showed about 18%, down from nearly 70%. A hike that is no longer expected is, functionally, a tax cut for stock valuations.

**Oil and the Middle East, still the wildcard.** Crude eased today but remains near multi-year highs on Strait of Hormuz supply disruption. This is the thread that ties everything together: expensive oil feeds inflation, inflation is what has the Fed hiking, and Fed hiking is what pushed Treasury yields to multi-decade highs. Watch oil and you are watching the Fed indirectly.

**A quiet but important bond-market subplot.** Yields finished higher despite soft employment data, and recent Treasury auctions have drawn lukewarm demand. When the government has to pay up to find buyers for its debt, that raises borrowing costs across the economy regardless of what the Fed does.

## Today's lesson: buybacks, share count, and earnings per share

Today's lesson is a fundamental one — fundamental analysis meaning the study of the business itself, its earnings and its balance sheet, rather than the behavior of its price. And our example is Nvidia, which last week authorized the largest share repurchase increase in American corporate history and hit a record close today.

Start with the number nearly every stock story is built on: **earnings per share**, usually written as EPS. It is simple division. Take the company's profit for a period and divide it by the number of shares that exist. If a company earns $1 billion and has 1 billion shares, EPS is $1.00. That per-share figure is what analysts forecast, what "beats" and "misses" are measured against, and what gets multiplied by a valuation ratio to argue a stock is cheap or expensive.

Now notice that the division has two sides. Profit is the numerator. Share count is the denominator. A company can raise EPS by earning more money — the hard way — or by reducing the number of shares. A **share buyback**, also called a repurchase, is the company using its own cash to buy its stock on the open market and retire it, permanently shrinking the share count. Nvidia's board added **$150 billion** to its repurchase authorization, bringing its remaining capacity to roughly $235 billion through fiscal 2028. Analysts estimate that could shrink the share count by about 4.2% and add roughly 1.6% to EPS by 2028. Think of a team that scores 100 points a night. If the roster shrinks from ten rotation players to nine, points per player goes up — even though the team is not scoring one additional point. That is the denominator doing the work.

This is where you want to stay clear-eyed, and it cuts both ways. A buyback is real money returned to shareholders, and when a company genuinely has more cash than it has good projects to fund, returning it is sensible. But the EPS it adds is arithmetic, not operating improvement — the business did not get better at its business. So when you see a company's EPS rising, it is worth asking which side of the fraction moved. Two more things to hold onto: an **authorization** is permission from the board, not a promise, so companies routinely buy back less than the headline number and on their own timetable; and buybacks are funded with cash, which is cash not spent on factories, research or paying down debt. In Nvidia's case the striking fact is the scale relative to the effect — $150 billion of permission, for a projected 1.6% EPS lift — which tells you something about how enormous the company has become. The word you will see in deal coverage for this idea is **accretive**, which simply means "increases EPS." It appeared today in the onsemi–Synaptics announcement too. It describes the math, not the merit.
