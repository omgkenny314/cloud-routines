# Market Close Brief — Friday, September 11, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Stocks finally caught a break. After four straight losing sessions, all three major indexes rose about 1% on Friday, and the reason was not the inflation report everyone was waiting for — it was oil. Iranian state media said Tehran will sit down with Gulf states in Oman next week to talk about the Strait of Hormuz, and that single headline knocked the war premium out of crude. West Texas Intermediate, the U.S. oil benchmark, fell back below $100 a barrel after breaking above it earlier in the week. Meanwhile the August Consumer Price Index landed almost exactly where economists expected, with one small blemish: the "core" reading came in a tenth of a percentage point hot. Traders read that blemish as confirmation and pushed the odds of a Federal Reserve interest-rate hike next Wednesday to somewhere between 86% and 90%, up from about 72% on Thursday.

So the storyline of the day is a genuine oddity worth sitting with: the market rallied on a day when the odds of a rate hike went *up*. That happened because the thing scaring investors this week was not the Fed as such — it was oil driving inflation higher in an uncontrolled way. Cheaper crude took the scariest version of that story off the table, and stocks rallied even as the near-term Fed outcome got tighter. It still was not enough to save the week: the S&P 500 fell 0.8%, the Nasdaq fell 0.7%, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1.6% — its worst week since March.

## The numbers

**S&P 500: closed at 7,656.98, up 0.86%.** Snapped a four-day losing streak. Ten of the eleven sectors finished higher, led by communication services, up about 1.9%.

**Dow Jones Industrial Average: closed at 52,573.29, up 509.19 points, or 0.98%.** The four-day slide it ended was its longest since late April.

**Nasdaq Composite: closed at 26,333.04, up 0.96%.** Technology and artificial-intelligence hardware names did the heavy lifting.

**10-year Treasury yield: roughly 4.91% to 4.95%, down slightly on the day.** Sources I checked disagreed on the exact figure — one reported a drop of about 3.4 basis points to 4.91%, another a drop of about 1 basis point to 4.955%, so treat the precise number as unconfirmed. The direction is not in dispute: yields eased after the inflation report came in close to expectations, a pause in a week-long global bond selloff that had pushed this yield up roughly 16 basis points and near multi-year highs. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.

**Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate settled at $99.66, down about 2.8%; Brent, the international benchmark, settled at $104.61, down about 2.8%.** Both fell on the Oman diplomacy headline. Even after that drop, WTI still gained roughly 9% on the week and Brent roughly 8.7%, which tells you how violent the week was.

**Bitcoin: traded roughly $78,000 to $79,000 late in the afternoon, up about 2% to 3% from the morning.** It opened near $76,536, its lowest in about two weeks, then spiked as high as $79,837 after the inflation report matched forecasts, before settling back. Bitcoin trades around the clock, so there is no official close — treat any single figure as a snapshot. The swing triggered more than $732 million in liquidations across crypto.

## Big movers and why

**Hewlett Packard Enterprise (ticker HPE) closed at $61.49, up about 11%** — its strongest session of 2026, adding roughly $7 billion of market value in a day. **Dell Technologies (ticker DELL) rose about 11% as well**, and **Super Micro Computer (ticker SMCI) gained about 7%.** These three moved together for the same reason: Oracle, reporting Thursday night, guided full-year capital spending to $90 billion to $95 billion. Capital spending is the money a company lays out on buildings and equipment. When Oracle says it will spend $90 billion-plus on data centers, that is not just an Oracle number — it is a forward order signal for every company that sells the servers, networking gear and cooling systems that go inside. Dell got a second boost from RBC starting coverage of the stock with an "outperform" rating, citing its free cash flow and its position in AI infrastructure.

**Oracle (ticker ORCL) had a wild session.** It traded as high as $167.67 and as low as $150.65 — a swing of about 11% from top to bottom. The morning pop came from a clean quarterly beat: $1.92 in adjusted earnings per share against $1.74 expected, on $19.35 billion of revenue. The fade came as investors digested the cost side of that same story: the enormous spending and borrowing required to deliver on its contracts. I could not verify Oracle's exact closing change — reports I found ranged from roughly plus 7% to roughly flat — so I am not going to give you a number I cannot stand behind. The intraday range, though, is well documented, and we will come back to it in today's lesson.

**Adobe (ticker ADBE) fell about 2.4% to close at $248.83.** Adobe reported Thursday night and actually beat: record quarterly revenue of $6.76 billion and adjusted earnings of $6.13 per share, both about 1% above expectations. What hurt was the outlook — the midpoint of its revenue guidance for the current quarter came in just short of what analysts wanted. Adobe also announced a leadership handoff: longtime chief executive Shantanu Narayen will move to executive chair on December 1, with Anil Chakravarthy, who currently runs the customer experience business, becoming president and chief executive.

**Kroger (ticker KR), the supermarket chain, closed at $58.39 after opening at $55.81.** It had been quoted down 2.6% before the opening bell. The company beat on profit — $1.05 per share on $34.6 billion of revenue — but cut its sales outlook, and cut it meaningfully: it now expects identical sales excluding fuel to grow just 0.2% to 0.8% this year, down from 1% to 2%. Identical sales measure stores open at least a year, so the number strips out growth that comes merely from opening new stores. About 140 basis points of that cut comes from the Inflation Reduction Act, which lowered what Medicare beneficiaries pay for prescription drugs and therefore lowered Kroger's pharmacy revenue. Crucially, Kroger left its full-year profit guidance unchanged at $5.10 to $5.30 per share, and the stock climbed all day.

**Skyworks Solutions (ticker SWKS) rose more than 8%** after saying at a Goldman Sachs conference that its acquisition of Qorvo should close before year end — removing a large piece of deal uncertainty.

**Vicor (ticker VICR), which makes power components, rose about 11%** after announcing it is buying two sites in New Hampshire to expand chip manufacturing.

**Shopify (ticker SHOP) gained nearly 4%** after Bernstein started coverage with an outperform rating.

**GameStop (ticker GME) rose more than 3%** after chief executive Ryan Cohen disclosed he had personally bought one million shares at an average price of $20.375.

## Headlines that matter

**The August Consumer Price Index was the main event, and it was a near-perfect "in line" report with one hot corner.** Headline prices rose 0.4% from July, the biggest monthly jump in three months, leaving the annual rate at 3.4% — unchanged from July and exactly what economists forecast. Gasoline rose 3.9% and accounted for more than a third of the entire monthly increase, which is the oil story arriving on your grocery receipt. Core CPI — the version that strips out food and energy, because those two swing wildly and can disguise the underlying trend — rose 0.3% for the month against a 0.2% forecast. That is the blemish. But the annual core rate actually *fell* to 2.4% from 2.5%, its lowest since 2021.

Why it matters: the Fed cares more about core than headline, because core is the part it can actually influence. A tenth of a percentage point sounds trivial, and in isolation it is. Coming one business day before the Fed's meeting begins, after a hot producer-price report on Thursday, it was enough to tip the balance. Odds of a quarter-point rate hike next Wednesday jumped from about 72% to roughly 86-90%, depending on which source and what time of day you check.

**The Federal Reserve meets Tuesday and Wednesday, September 15 and 16, with the decision Wednesday afternoon.** Officials are in their blackout period — the stretch before a meeting when they do not speak publicly — through Thursday, September 17. That means no Fed commentary between now and the decision, so the market has to sit with what it already knows. Worth pausing on how unusual this situation is: for most of the last few years, the debate was about how fast the Fed would *cut* rates. The market is now pricing a near-certain *hike*.

**Oil retreated on diplomacy, not on supply.** Iranian state media said Tehran will meet Gulf states in Oman next week regarding the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes. That was enough to pull crude back below $100. Nothing has actually been resolved; a meeting was announced. That is worth remembering, because a week of escalation added roughly 9% to the oil price and a single headline took about 3% back. Oil is currently the main channel through which geopolitics becomes inflation becomes Fed policy becomes your portfolio, which is why a Middle East headline moves your index fund.

**Bond yields eased but remain the thing to watch.** The 10-year Treasury yield had been climbing all week toward 5%, part of a global government-bond selloff, and near its highest since 2023. It backed off modestly after the inflation data. Higher yields do two things to stocks: they make bonds a more competitive alternative, and they raise borrowing costs for every company that funds itself with debt — which very much includes the companies building AI data centers.

## After the bell

It is early in the after-hours session, and Friday evening is the quietest slot on the market calendar. Companies overwhelmingly schedule results for Monday through Thursday, when there are trading sessions on either side to absorb the news.

As of this writing I found no significant earnings reports or major after-hours moves since the 3:00 p.m. Central close, and no notable companies scheduled to report tonight. Kroger, the one recognizable name on today's earnings calendar, reported before the opening bell this morning rather than after the close.

If anything does break this evening, it is most likely to be a corporate filing rather than an earnings release — Friday afternoons are when companies quietly file disclosures they would rather not have a full trading day to digest. I could not verify any such filing as of this writing.

## Today's lesson: the four prices of a trading day, and why the close is the one that counts

Every stock quote you hear on the news is one number. But a single trading day actually produces four prices, and learning to hear all four instead of one is one of the highest-return habits in technical analysis — the study of price behavior itself, as opposed to studying the business underneath.

The four are: the **open**, the first price traded at 8:30 a.m. Central; the **high**, the most anyone paid all day; the **low**, the least anyone accepted; and the **close**, the final price at 3:00 p.m. Central. The distance from the high to the low is called the **range**. Those four numbers, taken together, describe not just where a stock ended up but the argument it had with itself along the way.

Today handed us two textbook examples. Oracle opened strong after good earnings, ran up to $167.67, sank all the way to $150.65, and finished somewhere in between. That is roughly an 11% range in a single session for a company worth hundreds of billions of dollars. A range that wide is not noise — it is a measurable, public disagreement. One set of buyers looked at Oracle's results and saw enormous contracted future revenue. Another set looked at the same report and saw enormous spending and debt required to deliver it. Both groups traded aggressively, and neither won decisively. When you see a wide range, that is what you are looking at: high conviction on both sides, low agreement about value.

Now Kroger, which is the more instructive example. Before the opening bell, Kroger was quoted down 2.6% at $55.48 — it had just cut its annual sales forecast, and the first reaction was negative. It opened at $55.81. It traded as high as $59.48. It closed at $58.39. So the stock started the day lower than where it had finished Thursday and ended the day higher. If you only heard the premarket quote, you learned the opposite of what actually happened. The opening print reflected the first, fastest, thinnest reaction to a headline. The close reflected a full day of people reading the actual report — and noticing that while Kroger cut its *sales* outlook, it left its *profit* outlook untouched, and that a big chunk of the sales cut came from a change in prescription drug law rather than from shoppers abandoning the stores.

This is why traders say the close is the most important price of the day. The open is a reaction. The close is a verdict — it is where the market is willing to hold a position overnight, with real money at risk and no ability to trade until morning. Here is the basketball version: the open is the score after the opening tip-off run, when one team has hit two quick threes and the building is loud. The high and the low are the biggest lead each team held. The close is the final score. Announcers talk about runs; standings only record finals.

The practical habit is simple. When you hear a stock is "up 3% today," ask two follow-up questions. Where did it open — did it gain that 3% steadily, or did it open up 8% and give most of it back? And how wide was the range — did it trade in a quiet 1% band, or did it whip through 11% like Oracle? Same headline number, completely different stories underneath. One caution: a wide range by itself is neither bullish nor bearish. It tells you conviction and disagreement are both high, which usually means the next few sessions matter more than usual. It does not tell you which side is right.
