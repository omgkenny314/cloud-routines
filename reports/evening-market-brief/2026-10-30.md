# Market Close Brief — Friday, October 30, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was an ugly, broad, tech-led sell-off that closed out the month on a sour note. The Nasdaq Composite had its worst single day since early June, falling nearly 3%, and roughly 70% of all U.S. stocks fell. The single biggest storyline was Intel: it reported a *better* third quarter than analysts expected, then told investors the current quarter would be far worse than they thought, and the stock collapsed about 29% — reportedly its worst day since 1974. But Intel was only the loudest voice in a bigger conversation that has gripped the market all week. Investors are no longer cheering the enormous sums big technology companies are spending on artificial intelligence; they are starting to ask when that spending turns into cash. Alphabet took a roughly 12% beating on Thursday on that question, Amazon took its turn today, and the worry spread to anything connected to AI, chips, or cloud computing. Add a $6.3 billion hack at the crypto exchange Binance, oil jumping on a tanker attack in the Strait of Hormuz, and gold falling to a one-year low, and you get a day where almost nothing worked. Note the shape of it, though: the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 1.1% while the Nasdaq fell about 2.9%. This was not a panic about the economy. It was a repricing of one specific theme.

## The numbers

A note on precision: for today's closes, different reputable outlets printed figures a few points apart, and in one case a different percentage. Where that happened I give the range and say so rather than pick a favorite.

- **S&P 500 — about 7,580, down about 1.82%.** This is the broadest of the three, 500 large U.S. companies. Three sources put it between 7,578.97 and 7,581.40, with two independently reporting -1.82%, so I am confident in that. It fell because its biggest members are technology companies, and technology was where the selling was.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average — about 51,770 to 51,786, down roughly 1.1%** (one source reported a loss of 579.15 points, or -1.11%). The Dow holds only 30 companies and leans toward industrials, banks, and health care, so it had less exposure to today's damage. One outlet printed -1.31% instead; I could not reconcile that with the others, so treat the exact figure as unsettled and the rough size — down about one percent — as solid.

- **Nasdaq Composite — about 25,437 to 25,442, down about 2.86%.** This is the tech-heavy index, and this was its biggest one-day percentage drop since June 5. It fell hardest for the simple reason that it holds the most of what was being sold: chipmakers, cloud companies, and anything AI-adjacent.

- **10-year Treasury yield — about 4.91%, down roughly 6 basis points on the day.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so that is a move of about 0.06%. Yields fall when bond prices rise, and bond prices rose because frightened money moves into government bonds on days like this. One source notes the 10-year fell about 36 basis points over the month, its largest monthly decline since December 2023.

- **Crude oil — Brent at about $117.64 a barrel, up about $2.76 or 2.4%.** Brent is the international benchmark. A tanker caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz after explosive devices were attached to its hull, reviving fears of a U.S.-Iran escalation that could choke off supply through that waterway. Brent is up roughly 14% for October. The same source put West Texas Intermediate, the U.S. benchmark, at $104.12, but I could only verify that from one place, and a $13 gap between the two benchmarks is unusually wide — so hold the WTI figure loosely.

- **Bitcoin — about $77,446, down about 4.1%,** dipping below $77,000 for the first time in more than a month. Two things hit it at once: the Binance hack, now put at about $6.3 billion and described as the largest crypto theft on record, and the same risk-off mood that hit stocks. Roughly $1.57 billion of leveraged crypto positions were force-sold.

- **Worth adding, though you did not ask: gold fell about 3.1% to roughly $3,897,** a one-year low, and is now about 30% below its January record near $5,595. Gold usually rises on scary days. It did not today, which tells you the market is more worried about interest rates staying high than about an economic accident.

## Big movers and why

- **Intel (ticker INTC), the chipmaker, closed down about 29.27%** — the widest single-session move in the large-cap universe and, by several accounts, its worst day since 1974. The confusing part is that the quarter it just finished was strong: adjusted earnings of $0.52 per share against $0.40 expected, a 30% beat, on revenue of $16.51 billion, up 21% from a year ago, with data-center and AI revenue up nearly 80% to $7.3 billion. What broke the stock was the *next* quarter. Intel guided fourth-quarter revenue to $15 billion to $16 billion against roughly $17 billion expected, and earnings of $0.25 per share against $0.43 expected. The chief financial officer said business had been slowing for several weeks because high memory-chip prices are making computers expensive enough that people are not buying them, and he did not expect improvement until at least the second half of 2027. Context that makes the fall less mysterious: the stock had nearly quadrupled from the start of 2026 to a record in late June. Today's lesson is built on this report, so hold onto it.

- **Reddit (ticker RDDT), which runs the online discussion site, fell about 29%** (one reading had it 29.35% lower just before the close, another 25% at midday). Same pattern as Intel in miniature: it beat third-quarter expectations and then guided fourth-quarter revenue below what Wall Street wanted.

- **Twilio (ticker TWLO), which sells the software other companies use to send texts and make calls inside their apps, fell about 17% to 19%.** Again: a good quarter, even a raised full-year outlook, undone by a weak forecast for organic revenue growth — "organic" meaning growth from the existing business rather than from buying other companies.

- **Coinbase (ticker COIN), the cryptocurrency exchange, fell about 13%,** hit from two directions — the Binance hack spooked everyone in crypto, and Coinbase's own results showed weaker trading revenue. Alongside it, Robinhood (ticker HOOD) fell about 8%, Circle (ticker CRCL) about 8.5%, and Strategy (formerly MicroStrategy, ticker MSTR) about 8%.

- **Amazon (ticker AMZN) fell, but I could not verify by how much.** Intraday reports ranged from about -1.4% to about -3%, after a premarket indication near -7%, and I found no source with a confirmed closing figure. The story is clear even if the number is not: third-quarter earnings of $1.89 per share missed, and attention landed on spending rather than growth. Amazon kept its 2026 capital expenditure plan at about $220 billion and reported negative free cash flow for the second straight quarter. The bullish side was genuinely strong — Amazon Web Services, its cloud business, grew 39% to $46 billion, its fastest pace in nearly five years, with $578 billion of contracted future revenue in backlog, more than double a year ago, and the CEO saying he expects AWS growth above 40% in 2027 and 2028. The market chose to look at the cash going out.

- **On the winning side: Regeneron Pharmaceuticals (ticker REGN) rose about 11.6% to 13.3%** on a third-quarter beat and a raised full-year outlook. **Rivian Automotive (ticker RIVN), the electric truck maker, rose roughly 5% to 6%** on results that beat estimates, record vehicle deliveries, and an improved full-year outlook, though I could not confirm its exact close. **CareDx (ticker CDNA), a transplant-diagnostics company, rose about 22%** after raising its 2026 revenue forecast.

## Headlines that matter

**The AI spending question is now the market's main argument.** This matters more than any single earnings report. For two years, investors rewarded technology companies for announcing bigger AI budgets, treating the spending as proof of ambition. This week that flipped. Alphabet fell more than 12% Thursday partly on cash burn; Amazon was pressed today on $220 billion of capital spending and negative free cash flow; Meta guided 2026 capital spending to $135 billion to $145 billion. One analysis framed the week as capital spending exceeding cash generation by $26.4 billion across the group, and the Nasdaq 100 is reported to be on track to shed roughly $2 trillion of market value this month. The reason it matters: capital spending is real money leaving the business today in exchange for profits that are promised later. As long as investors believe the later profits, they tolerate the spending. The moment they start demanding evidence, the same announcement that used to lift a stock starts to sink it. Microsoft, which reported $18.6 billion of positive free cash flow, was treated noticeably better — which tells you exactly what the market is now rewarding.

**The Federal Reserve held interest rates steady on Wednesday, October 28, at a target range of 3.75% to 4%, in a 10-2 vote.** The Fed sets the short-term interest rate that ripples into mortgages, business loans, and what savers earn. What makes this unusual is the direction of the disagreement: this hold followed a quarter-point *increase* in September, reportedly the first hike in more than three years, and one account says the two dissenters wanted to hike again — I found that detail in only one place, so treat the names and the direction of the dissents as unconfirmed. Chair Kevin Warsh said the inflation fight is unfinished and that politics played no role with midterm elections days away. He also announced that fewer 2027 meetings will carry a rate vote. Why it matters to you: a Fed that might still raise rates is the backdrop for both gold's fall to a one-year low and the market's new impatience with companies spending money today for profits years from now. When money is expensive, promises are worth less.

**The Employment Cost Index for the third quarter came in at 0.8%, exactly as forecast.** This is a quarterly reading of what it costs employers to pay people, wages plus benefits. Over the twelve months through September, wages rose about 3.1% and total compensation about 3.4% — but adjusted for inflation, compensation *fell* about 0.4%. Why it matters: for the Fed, slower wage growth means less risk of a wage-price spiral, which is mildly good news. For households, pay is losing a race with prices for the second straight quarter. Separately, the core PCE price index — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, which strips out food and energy — was reported Thursday at 3.1% year over year for September, in line with expectations but up from 3.0% in August. That is still well above the Fed's 2% target, which is why rate cuts are not on the table right now.

**The Strait of Hormuz tanker attack.** A tanker caught fire after explosive devices were attached to its hull, and oil rose on fears of renewed U.S.-Iran military escalation. The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes. Why it matters: oil is an input cost for nearly everything that gets made or shipped, so a sustained rise feeds into inflation — which loops straight back into the Fed story above.

**The $6.3 billion Binance hack.** Binance, one of the largest crypto exchanges, said an attacker may have gained entry through a compromised engineer's laptop and submitted unauthorized withdrawals. It paused withdrawals, said it expects to resume within about 48 hours, and said it would cover losses; other crypto firms froze more than $3 billion of the stolen funds. Why it matters beyond crypto: it is a reminder that in this corner of the market, the risk is not only the price of the asset but whether the place holding it stays standing.

**Earnings season is going well, which is the strange part.** With roughly half the S&P 500 reported, third-quarter earnings are on pace to rise about 31% from a year ago. A market falling during a 31% earnings quarter is the clearest evidence that today's selling was about expectations for the future, not results from the past.

## Today's lesson: guidance, or why a company can beat and still lose a third of its value

Today's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning the study of the business itself, its sales and profits and spending, rather than the behavior of its stock price. And Intel handed us the single cleanest example of this idea you will ever see, because the contradiction is so stark it is almost funny. Intel earned $0.52 per share when Wall Street expected $0.40. That is a 30% beat. Revenue grew 21%. Its data-center and AI business grew almost 80%. And the stock fell 29%, its worst day in over fifty years.

The word that explains it is **guidance**. Guidance is a company's own public forecast of its *next* quarter or year, given by management on the earnings call. It is not required and it is not audited — it is management telling you what they see coming. Analysts then build their models around it, and the average of their forecasts becomes what traders call **consensus**, or "estimates." The number you hear described as a "beat" or a "miss" is always a comparison against that consensus, not against the company's past.

Here is the crucial part. A stock price is not a scorecard for what a company has already done. It is a price people pay today for a share of profits that have not happened yet. The quarter Intel just reported is finished — that cash is already in the bank and already reflected in the share price people paid last week. What has value is the future. So when Intel said the current quarter would bring $15 to $16 billion of revenue instead of the $17 billion everyone had modeled, and $0.25 of earnings instead of $0.43, it was not revising the past. It was telling the market that the thing it had bought — a particular future — was smaller than advertised. And the chief financial officer made it worse by extending the problem out in time: high memory prices are discouraging computer purchases, and he expects no improvement until at least the second half of 2027. That is not one bad quarter. That is a lowered trajectory.

Think of it as a general manager signing a free agent. You are not paying for the 28 points a night he scored last season — those games are played, those wins are banked, and nobody can re-bank them. You are paying for the next three seasons. So if he puts up a great final game and then mentions at the press conference that his knee has been bothering him for weeks and the doctors say it will not be right until 2028, the great final game does not help you. The price you were willing to pay was built on a future that just got smaller. Intel played a terrific final game and then described the knee.

Two things to carry forward from this. First, when you read a headline that says a company "beat earnings," that sentence describes the past and is often the less important half of the story — the guidance is usually what moves the stock, and it is frequently buried further down the article. Reddit and Twilio both beat today and both fell hard for exactly the same reason as Intel. Second, notice that the market punished this far more violently than a normal disappointment, and ask why. Intel had nearly quadrupled since January. When a stock has run up that far, the price already contains an optimistic future, so there is a long way to fall when that future is revised. The size of a reaction tells you as much about what investors were already expecting as it does about the news itself.
