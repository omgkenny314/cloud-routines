# Market Close Brief — Monday, November 2, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a split market, and the split tells you almost everything. Oil collapsed more than 7% on real signs of progress toward ending the war with Iran, and that single fact lifted everything that burns fuel or benefits from cheaper energy — airlines, industrials, consumer names — which is why the Dow Jones Industrial Average closed up 0.41%. But the same day saw the technology sell-off that wrecked October pick up right where it left off, and because the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite are dominated by large technology companies, both of them closed *lower*: the S&P 500 down 0.51% and the Nasdaq down 1.07%. The biggest storyline is that this was a rally that failed. Stocks opened higher across the board on the oil news, the S&P 500 was up about 0.6% by midday, and then sellers took the afternoon back piece by piece until two of the three major indexes finished in the red. Two pieces of news did that work. Oracle filed to raise $20 billion in fresh stock and bonds to pay for its artificial-intelligence build-out, which revived exactly the worry that has been punishing tech for two weeks — these companies are spending more than they earn. And Marvell Technology agreed to buy Lumentum Holdings for $115 billion in stock, a deal so large that *both* companies' shares fell hard. Tomorrow is Election Day for the midterms, Apple and Palantir reported after the close, and the market is holding its breath on all of it.

## The numbers

Where sources differed I say so. For the three indexes, the closing levels below cross-check exactly against Friday's confirmed closes, which gives me good confidence in them.

- **S&P 500 — closed at 7,541.40, down 0.51%** (a fall of about 38.67 points from Friday's 7,580.07). This is the broad index, 500 large U.S. companies. It fell because roughly a third of its weight sits in technology, and technology fell. Two independent sources report the same level and percentage.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average — closed at 51,983.98, up 213.67 points, or 0.41%.** The Dow holds just 30 companies and leans toward industrials, banks, and health care rather than tech. It was the day's winner because cheaper oil is a straightforward cost cut for a lot of what it owns. Note that the Dow was up about 1% at midday, so it gave back more than half its gain too — it simply had further to fall before it went negative.

- **Nasdaq Composite — closed at 25,164.32, down 1.07%** (about 272.68 points below Friday's 25,437.00). This is the technology-heavy index, and it was the hardest hit for the plainest reason: it holds the most of what people were selling. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, which tracks chipmakers, fell about 3.3%.

- **10-year Treasury yield — about 4.87% to 4.92%, down roughly 5 to 6 basis points.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so that is a move of about 0.05%. One source citing Bloomberg data puts it at 4.871%, the lowest since September 10; another data provider showed 4.92%. The direction is not in doubt, and the reason is clean: the 10-year Treasury yield is the interest rate the U.S. government pays to borrow for ten years, and it tends to fall when investors expect less inflation. Oil down 7% means less inflation pressure, so yields fell.

- **Crude oil — West Texas Intermediate settled at $96.46 a barrel, down about 7.4%**, its biggest one-day loss since July 27. West Texas Intermediate, or WTI, is the U.S. benchmark grade. Brent, the international benchmark, settled at $104.77, down about 7.6%. Both figures are confirmed by two sources. WTI dropping back below $100 matters psychologically after months above it. The cause was diplomacy: a third straight day of Qatari-led talks aimed at ending the roughly eight-month war, with Iran's president saying the Supreme Leader had granted the government authority to negotiate.

- **Bitcoin — roughly $77,500 to $78,100.** I could not pin this to a single confirmed figure because cryptocurrency trades around the clock and has no official close; one live blog had it near $78,100 after a small bounce, another data provider showed about $77,545. It remains under pressure from the Binance exchange hack reported Friday, and Binance saw roughly $6 billion flow out once it reopened withdrawals.

## Big movers and why

An honest caveat on this section: the percentages below come from intraday reports timed through the afternoon rather than from confirmed closing prints, so treat them as the right size and direction rather than exact final numbers.

- **IDEXX Laboratories (ticker IDXX), which makes diagnostic tests for veterinarians, rose about 19%** — reportedly its best day since August 2025, and the biggest gain in the large-company universe. The report behind it was strong in every dimension that matters: third-quarter revenue of $1,214 million, up 10% both as reported and organically, and earnings of $4.01 per diluted share against about $3.71 expected, up 18%. "Organically" means the growth came from the existing business, not from buying another company, which is the higher-quality kind. It also raised its full-year earnings outlook to $14.96 to $15.16 per share, about 25 cents higher at the midpoint. Compare this to the Intel and Reddit reports from Friday: those companies beat on the quarter and then guided the *next* quarter lower, and were punished. IDEXX beat and raised. That is the combination the market is paying for right now.

- **United Airlines (ticker UAL) rose roughly 4% to 7%,** and airlines rallied as a group. The logic is direct: jet fuel is an airline's second-largest cost after labor, so a 7% drop in crude goes almost straight to the bottom line.

- **Amazon (ticker AMZN) rose about 3.3% to 4.7%** and led the Dow higher, clawing back part of Friday's loss. One source attributes the move to an analyst upgrade to "Buy" from Phillip Securities. I want to flag that I could not independently confirm an upgrade dated today — the most recent actions I could verify from that firm were in May and August — so treat the *reason* as single-sourced even though the move itself is well reported.

- **Oracle (ticker ORCL) fell somewhere between 4% and 8.5%,** and this was arguably the day's most consequential piece of company news. Oracle filed to sell $20 billion of new securities — $10 billion of common stock and $10 billion of senior notes, which are corporate bonds, in six tranches maturing between 2029 and 2046 — to help pay for AI data centers. Two offerings, structured independently, so either can proceed without the other. Why the stock fell on raising money: selling new stock divides the company's future profits among more shares, and selling bonds adds interest payments it must make regardless of how the AI bet turns out. The context is harsher still. Oracle completed a $25 billion debt deal and a $20 billion stock sale earlier this year and had indicated it did not expect to issue more bonds in 2026. Its shares are down about 33% in 2026 and about 50% over twelve months, and one of its investment-grade bonds maturing in 2031 now yields about 7.27% — a high yield for an investment-grade borrower, meaning lenders are demanding more to hold its debt.

- **Marvell Technology (ticker MRVL) fell about 10% to 11% and Lumentum Holdings (ticker LITE) fell about 10% to 17%,** on news that Marvell is buying Lumentum. Marvell designs chips for data centers; Lumentum makes the optical and photonic components that move data as light. The terms: all stock, 4.5 Marvell shares for each Lumentum share, an implied enterprise value of $115 billion, and about a 24% premium to Lumentum's October 28 price. Marvell holders would own roughly 67% of the combined company, Lumentum holders roughly 33%, and the companies expect more than $1 billion in annual cost savings. Both boards approved it unanimously, but it is not expected to close until the first half of 2028 and needs both shareholder votes plus regulators. Two things here are genuinely unusual and worth noticing. First, the company being *acquired* fell — normally a takeover target jumps toward the offer price. That happened because the offer is paid in Marvell stock, so when Marvell fell 10%, the value of the offer fell with it. Second, Lumentum traded around $765 against an implied deal value near $1,132, a gap of roughly 9%, which is the market saying it is not at all sure this closes.

- **Energy stocks were the day's weak sector,** with the Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund (ticker XLE), a basket of large energy companies, down about 3%. The mirror image of the airlines: what is a cost for one is revenue for the other.

## Headlines that matter

**Oil fell 7% because the Iran war may be ending — and that is the most important macroeconomic news of the day.** Talks led by Qatar ran for a third straight day, Iran's president said the Supreme Leader had given the government authority for peace negotiations, and the Iranian foreign minister said Tehran was prepared for both defense and diplomacy. Why it matters far beyond energy: oil is an input cost for nearly everything that gets manufactured, shipped, or flown, so sustained high crude feeds directly into inflation. Falling crude does the opposite, which is why Treasury yields fell today alongside it — the bond market read cheaper oil as less inflation, and therefore less pressure on the Federal Reserve. Two cautions keep this from being a clean victory. One analyst at Raymond James warned that there have been many "false dawns" in these talks over the past several months, no agreement has actually been signed, and tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz are still driving up shipping costs. OPEC+ also left its December production targets unchanged, which neither helps nor hurts.

**Manufacturing data came in strong, with one uncomfortable detail.** The ISM Manufacturing PMI for October registered 55.5, beating the roughly 54.5 expected and up from 54.5 in September. This survey asks purchasing managers at factories whether business is better or worse than last month; above 50 means expansion, and this was the tenth consecutive month above it. Production rose to 59.0, the highest since November 2021. Employment eased to 52.4 but still expanded for a fourth month. ISM's own chair said the reading is historically consistent with about 2.7% annualized growth in real gross domestic product. Here is the uncomfortable part: the Prices Paid index was 75.2. It came down from 77.9, but a number that high means factories are still paying sharply more for their inputs. Why it matters: a strong economy with stubborn input inflation is precisely the combination that argues for *higher* interest rates, not lower ones. Note also that a separate survey, the S&P Global manufacturing PMI, was revised down to 54.9 from an initial 56.0 — the same direction, less heat.

**The Federal Reserve is probably going to raise rates in December, and that is the quiet weight on everything.** At its October 28 meeting the Fed held its target range at 3.75% to 4.00% in a 10-2 vote, with the two dissenters — reported as Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack — wanting a quarter-point *increase*. That hold followed an actual rate hike in September. Chair Kevin Warsh said the inflation fight is unfinished and that politics played no role with the midterms days away. Market-implied odds of a December 9 hike run somewhere between 71% and 85% depending on which source and timestamp you take. Why this matters for the stories above: when interest rates are high and may go higher, money today is worth meaningfully more than money promised in 2030. That is the arithmetic behind the whole AI-spending revolt — and it is why Oracle borrowing $20 billion to build data centers got treated as bad news rather than ambition.

**The midterm elections are tomorrow.** All 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are up. One source reports prediction markets pricing roughly 75% odds of a Democratic sweep, which I could only find in that one place, so hold it loosely. The useful framing from the analysis I read is that for markets the *result* usually matters less than whether the result is a *surprise*. Markets price in what they expect; they move on what they did not.

**The backdrop you should keep in mind: October was the Nasdaq Composite's worst month since March 2025**, driven by exactly the AI-spending anxiety that produced Friday's rout and today's afternoon fade. Earnings themselves have been good. What has changed is that investors have stopped treating enormous capital spending announcements as proof of ambition and started treating them as cash walking out the door.

## After the bell

It is early in the after-hours session, but the two reports everyone was waiting for are already out.

**Apple (ticker AAPL) delivered a record quarter and the stock barely moved.** For its fiscal fourth quarter, Apple reported revenue of $114.5 billion, up 12% from a year ago and ahead of the roughly $113.4 billion expected, with earnings of $2.10 per diluted share, up 14%. That figure includes about 5 cents of benefit from tariff refunds. iPhone revenue was $57.58 billion against about $56.54 billion expected — up sharply from $49.02 billion a year ago. Mac beat as well; iPad came in light at $6.23 billion against about $6.7 billion expected. Gross margin was 47.9%, which also included roughly one percentage point of help from those tariff refunds. This was the first report under John Ternus, who became chief executive on September 1 succeeding Tim Cook, and he said Apple "reluctantly" raised prices to offset rising component costs — memory chips in particular, which was the worry that had the stock down about 2% going into the print. Early after-hours reaction: little changed to down about 1%. I could not find Apple's guidance for the December holiday quarter, which is the number that usually drives the stock, so that may still move when the call concludes.

**Palantir Technologies (ticker PLTR) beat enormously, and the stock did not obviously care.** Third-quarter revenue was $2.313 billion, up 96% from a year ago and up 20% from the prior quarter, against roughly $2.18 billion expected and its own guidance of $2.160 to $2.164 billion. Adjusted earnings were $0.44 per share against about $0.41 expected. U.S. commercial revenue was $984 million, up about 148%, and for the first time exceeded its U.S. government revenue — a genuine milestone for a company built on defense and intelligence contracts. It raised full-year revenue guidance to $8.530 to $8.534 billion, a large increase from the $8.150 to $8.158 billion it guided in August, and raised its adjusted operating income and free cash flow outlooks too. The soft spot analysts seized on: closed total contract value was $2.918 billion, up only 6% from a year ago. Total contract value is the full dollar value of deals signed in the quarter, so it is a read on *future* business, and 6% growth in new signings against 96% growth in recognized revenue is a gap worth watching. On the stock reaction, my sources genuinely conflict: two reports describe shares as initially flat to up about 2%, while one headline claimed a jump of nearly 15%. I could not corroborate the larger figure, and after-hours prices move fast on thin volume, so I will not pick a number. Options traders had been pricing a move of roughly 12% to 13% in either direction.

**Also reporting tonight:** Clorox (ticker CLX), the household products company, was scheduled to report fiscal first-quarter results after the close, with about $1.07 per share expected on roughly $1.88 billion of revenue. I found no results from it yet. Beyond Clorox I could not confirm other significant after-close reporters for tonight — Monday evening is a thin slot, and Apple and Palantir were the main event.

## Today's lesson: the shape of a day, and why the close is the only price that counts

Today's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning we read the behavior of the price itself rather than the company's financial statements. And today handed us the cleanest possible example of a pattern worth recognizing for the rest of your investing life: the **failed rally**, sometimes called an intraday reversal.

Here is what happened to the Nasdaq Composite today, in order. It opened higher, up about 0.6%. By late morning it was up roughly 165 points, trading around 25,600. Then it slid for the rest of the day and closed at 25,164.32, down 1.07%. From its best level of the morning to where it finished, the index traveled about 435 points *downward*. Anyone who checked the market at lunch would have told you it was a good day. The scoreboard that goes in the record book says otherwise.

This is where the basketball analogy actually earns its place. A team goes up ten points in the first quarter, and then gets outscored for the remaining three. The final score is what goes in the standings — nobody records the halftime lead. In markets, the **close** is the final score: it is the price used to calculate the index's official daily change, the price that sets the value of most mutual funds and exchange-traded funds, and the price that chart analysts plot. Every other price during the day is a moment that passed.

So what does a day like this *mean*, and what is it evidence of? Technical analysts read the four prices of any trading day — the open, the high, the low, and the close — as a short story about who won. When a market opens up, trades up, and then closes near the low of its day, the story is that sellers were waiting at higher prices and had more conviction than buyers. The jargon for a price level where sellers reliably show up is **resistance**: a ceiling the market keeps bumping into and failing to break through. The Nasdaq ran up toward 25,600 this morning, found sellers there, and got pushed back. One day does not prove a ceiling exists. But if the index rallies toward that same area again this week and fails again, you are watching resistance form in real time, and you will have learned it by watching rather than by being told.

The one thing to be careful about — and this is why technical and fundamental analysis belong together rather than in opposition — is that the shape of a day tells you about *supply and demand for shares*, not about the quality of the businesses. Today's reversal is genuine information: it says the buyers who showed up for cheap oil were outnumbered by people wanting out of technology. It does not say whether Oracle's data centers will pay off or whether Palantir is worth what it costs. Those are fundamental questions, answered with financial statements. The chart tells you what the crowd did. It does not tell you whether the crowd was right.
