# Market Close Brief — Thursday, October 8, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a split-screen day, and the dividing line ran right through artificial intelligence. The Dow Jones Industrial Average actually finished higher. The S&P 500 slipped less than half a percent. But the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.25%, and a gauge of big semiconductor companies fell about 3%, because of one news story: the Financial Times reported that OpenAI had told investors its annualized revenue was running near $50 billion at the end of September — well short of the roughly $68 to $70 billion figure that had been circulating a month earlier. OpenAI is not a public company, so you cannot sell its stock. What you can sell is everything built on the assumption that it will keep buying: the chips, the servers, the rented cloud capacity, the data centers financed with borrowed money. That is exactly what happened. Oracle, CoreWeave, Advanced Micro Devices, Broadcom and Intel all fell hard, while Nvidia slipped a more modest 3%. Meanwhile the rest of the market had a perfectly decent session — PepsiCo rose on good earnings, oil jumped almost 4% on fresh tanker attacks near the Strait of Hormuz, and the bond market, which has been the villain of the past two weeks, finally gave stocks a break as long-term Treasury yields eased after a reasonable 30-year bond auction. The single biggest storyline is the one worth sitting with: the market spent today repricing a customer it cannot buy or sell, only guess at. There is a reason the exact number was disputed by day's end, and that dispute is today's lesson.

## Big movers and why

An important caveat on this whole section: the index closes above are confirmed, but most individual stock percentages I could verify today came from intraday reports filed before 4:00 p.m. Eastern. Read them as the shape and scale of each move, not as exact final figures.

**Oracle (ORCL), down roughly 5% to 6%.** The clearest casualty. Oracle has staked an enormous amount on providing cloud computing capacity to OpenAI, and much of that buildout is funded with debt. When the expected revenue of your single most important future customer is revised down by roughly a third, investors immediately ask whether the contracted demand backing those borrowings is as solid as advertised. Oracle fell more than any of the big chipmakers.

**CoreWeave (CRWV), down about 7% to 8%, trading near $81.94 in the early afternoon.** CoreWeave rents out AI computing power and borrows heavily to buy the chips it rents. **Advanced Micro Devices (AMD), down about 5%.** AMD fell more than Nvidia, which is worth noticing. ## Headlines that matter

**The OpenAI revenue report, and the detail almost everyone skipped.** The Financial Times reported, based on financial documents shared with investors, that OpenAI's annualized revenue was near $50 billion at the end of September. The widely circulated figure a month earlier had been about $68 to $70 billion. CNBC confirmed the $50 billion number.

*Why it matters:* semiconductor and server companies are not valued on what they sold last quarter. They are valued on what analysts believe they will sell over the next several years, and a very large share of that belief traces back to a handful of AI buyers. Shave the expected growth of the most prominent one, and every forecast downstream gets a haircut too. That is the mechanism by which a private company's internal numbers knocked more than 300 points off the Nasdaq.

*But here is the catch, and it is a good one.* CNN reported that a person familiar with the documents said the $70 billion figure did not come from OpenAI at all, and likely reflected a comparison against Anthropic's reported numbers — which count *gross* revenue including what flows through cloud providers, while OpenAI's figure is *net*. If that is right, then a meaningful part of today's selloff was caused not by a business deteriorating but by two companies being measured with different rulers. Neither OpenAI nor its partners have publicly reconciled the two numbers. This is the heart of today's lesson.

**Jobless claims came in at 197,000 for the week ended October 3, the lowest since July.** New filings for unemployment benefits eased by 2,000 and came in below the roughly 200,000 economists expected. The four-week average, which smooths out weekly noise, fell to 198,000 — the lowest since early October 2022. Continuing claims, meaning people still collecting benefits, rose to 1.716 million for the week ended September 26.

*Why it matters:* this is the single cleanest weekly read on whether employers are firing people, and the answer remains no. That is genuinely reassuring, but it sits awkwardly beside the September jobs report, which showed the economy adding only 29,000 jobs against expectations near 90,000. Put the two together and you get a labor market where companies are not letting people go but are not hiring either — a freeze rather than a downturn. Those are very different problems for the Federal Reserve, which is why this tension keeps showing up in how the market trades.

**The Treasury sold $22 billion of 30-year bonds, and it went well enough.** The auction cleared at a high yield of 5.618%, up 31 basis points from September's 5.308%. Indirect bidders, a category that includes foreign central banks, took 72.3% versus a 68% recent average; direct bidders took 20.9% against a 22% average.

*Why it matters:* for two weeks, the dominant question in this market has been whether buyers will keep absorbing U.S. government debt at these yields without demanding even higher ones. Each auction is a live test. Today's passed — one strategist called it "decent but nowhere close to as good as the 10-year auction yesterday" — and long-term yields fell after it. That is why stocks fell only modestly on a day with genuinely bad tech news: the bond market, for once, was helping.

**The Strait of Hormuz is the geopolitical story moving actual prices.** Nine tankers have been attacked in and around the strait over the past week. About 9.5 million barrels a day transited Hormuz in the week ended Tuesday, roughly 30% below pre-conflict normal, according to vessel-tracking firm Kpler. NBC News reported that the administration was weighing renewed strikes; President Trump then said the U.S. would not attack Iran before the midterms, which took oil off its highs.

*Why it matters:* expensive oil is an inflation problem, and an inflation problem is an interest-rate problem. The Fed raised rates a quarter point on September 16 to a target range of 3.75% to 4.00% — its first increase since 2023 — and Chair Kevin Warsh has repeatedly said inflation is still too high. Energy prices tied to this conflict were specifically named in the September meeting minutes as a reason inflation has stayed elevated. So a tanker attack in the Persian Gulf is not a foreign-news item for this market; it is a direct input into what your mortgage and your car loan cost next year. The next Fed decision is October 28.

## Today's lesson: gross versus net revenue, or why two companies' "revenue" may not be the same word

Today's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning the study of the business itself, its revenue, profits and balance sheet, rather than the behavior of its stock price. And today handed us an unusually expensive example of why one word on a financial statement can mean two different things.

Start with the discrepancy that moved the market. One figure said OpenAI was running at about $70 billion of annualized revenue. Another said $50 billion. The gap is $20 billion, which is larger than the entire annual revenue of most companies in the S&P 500. And according to the explanation CNN reported, a meaningful part of that gap may not be a change in the business at all. It may be the difference between **gross revenue** and **net revenue**.

Here is what those terms mean. Imagine you sell concert tickets. A fan pays you $100. You keep $15 and pass $85 to the venue, which you were always obligated to do. How much revenue did you make? Under **gross** accounting, $100 — that is the full amount that passed through your hands. Under **net** accounting, $15 — that is the amount that was ever actually yours. Both are defensible descriptions of the same transaction. They differ by nearly seven times. Accountants have real rules about which to use, turning on whether you *control* the thing being sold or are merely an **agent** arranging someone else's sale, but the practical point for you is simpler: the word "revenue" by itself does not tell you which convention you are looking at. In today's case, the reported explanation is that Anthropic's figure includes gross revenue flowing through cloud providers while OpenAI's is net of what it owes partners. Different rulers, same word.

Think of it as a basketball box score. A guard throws the pass that leads to a dunk. Did he score two points, or did he record one assist? He plainly did something valuable either way, but if one league credits the passer with the points and another credits only the finisher, then comparing scoring averages across the two leagues tells you almost nothing about who is the better player. You would have to go read each league's rulebook first. The market today, in effect, compared scoring averages across two leagues and sold several hundred billion dollars of stock on the result.

So the usable habit is this. When you read that a company's revenue grew, or that one company's revenue is larger than another's, ask what is actually inside that number before you accept the comparison. Does this business keep what passes through it, or merely route it? Is the figure before or after what it owes partners? Is the growth rate measured the same way in both periods? Companies that report both gross and net figures — payment processors, marketplaces, travel sites and ad platforms all commonly do — will usually show you both somewhere in the filing, and the gap between them is often the most informative number on the page. None of this tells you whether a stock is a good or bad holding. It tells you whether the comparison you are making is a real comparison at all. Today, quite possibly, it was not.
