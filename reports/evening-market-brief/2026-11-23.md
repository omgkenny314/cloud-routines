## The day in one paragraph

Today was a violent, one-sided rebound — the best day for U.S. stocks since April 2025 — and it had exactly one cause. At midday, Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive, twelve days after he resigned and roughly a week after that same board said publicly it did *not* intend to bring him back. Nvidia stock rose more than 31%. Everything connected to artificial intelligence rose with it: chipmakers, memory makers, the data-center companies, the power suppliers. The context is what makes the size of the move understandable. Last week was the worst week for the Nasdaq since March 2020, a roughly 10% drop that erased the index's entire gain for 2026 and left both the S&P 500 and the Dow in what is called a correction — more than 10% below their record highs. So today was not a fresh burst of optimism about anything. It was the unwinding of a specific fear, by the specific piece of news that removed it. Worth holding onto: nothing about the actual demand for AI chips changed today, and the thing most likely to matter next is still the Federal Reserve, which the market now believes is about 98% certain to *raise* interest rates in December.

## Headlines that matter

**Nvidia reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO.** The board voted unanimously, said it was satisfied as to Huang's "candor and ability to lead NVIDIA," and returned him to the board as well. Colette Kress, who had been interim CEO, stays on as chief financial officer. *Why it matters:* this is the clearest example you will see of a company whose perceived value is tied to one person. Nvidia lost enormous market value when Huang left on November 11 and recovered much of it the moment he returned, with no change whatsoever to its products, customers or orders. When analysts talk about "key man risk," this is the thing itself.

**The board also reversed itself in public.** On its earnings call last Tuesday, Nvidia's board said it did not intend to reinstate Huang. Today it did. *Why it matters:* a board reversing a stated position in eight days tells you the pressure on it was severe — and it means future statements from that board carry less weight with investors, which is itself a cost.

**Nvidia said it received input from "senior levels of the United States government" that the reinstatement poses no national security risk, and the underlying investigation continues.** The probe concerns a reported foreign intelligence effort targeting Huang; Nvidia says he was the target of it, and an independent committee's work will run to conclusion. *Why it matters:* the stock has re-priced as if the issue is resolved, but it is not resolved — it is ongoing. That is an open risk sitting underneath today's gain.

**Congress reacted angrily, on both sides.** The Senate Banking Committee announced it expects to question Huang, House members expect to do the same when they return in December, and Republicans including Senators John Cornyn and John Curtis and Representative Bill Huizenga publicly questioned the basis for the board's national-security conclusion and the White House's involvement. Democrats vowed investigations and legislation. *Why it matters:* for a company that needs export licenses and government goodwill to sell advanced chips abroad, hostile attention from both parties is a real business risk, not just political noise.

**CrowdStrike agreed to buy Zscaler for about $43.9 billion in an all-stock deal.** Each Zscaler share converts into 1.135 CrowdStrike shares, valued at $260.41 per share against Friday's close — a 34% premium. CrowdStrike expects $500 million of annual cost savings within 18 to 24 months of closing, which it expects in the second half of its fiscal 2028. *Why it matters:* "all-stock" means CrowdStrike is paying with newly issued shares of itself, so every existing CrowdStrike shareholder's slice of the company gets smaller. That is called dilution, and it is the main reason the buyer's stock fell 10% on a day when nearly everything rose. The market is also pricing integration risk — large software mergers frequently disappoint — and this is the largest cybersecurity deal in some time, which analysts read as consolidation in that industry entering a more serious phase.

**The Fed is now expected, almost unanimously, to raise rates in December.** Odds of a quarter-point increase at the December 8–9 meeting moved to roughly 98% today, from about 88% on Friday, per CME FedWatch. The current target range is 3.75%–4%. New York Fed President John Williams was scheduled to speak this afternoon; I could not verify what he said in time for this brief. On November 17 he said more increases are likely if inflation stays persistent, while ruling out any larger, dramatic move. *Why it matters:* this is the single most important fact in the market right now and it runs opposite to today's cheerful tape. Rising interest rates reduce what future profits are worth today, which hits expensive, growth-dependent stocks hardest — exactly the stocks that soared today.

**Oil kept sliding on supply and peace-deal hopes.** Brent fell about 4.5%, with reporting pointing to optimism about a Russia-Ukraine settlement, reduced odds of Iran escalation, higher OPEC+ output and building U.S. inventories. *Why it matters:* cheaper energy is a genuine help on inflation, which is the Fed's problem — but it is bad news for the energy sector's profits, so it cuts in both directions depending on what you own.

## Today's lesson

Tonight's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning we read the behavior of the price itself rather than the company's financial statements. The concept is **retracement**, and today is close to a perfect teaching case, because the market handed us three different answers to the same question on the same day.

Here is the idea. When a market falls hard and then bounces, the bounce is almost never a straight return to where it started. So the useful question is not "did it go up?" — it obviously did — but *how much of the fall did the bounce take back?* That fraction is the retracement. The arithmetic is simple division: the points recovered, divided by the points lost. Technicians watch it because a bounce that recovers a small slice of a decline looks like temporary relief inside a continuing downtrend, while one that takes back most of the decline looks more like the decline itself was the mistake. The halfway mark, 50%, is the line most people watch, for no deeper reason than that it is the natural midpoint and enough participants watch it to make it matter.

Now the numbers, and these all reconcile exactly, which is why I like them. Last week each index fell from its November 13 close to its November 20 close. The S&P 500 went from 7,561.67 to 6,910.39 — a loss of 651.28 points. Today it recovered 326.78 of those points. Divide: 326.78 ÷ 651.28 = **50.2%**. Almost exactly half. The Nasdaq Composite fell 2,518.54 points and recovered 1,471.83 today, which is **58.4%** — more than half. The Dow fell 4,200.22 points and recovered 1,447.98, which is only **34.5%** — about a third. So on the single best day since April 2025, the three indexes took back a third, a half, and nearly three-fifths of last week's damage. That spread is the information. The index that fell hardest bounced hardest, because the same handful of AI stocks caused both moves. The Dow lagged in both directions for the same reason.

Here is the piece of arithmetic that catches almost everyone, and it is the real reason retracement is worth measuring. The Nasdaq fell 10.02% last week. Today it rose 6.51%. You might expect it to be close to even. It is not — it is still 4.16% below where it stood on November 13. Percentage losses and percentage gains are not symmetric, because a loss is calculated on the bigger number and the recovery on the smaller one. To fully undo a 10.02% fall, the Nasdaq needs to rise **11.14%**, not 10.02%. The hole is always deeper than it looks from the bottom. This is why experienced investors are unimpressed by a big green day after a big red one, and it is why they measure the bounce as a fraction of the fall rather than celebrating the percentage on its own.

Think of it the way you would a basketball game. Your team goes down 20 points in the third quarter, then scores 10 straight. The run is real, the building is loud, and you are still losing by 10. You have retraced half the deficit — that is genuinely better than not having done it, and it tells you something about which team has momentum. But the scoreboard is what goes in the record book, and the scoreboard still says you are behind. Today was a 10-point run. It was not a lead.
