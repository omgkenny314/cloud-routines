# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, September 15, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a quiet-looking down day sitting on top of a very loud bond market. All three major U.S. stock indexes fell for a second straight session, but none of them fell much — the S&P 500 lost less than half a percent. The real story was in Treasuries, where the yield on the 10-year government bond climbed to 5.04%, its highest level since 2007. That is a 19-year high, and it happened on the eve of a Federal Reserve decision that traders now overwhelmingly expect to be an interest rate *hike* — the first since July 2023. Underneath that sits oil: crude settled above $105 a barrel as the Saudi East-West pipeline stayed shut and the Strait of Hormuz remained disrupted, and expensive energy feeds straight into inflation, which is what the Fed is reacting to. So the single biggest storyline is simple to state and hard to escape: borrowing costs are going up because oil is going up, and stocks spent the day getting out of the way rather than panicking. The one place there *was* a sharp, news-driven move was crypto, where a long-awaited Senate vote on digital-asset regulation failed in the afternoon and took crypto-linked stocks down with it.

## The numbers

**S&P 500** — closed at 7,585.73, down 0.45%. A broad, orderly retreat ahead of tomorrow's Fed decision, cushioned by gains in a handful of AI-linked chip names.

**Dow Jones Industrial Average** — closed at 52,093.11, down 328.09 points, or 0.63%. The Dow leaned lower on the same rate worry; its blend of older industrial and consumer companies gives it no AI cushion.

**Nasdaq Composite** — closed at 25,981.57, down 0.78%. The weakest of the three, which is typical when yields jump: the Nasdaq is packed with fast-growing technology companies whose value rests on profits far in the future, and higher rates shrink what future money is worth today.

**10-year Treasury yield** — rose as high as 5.04% and was around 5.02% late in the day, the highest since 2007. Driven by expectations of a Fed rate hike tomorrow plus inflation worry from $100-plus oil. (The 30-year bond yield rose roughly 3 basis points to about 5.36%; a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point.)

**Crude oil** — U.S. West Texas Intermediate crude settled at $105.83 a barrel, up more than 4%; Brent, the international benchmark, settled at $108.75, up nearly 3%. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline remains shut after Houthi strikes, removing the main route for exporting crude without using the blockaded Strait of Hormuz. Repairs are reported to take weeks.

**Bitcoin** — traded around $76,000 to $77,000 late in the session, down from an opening level near $78,200. It slid after the Senate failed to advance the CLARITY Act, the crypto industry's regulatory bill. Note that bitcoin trades 24 hours a day, so it has no official "close" the way a stock does — any number you hear is a snapshot at a moment in time.

## Big movers and why

**Dave & Buster's Entertainment (PLAY)** — down about 17%, the day's most brutal single-stock move. The arcade-and-restaurant chain reported its second-quarter results and missed on essentially every line: revenue of $544.1 million against roughly $556.8 million expected, and an adjusted *loss* of 27 cents per share where analysts had penciled in a profit of about 18 cents. Adjusted EBITDA — a rough measure of cash profit from operations — came in at $98.9 million versus roughly $120.4 million expected. When a company misses on revenue, profit, *and* cash profit simultaneously, the market reads it as a demand problem, not an accounting quirk.

**Enova International (ENVA)** — down more than 15%. The online consumer lender withdrew its regulatory applications to acquire Grasshopper Bancorp, abandoning a deal that would have given it a bank charter. Investors had been valuing some of that strategic upside in the price; pulling the application took it back out.

**The crypto-linked group** — Circle (CRCL) fell about 8%, Coinbase (COIN) about 6.7%, Bullish (BLSH) about 4.6%, and Robinhood (HOOD) about 3.6%. All four moved on the same headline: the Senate's failed vote on crypto market-structure legislation (details in the next section). These are companies whose revenue depends on crypto trading volumes and on regulatory permission to expand, so a legislative failure hits them harder than it hits bitcoin itself.

**The AI chip names went the other way** — Qualcomm (QCOM) rose more than 4%, Advanced Micro Devices (AMD) about 2%, and Coherent (COHR) nearly 2%. Their strength is what kept the S&P 500's loss under half a percent on a day when the average stock was lower. Worth noticing: these same names were part of Monday's AI-driven selloff, so this looks like a partial bounce rather than fresh good news.

**Skyworks Solutions (SWKS)** appeared again among the day's most-traded movers. The driver all month has been its $22 billion merger with Qorvo (QRVO) clearing its final regulatory hurdle in China, with management targeting a close by year-end; the stock has risen roughly 28% over the past week and was on pace to be the S&P 500's best weekly performer. I was not able to verify Skyworks' exact percentage move for today specifically, so treat the week-long figure as the reliable one.

**Revvity (RVTY)**, the life-sciences instruments maker, set a new 12-month high today — a notable piece of quiet strength on a down day, though I could not verify a specific news catalyst behind it.

## Headlines that matter

**The Fed is expected to raise rates tomorrow — and that is a genuine regime change.** Futures markets put the odds of a quarter-point hike at better than 92%. That would lift the federal funds rate from its current 3.50%–3.75% range to 3.75%–4.00%, and it would be the first increase since July 2023. *Why it matters:* for three years the working assumption in markets has been that the next Fed move, whenever it came, would be downward. A hike flips that assumption. The federal funds rate is the rate banks charge each other overnight, and it cascades outward into mortgage rates, credit card rates, business loans, and — crucially — into what investors demand to be paid for owning stocks instead of safe government bonds. Markets also price better than 75% odds of *another* hike by December, which tells you traders think this is a cycle, not a one-off.

**August inflation is what forced the Fed's hand.** Headline consumer price inflation ran at 3.4% year over year, against the Fed's 2% target, while core inflation — which strips out food and energy because those two bounce around — sat at 2.4%. *Why it matters:* the gap between 3.4% and 2.4% is almost entirely energy, which is up 16.3% over the past year. That gap is the whole argument inside the Fed right now. One camp says energy shocks are temporary and the Fed shouldn't chase them; the other says an oil shock that lasts long enough seeps into everything and becomes permanent. Tomorrow's decision tells you which camp is winning.

**The Empire State manufacturing survey came in soft, with a hot price signal inside it.** The New York Fed's gauge of factory activity in New York State registered 7.6 in September, down about 13 points from August and below forecasts. Any reading above zero means activity is still growing, just more slowly. But the survey's prices-paid index rose five points to 63.1, edging above its four-year high from May. *Why it matters:* that is the exact combination central bankers dread — slowing growth alongside accelerating input costs. It gives ammunition to both sides of tomorrow's argument, which is part of why markets drifted rather than picking a direction.

**The Senate failed to advance the CLARITY Act.** The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act needed 60 votes to clear a procedural hurdle called cloture and got roughly 46, with more than 40 senators opposed. *Why it matters:* this bill was the crypto industry's central legislative goal — it would have divided oversight of digital assets between the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, ending years of ambiguity about which regulator governs what. Failing the cloture vote effectively ends the effort for 2026. For companies like Coinbase and Circle, regulatory clarity is not an abstraction; it determines which products they can legally offer in the United States.

**Geopolitics is now an inflation story, not just a news story.** The Saudi East-West pipeline remains offline after Houthi strikes, and the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes — has no reopening timeline. *Why it matters:* this is the mechanism connecting a conflict thousands of miles away to the Fed's decision tomorrow and to the mortgage rate an American homebuyer is quoted next month. Oil above $100 lifts measured inflation, which lifts bond yields, which lifts borrowing costs everywhere.

## After the bell

Tonight is one of the lightest earnings evenings of the month. The published calendars showed only a handful of companies scheduled for the entire day, none of them household names, and as of this writing I was not able to verify any specific after-hours earnings release or any significant post-close stock move. Rather than guess at names, I'll say plainly: there is nothing material out yet.

That is not really surprising. We are between earnings seasons — the big quarterly wave finished in August, and the next cluster of notable reporters starts tomorrow evening. The after-hours session is also being overshadowed by the Fed: with a policy decision landing at 2:00 PM Eastern tomorrow, most companies with discretion over timing would rather not release results into this particular news cycle.

The next real test comes tomorrow after the close, when homebuilder **Lennar (LEN)** reports. Watch also for **General Mills (GIS)** and **Cracker Barrel Old Country Store (CBRL)** tomorrow.

## What to watch tomorrow

**8:30 AM Eastern (7:30 Central) — August retail sales.** This is the government's first read on what American consumers actually spent last month. It lands five and a half hours before the Fed decision, which makes it the last real data point the market gets to chew on.

**8:30 AM Eastern — August import and export prices.** A secondary inflation gauge, but a relevant one right now, because it captures how much of the oil and shipping disruption is showing up in the cost of goods arriving in the country.

**2:00 PM Eastern (1:00 Central) — the Federal Reserve decision, plus the Summary of Economic Projections.** The projections matter as much as the rate itself. They include what is informally called the "dot plot," a chart where each Fed official anonymously marks where they think rates should be at the end of this year and the next few. It is the closest thing markets get to a forecast from the people actually setting the rate.

**2:30 PM Eastern — Chair Kevin Warsh's press conference.** Markets frequently move *more* during the press conference than on the decision itself, because the decision is largely priced in while the tone is not.

**Earnings:** General Mills, Cracker Barrel, and — after the close — Lennar. Later in the week, Darden Restaurants reports Thursday morning and FedEx Thursday after the close; FedEx is worth knowing about because it ships for everyone, which makes its volume commentary a live read on the economy.

## Today's lesson: support, resistance, and why round numbers matter

Our recent lessons have covered dilution, the four prices of a trading day, backlog, and what makes a trend. Today's is technical — that is, about the behavior of price itself rather than the business underneath — and the best example today isn't a stock at all. It's that 5% level on the 10-year Treasury yield.

Here is what happened over two days. On Monday, the 10-year yield rose and touched 5% — and then reversed and closed below it. On Tuesday, it came back and pushed through, reaching 5.04%, the highest since 2007. That two-day sequence is a textbook illustration of **resistance**, and of what it means when resistance breaks.

Resistance is a price level where sellers have repeatedly shown up and stopped an advance. **Support** is the mirror image: a level where buyers have repeatedly shown up and stopped a decline. Neither is a law of physics. They exist because large numbers of people remember the same number and act on it — a fund manager who decided months ago that she'd buy bonds if the yield ever hit 5%, a trader whose risk limits trigger at that level, an algorithm programmed around it. Enough people making the same decision at the same number turns that number into a real, observable floor or ceiling in the price.

Round numbers are the strongest version of this, because they are the easiest to remember and the most likely to be chosen as a threshold. Nobody sets a plan around 4.97%. Plenty of people set plans around 5%. That is why Monday's touch-and-retreat happened: at exactly 5%, a wall of pre-committed buyers stepped into the bond market, bought, and pushed the yield back down. (For bonds, remember the seesaw: buying pushes the price up, which pushes the yield down.)

The basketball version: a team that has never once won on a particular opponent's court. There's nothing structurally different about that floor — same dimensions, same ball. But everyone on the roster knows the streak, plays a little tighter, and the streak sustains itself. Then one night they win there. The next visit feels ordinary. That is what breaking resistance does — it removes the psychological ceiling, and the level that used to act as a lid often starts acting as a floor instead.

So the useful question after today isn't "will yields keep rising?" — nobody knows. The useful observation is that 5% stopped being a ceiling. Technicians would now watch to see whether yields hold *above* 5% on any pullback, which would confirm the level has flipped from resistance into support. One caution worth carrying: support and resistance are descriptions of crowd behavior, not predictions, and they break all the time. Their value is that they tell you where a lot of people have drawn a line — which is genuinely useful information, as long as you don't mistake it for knowing what happens next.
