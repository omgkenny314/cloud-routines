# Market Close Brief — Monday, August 24, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a split-screen day, and the split is the story. The Dow finished up, the Nasdaq finished clearly down, and the S&P 500 landed slightly negative in between — three headline indexes disagreeing about the same eight hours of trading. The reason was concentrated in one corner of the market: memory chip stocks got hit hard. Over the weekend it emerged that Nvidia is telling big customers the price of servers built around its AI chips is going up more than 15%, and the driver of that increase is the soaring cost of memory chips. Investors read the rising cost of memory not as good news for memory makers but as a sign the whole AI hardware chain is straining, and they sold the memory names. Layered on top was a report that Apple has been testing memory chips from CXMT, a Chinese manufacturer — a potential loss of a huge customer for American memory makers. Meanwhile, everything outside of tech had a pleasant day: Treasury yields fell after a report that the Treasury Department may tap a roughly $1 trillion cash account to fund bond buybacks, oil dropped despite the U.S. announcing its toughest-ever sanctions campaign against Iran, and gold hit a three-month high. So money did not leave the market today — it moved from one room of the house to another.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: closed at 7,652.86, down 0.28%** (about 21 points). A handful of chip stocks were heavy enough to outweigh gains in most of the rest of the index.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: closed at 53,417.16, up 140.15 points, or 0.26%.** The Dow owns very little of the memory-chip complex, so it was free to follow the friendlier parts of the day — lower interest rates and steady non-tech names.
- **Nasdaq Composite: closed at 25,980.19, down 0.76%** (about 200 points). Most tech-heavy of the three, so it absorbed the most damage from the semiconductor selling.
- **10-year Treasury yield: about 4.70%, down roughly 3 to 4 basis points.** (A basis point is one one-hundredth of a percentage point, so this is a small but real move.) Yields fell on a CNBC report that the Treasury could pull from its General Account — its roughly $935 billion cash cushion as of August 20 — to fund a larger program of buying back its own bonds. More buying pressure on bonds means higher bond prices, and higher bond prices mean lower yields. The 30-year yield fell about 4 basis points to roughly 5.23%, backing away from levels it hadn't touched since 2007.
- **Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate around $85.65 a barrel, down about 1.6%; Brent around $93.09, down about 1.4%.** Oil fell on the same day the U.S. announced sweeping Iran sanctions, which sounds backwards — more on that below.
- **Bitcoin: roughly $78,900 in the mid-afternoon, up on the day from an open near $77,730.** Bitcoin trades around the clock, so it has no official "close"; this is a snapshot near the stock market's closing bell. It sits at its highest level since May, driven by demand for spot bitcoin ETFs and a general appetite for risk assets.

One honest caveat: gold, oil, and bitcoin levels came from fewer independent sources than the stock index numbers, which I was able to reconcile against Friday's closes to the penny. Treat the index figures as firm and the commodity and crypto figures as close approximations.

## Big movers and why

Almost every notable move today traced back to memory chips — the components that store data, as opposed to the processors that do the calculating.

- **SanDisk (SNDK)** was the worst performer in the entire S&P 500, down roughly 6% to 7%. It is a pure-play memory company, so it takes the full force of any memory story.
- **Micron Technology (MU)** fell roughly 6% (some sources put it closer to 7%). Micron is the largest U.S. memory maker and the name most investors use to express a view on the sector.
- **Seagate Technology (STX)** fell about 6.5% and **Western Digital (WDC)** dropped more than 5% — both storage companies caught in the same wave.
- **Advanced Micro Devices (AMD)** fell more than 3% and **Broadcom (AVGO)** more than 2%, showing the selling spread beyond memory into the broader semiconductor group.
- **Coherent (COHR)** and **Lumentum (LITE)**, which make optical components for data centers, each fell more than 4%. **Corning (GLW)**, which supplies the glass and fiber underneath a lot of that infrastructure, fell nearly 3%.
- **Nvidia (NVDA)** itself fell about 1% — a seventh consecutive down day, leaving it off more than 7% during that stretch. Notice the asymmetry: the company doing the price-raising fell about 1%, while the memory suppliers fell six times as much. The market is treating rising memory costs as a squeeze that Nvidia can largely pass along to customers, but which signals trouble in the wider chain.
- **Applied Optoelectronics (AAOI)** plunged about 11% after announcing a $600 million equity offering — the company selling new shares to raise cash. New shares divide the same company into more pieces, so each existing share represents a smaller slice.
- **Canada Goose (GOOS)** fell after Wells Fargo downgraded the stock to underweight from overweight, citing weather and tariffs weighing on the parka maker's full-year earnings — a small but tidy illustration of the trade story reaching real companies.

On the winning side, the strength was in defensive and non-technology corners of the market, which is what kept the Dow green. I was not able to cross-check individual Dow leaders against two independent sources this soon after the close, so I'll leave those names out rather than report something I can't stand behind. **Gold** rose about 0.75% to roughly $4,637 an ounce, its highest since mid-May.

## Headlines that matter

**Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent launched "Operation Economic Outcast," an expanded sanctions campaign against Iran.** Treasury issued new sectoral determinations covering five areas — digital assets, technology, gold, aviation, and shipping — and its Office of Foreign Assets Control sanctioned more than 60 entities, individuals, and vessels tied to Iranian oil revenue, nuclear and missile procurement, and cyber operations. Bessent framed it as a "zero-leakage" effort to cut off the regime's money.

Why this matters, and why oil fell anyway: markets price expectations, not announcements. Traders had spent the weekend bracing for something that would physically remove Iranian barrels from the world market. What arrived was a broad financial and enforcement campaign without an immediate supply shock, and several outlets noted the announcement was light on specifics. When the reality lands softer than the fear, the risk premium that had been built into the price comes back out — so oil fell. This is one of the most useful patterns to internalize: a scary headline can coincide with a falling price when the headline is less scary than what was already assumed.

**The Treasury may tap its General Account to fund bond buybacks.** CNBC reported, citing two Treasury officials, that the department could draw on its roughly $1 trillion cash account to pay for an expanded program of buying back its own outstanding bonds. Last week Treasury surprised markets by doubling the minimum size of its long-end buybacks from $2 billion to at least $4 billion. This matters because long-term interest rates have been the market's sore spot — the 30-year yield topped 5.3% last week, its highest in nearly two decades. Those rates set the cost of mortgages, corporate borrowing, and they're the yardstick against which stocks are valued. A government that stands ready to buy its own long-term bonds puts a bid under that market and pulls yields down, which is exactly what happened today and part of why the non-tech market felt fine.

**The U.S.–Canada trade fight escalated.** A new 50% U.S. tariff on a long list of Canadian goods took effect Saturday after negotiations collapsed, and Prime Minister Mark Carney said Canada will retaliate dollar-for-dollar with counter-tariffs on $20 billion of American products starting September 8, hitting steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. The two countries traded roughly $880 billion in goods and services last year. This matters for company-level earnings across metals, lumber, autos, and retail — cost increases that show up in profit margins one or two quarters from now, not today.

**The AI hardware cost story got sharper.** Nvidia notifying customers of server price hikes above 15%, effective on systems shipping early next year and covering its Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell generations, is the market's clearest evidence yet that memory scarcity is now a real constraint on the AI build-out. Add the report that Apple is evaluating Chinese memory supplier CXMT — over the objections of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and several senators — and you have both a cost problem and a competition problem for U.S. memory makers on the same morning.

**The week's two big events are still ahead.** Nvidia reports earnings Wednesday, August 26, after the close, with Wall Street looking for roughly $91 billion in revenue and adjusted earnings around $2.09 per share. Then the Federal Reserve's Jackson Hole symposium runs August 27–29, with new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivering the keynote Friday, August 28. Much of today's cautious tone in tech was investors trimming risk ahead of those two events rather than reacting to anything that happened today.

## Today's lesson: why three indexes disagreed, and what "breadth" tells you

Today handed us an almost perfect teaching example. Same market, same day, same news — and the Dow finished up 0.26% while the Nasdaq finished down 0.76%. That gap of roughly one full percentage point isn't a contradiction. It's a lesson in how indexes are built and what "breadth" means.

An index is just a scoreboard for a chosen list of stocks, and the rule for keeping score changes the answer. The **Dow Jones Industrial Average** holds 30 large companies and is *price-weighted*: a stock's influence depends on its share price, not the company's size. The **S&P 500** holds about 500 companies and is *market-cap weighted* — a company's influence depends on its total market value, which is share price times shares outstanding. The **Nasdaq Composite** holds thousands of companies listed on the Nasdaq exchange, also market-cap weighted, and is heavily tilted toward technology. Different rosters, different scoring rules, different answers.

Now add **breadth** — the term for how many stocks are participating in a move, as opposed to how far the index traveled. The index level tells you the score; breadth tells you who scored. Today the S&P 500 fell only about a quarter of a percent, but that small number hides a lopsided day: a handful of memory and semiconductor names fell 5% to 7% each, while most of the other 490-odd companies were flat to higher. That's *narrow* weakness — a big move in a few names dragging a mild-looking average. The Dow, holding almost none of those names, simply didn't feel it.

Here's the basketball version. Imagine your team loses by two points. That final score tells you almost nothing on its own. Did everyone shoot poorly? Or did four starters play a solid game while your center fouled out in the first quarter and your best shooter went 1-for-14? Those are completely different games with the same box-score margin, and they call for completely different responses. Breadth is the difference between "the whole team was off" and "one guy had a night." Today was one position group having a bad night, not the team collapsing.

Why bother learning this? Because it changes what a headline means. "Stocks fell today" is technically true of the S&P 500 and genuinely misleading about what most stocks did. The two questions worth asking on any red or green day are: *how many* stocks moved, and *was the move concentrated in one industry?* Advancing versus declining stocks, and whether the equal-weighted S&P 500 — a version where every company counts the same regardless of size — beat or lagged the regular one, are the standard ways to check. When an index moves a lot but breadth is narrow, the move is telling you about a sector. When an index moves a little but breadth is wide, something is shifting underneath the surface that the headline number is hiding. Today was the first kind.
