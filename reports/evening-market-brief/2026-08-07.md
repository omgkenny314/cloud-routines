# Market Close Brief — Friday, August 7, 2026

A note before we start: this brief is being written on Sunday, August 9. Markets were closed Saturday and Sunday, so this covers the most recent completed trading session, which was Friday, August 7.

## The day in one paragraph

Friday was one of those days that only makes sense once you know what the Federal Reserve is worried about. The government reported that the economy lost 23,000 jobs in July when economists had expected a gain of roughly 80,000 — a genuinely bad number. And the stock market went up anyway, closing at a record. The reason is that in 2026 the Fed is not debating whether to cut interest rates; it is debating whether to raise them, because inflation has stayed above its 2% target, largely thanks to the oil shock from the Strait of Hormuz crisis. A weak jobs report takes away the Fed's justification for hiking. So traders read "the labor market is cooling" as "the Fed can sit still," and they bought. That was the single biggest storyline of the day. Underneath it, a second storyline was doing just as much work: a wave of software earnings landed and several of them were blowouts. Atlassian and Twilio both jumped double digits, and that carried the tech-heavy Nasdaq to a much bigger gain than the Dow. The S&P 500 finished its strongest week since April.

## The numbers

- S&P 500: 7,757.64, up 0.62% (a gain of 47.68 points). A record closing high, and up 3.6% for the week — its best week since April. The jobs report drove it, and big software and technology names did the heavy lifting.
- Dow Jones Industrial Average: 54,036.93, up 0.28% (a gain of 151.83 points). The Dow lagged the other two because it holds fewer high-growth technology names and more industrial and consumer companies, which had a quieter day.
- Nasdaq Composite: 26,690.62, up 1.30% (a gain of 342.26 points). Up 5.2% for the week. This is the technology-heavy index, and technology is exactly where Friday's gains were concentrated — software earnings plus a rebound in chip stocks.
- 10-year Treasury yield: about 4.60%, down roughly 7 basis points. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point, so 7 basis points is 0.07%. Yields fall when bond prices rise, and bond prices rose because a weak labor market makes a Fed rate hike less likely. I saw slightly different closing figures across sources — some showed 4.64% — so treat 4.60% as approximate; the direction and size of the move are well corroborated.
- Crude oil: Brent crude around $83.52 per barrel, up about 1.25% on the day; West Texas Intermediate around $78. Oil rose Friday on news that Iran published a restrictive draft plan for the Strait of Hormuz. But the far bigger story is the week: crude fell roughly 8% to 10% over the five days as the United States, Iran and Oman worked toward an interim deal on ship traffic through the strait. I could not pin the exact Friday settlement price down to two sources that agreed, so I am giving you the approximate level rather than inventing precision.
- Bitcoin: about $64,940, up roughly 0.8% on the day and about 2.8% over the week. Same logic as stocks — a Fed that is less likely to hike is friendlier to risky assets. Worth keeping in perspective: bitcoin's all-time high was $126,080 back in October 2025, so it is trading at roughly half that.

## Big movers and why

Atlassian (ticker TEAM), up roughly 35%. The software company behind Jira and Confluence reported fiscal fourth-quarter revenue of $1.77 billion, up 28% from a year ago, against expectations of about $1.66 billion. Adjusted earnings came in at $1.87 per share versus the $1.50 analysts expected. Cloud revenue — the part of the business Wall Street cares most about — grew 31%, and that growth rate accelerated rather than slowing. Chief executive and co-founder Mike Cannon-Brookes said he plans to buy up to $250 million of the stock on the open market, which markets generally read as a confidence signal. This is our example in today's lesson below.

Twilio (ticker TWLO), up roughly 24%, and up as much as 31% at one point during the day. Twilio provides the messaging and communications plumbing that other companies' apps use. Second-quarter revenue was $1.5 billion versus $1.43 billion expected, up 22% from a year earlier, with adjusted earnings of $1.47 per share against $1.32 expected. The bigger deal was the guidance: Twilio raised its full-year 2026 revenue growth forecast to a range of 18% to 18.5%, up from 14% to 15%. That is a very large upward revision, and guidance often moves a stock more than the quarter itself.

Coherent (ticker COHR), up nearly 16%, and up about 47% for the week. Coherent makes photonics and optical components — the parts that move data around inside AI data centers. Shares moved on a Reuters report that the Trump administration is drafting a ban on imports of Chinese data-center components, which would push demand toward domestic suppliers like Coherent.

Airbnb (ticker ABNB), up about 15%. Revenue rose 17% from a year ago to $3.61 billion, and the company raised its full-year outlook, guiding to at least mid-teens revenue growth. Travel demand held up better than investors feared.

SpaceX (ticker SPCX), up 12%, and up about 19% for the week. This one is not about earnings. Insider lock-up provisions expired, meaning early shareholders became free to sell — and rather than the flood of selling some expected, investors bought the newly available shares. Separately, SpaceX has been in the news because chief executive Elon Musk said earlier in the week that SpaceX will use Nvidia chips exclusively for its AI compute buildout, both on the ground and in orbit.

The Trade Desk (ticker TTD), down roughly 22%. The advertising technology company reported second-quarter revenue of about $715 million against expectations near $752 million, and adjusted earnings of $0.34 per share versus $0.40 expected. A string of analysts cut their price targets hard afterward. Reported percentage declines varied across sources from about 22% to 27%; either way it was the day's ugliest large-cap move.

Nvidia (ticker NVDA), up more than 1%. A modest day for the chipmaker, but it extended the gains from Musk's exclusivity announcement earlier in the week.

Wendy's (ticker WEN), down about 2%. The burger chain beat on earnings and revenue, but global sales fell more than 6%. A reminder that beating the earnings estimate does not save you if the underlying business is shrinking.

## Headlines that matter

The July jobs report was the whole ballgame. Nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 when economists expected a gain of roughly 80,000. Just as important, the prior two months were revised down by a combined 103,000 jobs — May was cut from +129,000 to +63,000, and June from +57,000 to +20,000. The unemployment rate actually ticked down to 4.1%, but for an unhealthy reason: fewer people were counted as working or looking for work, which shrinks the denominator. Average hourly earnings rose just 3.2% over the past year, the slowest since May 2021. Why this matters: the Fed watches wage growth closely because rising wages can feed back into rising prices. Slowing wage growth is evidence that the inflation problem may be easing on its own.

One wrinkle worth knowing: a large chunk of the job losses — 53,000 — came from government payrolls, and several economists think that reflects seasonal quirks that could get revised away later. Private payrolls actually grew by about 30,000. So the headline number was uglier than the underlying picture. This is a good habit to build: always look at what is inside a headline economic number before you accept the headline.

Rate-hike expectations moved sharply. Odds of a September rate hike fell to about 44% on the CME Group's FedWatch tool, which reads those probabilities out of interest-rate futures prices. Other measures showed roughly a 60% to 65% chance the Fed simply holds steady in September. Why this matters: nearly every asset price is partly a bet on interest rates. When the expected path of rates moves, everything reprices at once — which is exactly why stocks, bonds, and bitcoin all moved the same direction Friday.

The Strait of Hormuz situation is the inflation story underneath the Fed story. Roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes through that waterway. The U.S., Iran and Oman have been negotiating an interim deal on ship traffic, and progress there is why oil fell sharply over the week. But Iran published a restrictive draft plan — seeking to exclude U.S. and Israeli vessels and to charge fees to countries it considers hostile — and oil bounced Friday. Why this matters: expensive oil raises the cost of nearly everything that gets shipped, manufactured, or driven. If oil keeps falling, inflation cools and the Fed relaxes. If the strait closes back up, the whole "Fed can stay on hold" thesis that powered Friday's rally comes apart.

Software earnings were the sector story. Beyond Atlassian and Twilio, the iShares Expanded Tech-Software Sector ETF — a fund that holds a basket of software companies, ticker IGV — rose nearly 3%. Why this matters: for two years the AI trade has been mostly about companies that sell picks and shovels — chips, power, data centers. Software companies are the ones that have to prove AI actually generates revenue for them. Friday was a data point in that direction.

## Today's lesson: operating margin, and why Atlassian jumped 35%

Here is a question worth sitting with. Atlassian's revenue grew 28%. Its stock rose about 35%. Why did the stock move more than the business grew?

The answer is operating margin — and it is one of the most useful things you can learn to look at in a company's results. Operating margin is simply: out of every dollar of revenue that comes in the door, how many cents are left as profit after paying the costs of running the business? If a company brings in $100 and has $88 in costs, it keeps $12, and its operating margin is 12%.

Atlassian's operating margin did something dramatic. A year ago the company had an operating loss — it spent more running the business than it took in. This quarter it posted $211 million in operating income, a 12% margin. On the adjusted basis the company prefers to report, the margin went from 24% to 36%. So revenue grew 28%, but profit went from negative to solidly positive. That gap is the whole story of the stock move.

The reason this happens is called operating leverage, and it is a structural feature of software companies. Atlassian's biggest costs — engineers, product development, corporate overhead — are largely fixed. It costs roughly the same to build Jira whether 10,000 companies use it or 12,000. So when revenue climbs, most of the new dollars fall straight to the bottom line instead of being eaten by new costs. Revenue grows 28%, profit grows much faster than 28%. That is operating leverage working in your favor. It works brutally in reverse too: when revenue falls at a company with high fixed costs, profits collapse far faster than sales do.

Think of it in basketball terms. Revenue growth is taking more shots. Margin expansion is making a higher percentage of the shots you already take. A team that goes from 40% to 48% shooting without changing anything else scores a lot more points, and it is a much more convincing sign of a good team than simply chucking up more attempts. When you read an earnings report from here on, do not stop at "revenue grew X%." Ask the second question: did the margin go up, down, or sideways? A company growing revenue while its margin shrinks is buying its growth — spending more to get each new dollar. A company growing revenue while its margin expands is getting genuinely more efficient. Wall Street pays very differently for those two things, and Friday showed you exactly how differently.
