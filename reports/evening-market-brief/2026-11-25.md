# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, November 25, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a day the market lost badly for about an hour and then clawed almost all of it back. At the opening bell the Nasdaq Composite was down more than 4%, the S&P 500 was off about 2.8%, and it looked like the start of something ugly — two separate blows had landed before the market even opened. The first was a new tariff proposal from the administration covering fourteen trading partners. The second was a wall of economic data, released a day early because of Thanksgiving, that was strong on growth and hot on inflation — which in today's market is bad news, because it makes a Federal Reserve interest-rate *increase* in December more likely, not less. Then, starting late morning, buyers showed up. By the close the Dow Jones Industrial Average was actually **up** 0.69%, the S&P 500 had shaved its 2.8% loss down to 0.08%, and the Nasdaq finished down just 0.52%. Reuters noted that volume was low and described the buyers as bargain hunters stepping in ahead of the holiday.

The single biggest storyline, though, is not the reversal — it is where the pressure is coming from. For a second straight day, the selling concentrated in artificial-intelligence stocks, and the reason has shifted from "are these companies worth this much?" to "can they pay their debts?" The cost to insure the debt of Oracle, Alphabet, SpaceX and CoreWeave against default hit record highs today. That is a different kind of worry than a stock being expensive, and it is why the AI trade keeps failing to hold a bounce.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: 7,195.06, down 6.03 points or 0.08%.** Essentially flat, but that flatness hides a 2.8% morning loss that was almost entirely recovered. The broad index sits between the two extremes below.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 50,365.85, up 346.96 points or 0.69%.** The Dow holds few AI-linked names and more old-economy industrials and defensive companies, so money rotating *out* of technology had to land somewhere, and a lot of it landed here. The Dow closed above 50,000 for a third straight session.

- **Nasdaq Composite: 23,611.61, down 121.93 points or 0.52%.** The technology-heavy index took the brunt of the chip and AI selling. It had been down more than 4% intraday — that gap between the low and the close is the story of the day, and it is tonight's lesson.

- **10-year Treasury yield: about 4.84%, down roughly 10 basis points** from 4.904% on Tuesday (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point). This is the day's most interesting number. Yields fall when investors buy bonds for safety, and that is what happened during the morning panic — even though hot inflation data normally pushes yields *up*. Fear beat inflation today. This is near the lowest level since September 9.

- **Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate settled around $83.68, up about 1.99%; Brent around $94.61, up about 3.51%.** Oil went up while stocks went down, which tells you the driver was supply, not demand: fresh attacks on tanker shipping around the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway that a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes through.

- **Bitcoin: roughly $59,000 to $60,000.** It fell to about $55,130 earlier in the day — its weakest since September 2024 — then rebounded with stocks. **I could not verify an exact closing price**; crypto trades around the clock, so there is no official close, and sources disagreed on the level. Worth knowing: the Crypto Fear & Greed Index read 13 today, in "extreme fear," and bitcoin is down more than half from its October 2025 record near $126,200.

It guided next-quarter revenue to $70 million to $75 million, well below what analysts expected. The stock had already dropped far more in after-hours trading last night, so today's move is the rest of that same reaction.

The quarter itself was fine — adjusted profit of $0.73 a share on revenue of $772.4 million, up 15% and ahead of estimates. A good quarter plus a departing CEO plus soft guidance is a losing combination.

Fourth-quarter revenue came in at $16.0 billion, up 9.3% from a year earlier, beating expectations. Note how differently this went from Hewlett Packard Enterprise, the separate company that sells AI servers and fell 16% yesterday. Selling laptops is a less glamorous business, but nobody is worried about whether HP can service its debt.

- **Nvidia (ticker NVDA), the designer of the chips nearly all AI systems run on, fell roughly 5% to 6%.** **I could not confirm the exact close** — intraday readings I found ranged from about $142.58 to $147.84, and one report gave a close of $145.45, down 6.39%. Tuesday's close was $155.38. The context is remarkable: Nvidia surged more than 30% on Monday when its board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive, and has now given back a large part of that in two days. The leadership question got answered; the spending and debt questions did not.

- **CoreWeave (ticker CRWV), which rents out AI computing capacity, and Nebius (ticker NBIS), a similar cloud provider, fell roughly 8% to 10%** in premarket trading, and the big technology platforms — Oracle (ORCL), Alphabet (GOOGL), Amazon (AMZN), Meta (META) and Microsoft (MSFT) — were each down between 3.1% and 6.1% early. Six trading days after the largest initial public offering ever, the stock is underwater against the price institutions paid for it.

- **Autodesk (ticker ADSK), the design-software company, fell about 5%** after its results and forecast, and **Workday (ticker WDAY), the human-resources and finance software company, whipsawed** — down about 4% early, then roughly +1.9% by mid-afternoon. - **Gold fell sharply**, dropping below $3,500 an ounce to its lowest since September 2025, with COMEX gold down about 2.4% and silver down about 4%. Gold falling on a fearful day is unusual. The explanation is interest rates: gold pays you nothing to hold it, so when the odds of higher rates rise, gold gets less attractive compared with a Treasury bond paying 4.84%.

## Headlines that matter

**A new tariff proposal covering fourteen trading partners.** The U.S. Trade Representative proposed 15% tariffs on the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland, and a 20% baseline on most Chinese goods that could rise to 40% if Washington decides Beijing violated the terms of their truce. The stated justification is policies that created excess industrial capacity. China's Foreign Ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun called the overcapacity claim a false issue; the European Commission said only that it is taking note. *Why it matters:* this proposal follows a Supreme Court decision that went against the earlier round of global tariffs, so it is an attempt to achieve the same end by a route that survives legal challenge. For markets the mechanism is simple — a tariff is a tax on imported goods, it raises costs for companies that import, and those companies either eat the cost (lower profits) or pass it on (more inflation). Either way it lands on the same two things this market is already anxious about.

**Core inflation hit its highest level in nearly three years.** The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, the core personal consumption expenditures price index, rose 3.3% from a year earlier in October — up from 3.1% in September, above the 3.2% economists expected, and the hottest since November 2023. "Core" means food and energy prices are stripped out, because those bounce around for reasons that have little to do with the underlying trend. Headline PCE was 3.8%, in line with forecasts. *Why it matters:* the Fed's target is 2%. This number is going the wrong way, and it was the last major inflation reading before the Fed's December 8–9 meeting.

**The economy grew much faster than anyone thought.** The second estimate of third-quarter gross domestic product — the total value of everything the country produces — came in at a 4.3% annual pace, revised up sharply from the first estimate of 3.4% and well above the 3.5% expected. That is the fastest growth since the third quarter of 2023, and the upgrade came mostly from business investment, much of it spending on software and AI infrastructure. *Why it matters:* this is the clearest possible example of good news being bad news for stocks. A fast-growing economy with 3.3% core inflation gives the Fed every reason to raise rates. And notice the irony — a meaningful chunk of that growth *is* the AI build-out that the market spent today selling off.

**Unemployment claims fell to among the lowest levels on record.** Initial claims for jobless benefits dropped 13,000 to 189,000 for the week ended November 21, against the 200,000 expected; one outlet noted it was among the lowest in records going back to 1967. Continuing claims were about 1.697 million. *Why it matters:* same logic. A labor market this tight means employers compete for workers, wages rise, and wage growth feeds inflation. There is no slack here for the Fed to point at.

**Markets still expect a rate hike in two weeks.** The CME FedWatch tool put the odds of a quarter-point *increase* at the December meeting at about 92% after today's data, down slightly from 97% a day earlier. *Why it matters:* this is the fact underneath everything else in this brief. Higher interest rates hurt expensive, fast-growing companies much more than cheap, steady ones, because a growth company's value rests on profits far in the future, and higher rates make future money worth less today. That single asymmetry explains why the Dow rose 0.69% while the Nasdaq fell.

**The credit market is now the AI story.** The cost to insure the debt of Oracle, Alphabet, SpaceX and CoreWeave against default reached record highs, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence data. These are credit default swaps — essentially insurance policies on a company's bonds, where a higher price means the market sees more risk of non-payment. CoreWeave's five-year spread reached 1,314 basis points, a distressed level that implies roughly a 68% chance of default over five years under standard pricing models, and its 2031 bonds yielded near 18%. Oracle's was 3.56%. SoftBank's reached its highest since October 2022. *Why it matters:* a falling stock price means investors think a company is worth less. A distressed bond price means lenders think they might not get paid back. The second is far more serious, and it constrains behavior — a company whose borrowing costs spike has to slow down its spending, and in this case that spending is what other AI companies book as revenue. The underlying fear is straightforward: the returns on enormous data centers may arrive more slowly than the debt payments come due.

## Today's lesson: support, and what a 4% round trip tells you

Tonight's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning we read the behavior of the price itself rather than the company's financial statements. The concept is **support**, and today handed us an unusually clean example, because the market went down to a specific place, stopped, and turned around.

Support is a price level where buyers have stepped in before, and where the market therefore expects them to step in again. That is the whole idea — it is memory. If a stock or an index fell to a certain level a week ago and bounced hard from it, that level becomes a reference point. Traders who missed the bounce remember it and place orders there. Traders who bought there and watched it work will buy there again. Enough people acting on the same memory can make the level hold, which then reinforces the memory. Support is not a law of physics; it is a crowd's habit. The mirror image is **resistance** — a level overhead where sellers have shown up before.

Here is today's example, and the numbers are specific enough to be useful. Last Friday, November 20, the Nasdaq 100 — the index of the hundred largest non-financial companies on the Nasdaq — bottomed at an intraday low of 24,910.48. That became the reference point. This morning, in the first half hour of trading, with the tariff news and the inflation data both landing at once, the index fell to 24,970.66. That is about 60 points above Friday's low — close enough to call it a test of the same level, and it did not break. Buyers came in, and the Nasdaq Composite, which had been down more than 4%, finished the day down only 0.52%. The S&P 500 turned a 2.8% loss into 0.08%. The Dow went from down 1.4% to up 0.69%.

The basketball version: a team goes down 22 points in the first quarter, and the question is not whether the deficit was real — it was — but what happens at the moment the other team's lead stops growing. If you have watched this team claw back from 20 before, you do not write them off at the same number twice. Today the market reached the same scoreline where it had rallied five days earlier, and the bench stood up.

Two honest cautions, because support is the concept beginners most often overtrust. First, support is a **zone, not a line** — 24,970 versus 24,910 is a 60-point difference, and treating either number as exact would be false precision. The useful statement is "the market returned to roughly where it bounced last week and bounced again." Second, a level holding once tells you very little about whether it holds next time, and today's hold is weaker evidence than usual because, as Reuters specifically noted, **volume was low**. Volume is the number of shares traded, and it is the crowd-size measure behind every price move. A level defended by a thin holiday crowd has not really been tested by the full market. The same level, hit on a heavy-volume Monday with everyone back at their desks, is the test that counts — and when a support level does finally break, the move through it is often fast, precisely because all those buyers who were counting on it are now wrong at the same moment.
