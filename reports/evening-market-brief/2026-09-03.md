# Market Close Brief — Thursday, September 3, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a broad, comfortable rally, and it happened for one reason: a Federal Reserve official said something that made an interest rate hike this month look less likely. Fed Governor Christopher Waller said he would be inclined to support holding interest rates where they are at the September 15–16 meeting, as long as the inflation data due over the next two weeks keeps improving. That one sentence moved a lot of money. Traders had been pricing in a real chance the Fed would *raise* rates this month — remember, the story this year has been stubborn inflation, not rate cuts — and Waller's comment knocked those odds down by roughly 12 percentage points in a single afternoon. Bond yields fell, and stocks climbed across almost every sector. The Dow had its best day in a month. The rally was wide rather than narrow: this wasn't three big technology names carrying the market, it was most of the market moving together, which is generally the healthier version. The one sour note came from chips, where Broadcom fell despite good results, and the day's real drama arrived after the closing bell, when Lululemon reported a quarter that looked great on the profit line and fell apart everywhere else.

## Big movers and why

**Snowflake (ticker SNOW), up more than 20%.** The data-warehousing company reported second-quarter results after Wednesday's close: adjusted profit of 62 cents per share on revenue of $1.55 billion, beating expectations, with strong forward guidance. Guidance means the company's own forecast for upcoming quarters. A beat plus a raised outlook is the combination the market rewards most, and this was the day's biggest winner.

**Robinhood Markets (ticker HOOD), up roughly 11% to 15%.** No earnings here — this was Wall Street changing its mind. Morgan Stanley upgraded the stock and Deutsche Bank published research arguing that prediction markets, where users bet on the outcome of real-world events, are a large and growing revenue opportunity for brokerages. Morgan Stanley's note pointed out that only about 7% of Robinhood's user base has tried the product so far, which is the bullish part of the argument: lots of room left. It was the stock's best day since August 21.

**Broadcom (ticker AVGO), down roughly 3.5% to 4.5%.** The chipmaker actually beat on both lines Wednesday evening — revenue of $29.59 billion against a $29.36 billion estimate, adjusted profit of $3.32 per share against $3.24 expected — and its artificial-intelligence revenue grew 221%. It fell anyway, for two reasons. First, its forecast for the current quarter, about $34.8 billion in revenue, came in under the roughly $35.03 billion analysts wanted. Second, profitability is slipping: gross margin fell 210 basis points from the prior quarter, and the company guided fourth-quarter gross margin to 73%, down from 78% a year earlier. When a stock is priced for AI perfection, merely very good is a disappointment.

**Hewlett Packard Enterprise (ticker HPE), down about 4%,** also on its earnings report.

## Headlines that matter

**Waller's comments were the day's engine.** He said, "If this continues in the data due over the next two weeks, I would be inclined to support holding the target for the federal funds rate at its current setting," and added, "We can wait one meeting." Why this matters: the federal funds rate is the Fed's main lever, and it sets the floor under borrowing costs everywhere — mortgages, business loans, and the rate investors use to value future company profits. Higher rates make future profits worth less today, which is why stocks fall on hike fears and rally on hold signals. Market-implied odds of a September hike dropped sharply after he spoke; sources put the new figure somewhere between roughly 50% and 55%, down from the low-to-mid 60s a day earlier. Note the conditional, though — Waller left a hike on the table if inflation surprises. The August inflation report lands September 11, before the Fed meets.

**The inflation trend is genuinely improving.** Waller noted that the three-month rate on the Fed's preferred inflation gauge has fallen from 4.76% in February to about 3.05% now. That is the evidence behind his willingness to wait.

**ISM Services came in hot at 55.4 for August,** above the 54.2 expected and up from 54.1 in July. This survey measures activity in the services economy, which is most of the U.S. economy; any reading above 50 means expansion. New orders hit 60.9, a three-and-a-half-year high. But two components complicate the picture: prices paid rose to 72.6, which says businesses are still facing cost pressure, and the employment component was 47.8 — below 50, meaning services companies are still shedding jobs on balance. Strong demand, sticky prices, weak hiring.

**Weekly jobless claims were 206,000,** essentially flat and roughly in line with the 205,000 expected. Continuing claims rose slightly to 1.779 million. This is the "low-hire, low-fire" labor market the Fed keeps describing: companies aren't laying people off in large numbers, but they aren't hiring much either.

**The July trade deficit widened 24.4% to $88.6 billion,** as imports jumped 2.8% and exports fell 2.1%. Strong import demand says the American consumer is still spending, though a wider deficit is a direct subtraction from measured economic growth.

**The Iran conflict continues to sit under the oil price.** The U.S. launched fresh strikes on Iranian targets near the Strait of Hormuz this week after roughly a month of calm, and Iran retaliated with drones and missiles aimed at American bases in the region. President Trump said the strikes would be short-lived while signaling readiness for more. The Strait of Hormuz is the chokepoint through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes, so any threat to it puts a risk premium into crude. Oil held near six-week highs today even as Brent slipped, and expensive energy feeds straight back into the inflation numbers the Fed is watching.

## After the bell

**Lululemon Athletica (ticker LULU) fell about 15% in after-hours trading**, and the reason is worth slowing down for. On the surface the quarter was a blowout: adjusted earnings of $2.92 per share against roughly $1.82 expected. But $0.86 of that came from tariff refunds and associated interest — a one-time cash windfall, not the business selling more leggings. Strip it out and you get about $2.06, a much more ordinary beat. Meanwhile revenue fell 4% year over year to about $2.4 billion, missing the $2.46 billion estimate, and comparable sales dropped 9% (10% excluding currency effects), with the Americas down 12%. Guidance for the rest of the year came in well below what Wall Street was modeling. The stock was already down roughly 42% this year heading in.

**DocuSign (ticker DOCU) was also scheduled to report this afternoon.** Its results were not available at the time I wrote this, and analysts had been looking for revenue growth of around 8%.

## What to watch tomorrow

**The August jobs report at 8:30 a.m. Eastern (7:30 a.m. Central) is the whole day.** Economists expect nonfarm payrolls to have grown by about 53,000, with the unemployment rate holding at 4.1%. Context matters here: July payrolls actually *fell* by 23,000, and the two prior months were revised down by a combined 103,000.

This report matters more than usual because of the direction the Fed is leaning. In a normal cycle, weak jobs data would raise hopes of rate cuts and lift stocks. Right now the Fed is debating whether to *hike*, so the logic runs differently: a hot jobs number would argue that the economy can take higher rates and could push September hike odds back up, undoing today's rally. A soft number supports Waller's case for waiting. Either way, expect the bond market to move first and stocks to follow.

## Today's lesson: comparable sales, or the number that tells you if the business is actually working

Lululemon reported a profit nearly 60% above what analysts expected and the stock dropped 15%. The number that explains that gap is **comparable sales**, usually shortened to "comps" and sometimes called same-store sales.

Here is the idea. A retailer can grow revenue in two very different ways. It can open new stores, or it can sell more in the stores it already had open a year ago. Total revenue mashes those together. Comparable sales strips out the new stores and asks a narrower, harder question: at the locations that existed a year ago, are customers spending more or less than they did then? (Companies usually fold their established e-commerce sales into the same measure.) It's the cleanest available read on whether people actually want the product more this year than last.

Lululemon's comps fell 9% — and 12% in the Americas, its home market. That means the existing stores sold meaningfully less merchandise than the same stores did a year ago. No amount of accounting makes that a good number, and it's why the profit beat didn't save the stock. That beat leaned on $0.86 per share of tariff refunds, money the government sent back, which has nothing to do with selling athletic wear and will not repeat next quarter. Investors looked past the one-time cash to the underlying demand signal, and the underlying demand signal was negative.

The basketball version: a team's total points can go up because they played more possessions in a faster game. That doesn't tell you they got better. Points *per possession* does — same conditions, same yardstick, so you can actually compare. Comps are retail's points per possession. And it's worth noticing what today's two big earnings reactions had in common: Broadcom beat and fell, Lululemon beat and fell harder. In both cases the market ignored the headline number and went straight to the question underneath it — is this business getting stronger or weaker? That question is what actually moves stocks. This is context for your learning, not a suggestion to do anything with any of these stocks.
