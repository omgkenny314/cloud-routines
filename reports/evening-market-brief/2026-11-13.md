# Market Close Brief — Friday, November 13, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

This was a split day, and the split itself is the story. The Dow Jones Industrial Average finished slightly higher while the Nasdaq Composite fell nearly a full percent — the same divide you have been watching all week, only sharper. The headline driver was the second day of fallout from Jensen Huang's resignation as chief executive of Nvidia (ticker NVDA), the artificial-intelligence chipmaker. But the more interesting move was not in Nvidia itself, which fell modestly. It was in the stocks that *rose* on Thursday as money fled Nvidia and looked for a new favorite. Advanced Micro Devices (ticker AMD), Nvidia's closest rival, had surged roughly 30% over the prior week on exactly that logic. Today it gave back about 10% and was the worst-performing stock in the S&P 500. Underneath all of it sat a harder problem: wholesale inflation came in at its highest annual rate in nearly four years, the 10-year Treasury yield stayed above 5%, and traders now put the odds of the Federal Reserve *raising* interest rates in December at roughly nine in ten. That is the real backdrop. The Nvidia drama is loud, but the bond market is what is actually repricing everything.

That is the whole lesson of the move: it had risen about 30% in a week on the theory that Nvidia's leadership crisis was AMD's opportunity. Today that theory got re-examined and the trade unwound. A stock that goes up 30% on a story can give back 10% when the story is merely questioned.

## Headlines that matter

**Wholesale inflation hit a four-year high.** The October Producer Price Index, released this morning, rose 0.3% from September — slightly *cooler* than the 0.4% expected — but climbed **6.5% over the past 12 months**, the largest annual increase since November 2022. Core PPI, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.3% monthly and 5% annually, a rate last seen in January 2023. September's figure was revised *upward* to a 1.4% surge.

Why this matters: the PPI measures what producers receive at the wholesale level — the cost side of the economy, upstream of what you pay at the register. It is a preview of pressure that either shows up in consumer prices later or gets absorbed by companies in the form of thinner profit margins. Neither outcome is good for stocks. Much of the pressure traces to the energy shock from the Iran war; wholesale prices for fresh and dry vegetables alone jumped 26.7% in October.

**The Fed is now expected to raise rates, not cut them.** After the PPI report, futures markets put the probability of a quarter-point hike at the December 8–9 meeting at roughly 91%, up from the mid-to-high 80s earlier in the week. Roughly 9% odds are on a half-point move, and Governor Waller said the PPI data alone would not take a half-point hike off the table.

Why this matters more than almost anything else in this brief: for two years the market's working assumption was that rates were headed down. That assumption is now inverted. Higher rates make borrowing costlier for companies, make safe Treasury bonds a more attractive competitor to stocks, and — critically — reduce the present value of profits that a company expects to earn far in the future. That last point is why expensive, fast-growing technology stocks are taking more damage than the Dow's steadier, cash-generating businesses. It is not that AI suddenly stopped working. It is that the discount applied to distant profits got bigger.

**A nationwide FAA ground stop late in the session.** The Federal Aviation Administration ordered a halt to all U.S. flight departures, citing "extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety," after radio frequency and air-traffic-control communication outages were reported. Restrictions were reportedly being lifted gradually as the evening went on, with lingering delays in New York and Boston, but I could not find an official FAA statement confirming a full all-clear. Airline stocks were reported as little-moved. Worth knowing if you or anyone you know is flying this weekend; check directly with the airline.

**Oil supply is finally catching up with demand.** The International Energy Agency's November report said global supply moved ahead of demand for the first time since the Iran war began — supply up 2.3 million barrels a day to 105.4 million, with Gulf crude exports back to about 82% of pre-war levels. U.S. crude inventories rose 4.7 million barrels against expectations of a draw. This is the mechanism behind oil's second weekly decline, and it matters because energy prices are the main thing currently driving that 6.5% wholesale inflation number.

## Today's lesson: the gap between what companies pay and what they can charge

Tonight's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning we study the business itself, what it sells and what it costs to make, rather than the behavior of its stock chart. The concept is the **gap between producer prices and consumer prices**, and today's inflation report handed us an unusually wide one.

Start with the two measures. The **Consumer Price Index**, or CPI, tracks what you and I pay at the register. The **Producer Price Index**, or PPI, tracks prices one step upstream — what producers receive, and by extension roughly what the next company in the chain pays for materials, components and wholesale goods. CPI is the ticket price. PPI is the cost of fielding the team.

Now put today's numbers side by side. Wholesale prices rose **6.5% over twelve months**. Consumer inflation, by the most recent official annual reading, was **3.7%**, and a Bank of America estimate for October put it near 3.8%. So costs upstream are climbing at close to twice the rate of prices downstream. That gap — call it roughly two and a half to three percentage points — has to go somewhere, and there are only two places it can go.

It can go to you, as higher prices later, which is why economists treat PPI as a partial early-warning signal for future CPI. Or it absorbs into company profit margins, meaning businesses eat the cost increase because they do not believe customers will tolerate a price rise. Watch which companies can do which, because that is one of the most revealing tests of business quality there is. A company that raises prices 6% and keeps every customer has real pricing power — a genuine competitive advantage. A company that must swallow a 6% cost increase to hold its shelf space does not, however good its last quarter looked. Same input cost, two completely different businesses, and the difference shows up in the gross margin line one or two quarters later, not today.

Here is the basketball version. Player salaries across the league jump 6.5%. Ticket and concession prices are only up 3.7%. Every team faces the identical cost shock, but the outcomes diverge completely. The marquee franchise with a waiting list for season tickets raises prices and the building still sells out. The struggling team in a small market raises prices and the arena empties, so it absorbs the cost and the owner's profit shrinks — or it cuts payroll and gets worse. The cost increase was league-wide. What it *did* to each team depended entirely on how much pricing power that team had, and you could not tell from the salary number alone.

Two honest limits. First, PPI and CPI do not measure the same basket of goods, so the gap between them is suggestive, not a clean forecast — plenty of wholesale cost pressure never reaches consumers, and services are weighted very differently in the two indexes. Second, today's comparison is rougher than usual: the Bureau of Labor Statistics did not collect consumer prices in October 2025 during the government funding lapse, so there is **no official annual CPI rate right now**. The 3.7% figure is September's, and the 3.8% is a bank's estimate with an assumed baseline. The official annual rate is expected to return with the November report on December 10. I am showing you the comparison because the direction is clear and large, but you should know the denominator is partly estimated — and knowing which of your numbers is soft is itself a piece of analytical skill.
