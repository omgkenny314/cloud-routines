# Market Close Brief — Friday, September 25, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Stocks finished a rough, choppy week on a genuinely good note. All three major indexes rose, and all three ended the week higher — the Dow up 0.3% for the week, the S&P 500 up 1.2%, the Nasdaq up 2%. The single biggest storyline is that the two forces that have been fighting each other all week finally both leaned the same way for a few hours: oil fell hard, and stocks liked it. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, floated a proposal at the United Nations to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days if the United States lifts its naval blockade, waives sanctions on Iranian oil sales, and observes a ceasefire that would include Lebanon. West Texas Intermediate crude dropped more than 2%. The second storyline is one that did not cooperate: the bond market kept selling off anyway. The 10-year Treasury yield pushed above 5.2%, a fresh high since mid-2007, capping a week in which it climbed roughly 23 basis points. So Friday was not "all clear." It was stocks deciding that cheaper oil mattered more today than more expensive money.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: 7,743.41, up 0.51%.** Cheaper oil eased the inflation worry that has been pressing on the market all week. Still below the record close of 7,764.70 set Monday.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: 51,828.62, up 478.64 points, or 0.93%.** The best of the three, and it snapped a three-day losing streak. The Dow leans toward industrial, energy-consuming and financial companies, the parts of the market most sensitive to fuel costs.
- **Nasdaq Composite: 27,068.72, up about 0.5%.** Led by artificial-intelligence infrastructure names. Also still short of its record close of 27,244.28 from Tuesday.
- **10-year Treasury yield: about 5.22%, up roughly 1 basis point on the day.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. The small daily move hides the week: this is the highest level since mid-2007, after a roughly 23 basis point climb over the prior three sessions. The drivers are hawkish Fed commentary, strong economic data, and worry about how much debt the government has to sell.
- **Crude oil: West Texas Intermediate down 2.33% to settle at $92.41 a barrel; Brent down 2.14% to $104.32.** Entirely the Hormuz headline.
- **Bitcoin: roughly $84,000 to $85,000, essentially flat to slightly lower.** It traded near $85,200 in the morning and drifted back above $84,000 later. I could not verify a precise 4 p.m. price from two sources, so I am not giving one. It remains well below Wednesday's high near $87,265, pressured by rising odds of another Fed rate increase.

## Big movers and why

- **Atlas Energy Solutions (AESI), up about 17%.** The oilfield and energy-logistics company said two of its subsidiaries "executed separate cost reimbursement agreements with a leading frontier AI lab." That is an energy-services company being paid by an AI company — the clearest sign yet that the data-center buildout is reaching into corners of the market that have nothing obvious to do with software.
- **Genius Sports (GENI), up about 13%.** JPMorgan started covering the stock with an overweight rating, citing what it called a "scarce combo of diversified above-market growth, strong execution, improving profitability/[free cash flow], and attractive valuation." No news from the company itself — this was an analyst opinion moving a stock.
- **People Inc. (PPLI), up about 11%, closing at $40.05.** The Wall Street Journal reported that MGM Resorts International is weighing a takeover bid for People Inc., possibly within days. This is the mirror image of the story from two days ago, when People Inc. — the publisher of PEOPLE, Food & Wine and Better Homes & Gardens, formerly called IAC — withdrew its own $48.30-a-share proposal to buy the rest of MGM. People Inc. already owns roughly 27% of MGM. The hunter may now be the hunted.
- **Akamai Technologies (AKAM), up roughly 8%, after being up as much as 16.4% intraday.** The big one, and worth reading carefully. Akamai signed a seven-year, $11.6 billion agreement to supply computing capacity to Anthropic, expandable toward roughly $20 billion. Unusually, the capacity is central processing units rather than graphics processing units — CPUs, the general-purpose chips, rather than the GPUs that dominate AI headlines. Akamai also issued Anthropic a warrant, which is the right to buy shares at a set price later, covering up to about 5% of the company at $111.33 a share. Akamai expects no revenue from the deal this year, roughly $150 million to $300 million in 2027, and about $5.5 billion of capital spending tied to it. Piper Sandler raised its price target to $158 from $125. A note on the number: sources reported Friday's gain anywhere from 5% to 23%. The figure best supported across sources is a close up about 8%, from a prior close of $110.41, after an intraday high of $128.46. Today's lesson is about exactly that gap between the pop and the close.
- **Costco Wholesale (COST), barely higher.** Fiscal fourth-quarter adjusted earnings of $6.60 a share on revenue of $95.72 billion, against expectations of $6.53 and $94.86 billion. A beat on both lines, and the stock did close to nothing. When a good report produces no move, the market is telling you the good news was already in the price.

## Headlines that matter

**Iran's seven-day offer.** Araghchi laid out the proposal privately on the sidelines of the U.N. General Assembly and it was conveyed to Washington through mediators. It closely resembles the Memorandum of Understanding struck in June, but on a faster clock — that earlier deal had a 60-day interim period and collapsed when Iran resumed attacks on shipping. Why it matters: roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil normally moves through the Strait of Hormuz, and it has been largely closed through a conflict now in its seventh month. Oil near $92 is the single biggest input into the inflation fear that is driving the bond market. If the strait reopens, the inflation story changes, and so does the case for more Fed rate increases. President Trump has said he is in no hurry.

**Consumer sentiment is grim, and inflation expectations are getting worse.** The University of Michigan's final September consumer sentiment index came in at 48.1, revised up slightly from a preliminary 47.8, but down 7% from August and 12.7% from a year ago. Views of both current and year-ahead personal finances deteriorated by around 10%. Most important for the Fed: consumers now expect prices to rise 4.6% over the next year, up from 4.0% in August and the highest since June. Why that matters: the Fed watches inflation expectations closely, because what people expect prices to do shapes what they demand in wages and accept in prices — expectations can become self-fulfilling. One oddity in the data is worth noting — buying conditions for durable goods actually improved, partly because some consumers think buying now beats paying more later. That is inflation psychology showing up in behavior.

**Durable goods orders were flat.** August new orders for manufactured durable goods — long-lasting items like machinery, appliances and aircraft — were $338.6 billion, down $0.1 billion, essentially unchanged after a 0.9% rise in July. Excluding transportation, orders rose 0.3%. Transportation equipment fell 0.6%, its third decline in four months. A flat headline with a modestly positive core is a picture of manufacturing that is neither accelerating nor breaking.

**The Fed is in a tightening posture, and this is the thing to keep in your head.** At its September meeting the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point to a range of 3.75% to 4.00% — the first increase since 2023. Sixteen of eighteen officials see at least one more quarter-point increase possible this year, and four penciled in two. Markets are currently pricing roughly a two-thirds chance of another increase next month. Why it matters: for most of the last two years the market's default assumption was that the next move in rates would be down. That assumption has flipped. Everything else — the 5.2% 10-year yield, pressure on bitcoin, the Dow's sensitivity to oil — sits downstream of it.

## Today's lesson: the opening gap, and why the close tells you more than the pop

Today's lesson is a technical one — technical analysis meaning the study of price and trading behavior itself, rather than the business underneath. Akamai handed us the cleanest example I have seen in weeks.

A **gap** is when a stock opens at a meaningfully different price than where it closed the day before, leaving a blank space on the chart because no trading happened in between. Gaps come from news released outside market hours. Akamai's numbers: it closed Thursday at $110.41. On the Anthropic deal it traded up about 21% before the opening bell. In regular hours it reached $128.46, up 16.4%. It closed up about 8%, near $119.

So the stock gave back roughly half its gain during the session. That giveback is called **fading the gap**, and it is not a failure — it is information. Here is the mechanism. Overnight and in the first minutes, the people buying are reacting to a headline: $11.6 billion, seven years, Anthropic. Almost nobody has read the details yet. As the day goes on, the people who *have* read the details start trading too. And the details are more complicated than the headline: no revenue from this deal in 2026, only $150 million to $300 million in 2027, and about $5.5 billion of capital spending required to deliver it. Also, Anthropic got a warrant on about 5% of the company, which means existing shareholders own a slightly smaller slice of whatever comes. None of that is bad news. It is just slower and more expensive news than "$11.6 billion" sounds.

The basketball version: the gap is the crowd erupting when a player throws down a dunk in the first quarter. The close is the box score. Both are real, but only one of them tells you how the game went. A stock that gaps up 21% and closes up 8% had a genuinely good day — it just had a less good day than the first ninety seconds promised. What experienced traders watch for is the relationship between the two. A gap that holds most of its gain into the close suggests the buyers who studied the news agreed with the buyers who reacted to it. A gap that fades most of the way back suggests they did not.

One practical note, and it is the reason I flagged the number twice above. Different outlets reported Akamai's Friday move as 5%, 8%, 15%, 16% and 23%, because each was quoting a different moment — premarket, midday, intraday high, close. When you read that a stock "surged 20%," always ask *as of when*. The closing price is the one the market actually settled on, and it is the only one that goes in the record.
