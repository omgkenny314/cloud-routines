# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, August 25, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

A quietly good day for stocks, built on bad news about the economy. Three separate reports out this morning — consumer confidence, new home sales, and a regional factory survey — all came in soft. Weak economic data usually means the Federal Reserve has less reason to keep interest rates high, so bond yields fell, and falling yields make stocks more attractive. All three major indexes finished higher, with technology leading. The single biggest storyline, though, was a company-level disaster: DICK'S Sporting Goods (ticker DKS) lost roughly a quarter of its value in one session after its Foot Locker acquisition went badly wrong and management slashed its profit forecast for the year. Underneath all of it, the market is essentially holding its breath for two events later this week — Nvidia's earnings tomorrow after the close, and new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole speech on Friday.

## Big movers and why

- **DICK'S Sporting Goods (DKS) — down about 28%,** a drop of roughly $49.90 from Monday's $179.33 close. This was its worst day in three years. Different outlets reported the decline anywhere from 25% to 29% depending on when they snapped the price; the roughly 28% figure comes from the point drop against the prior close. The cause: Foot Locker, which DICK'S bought for $2.5 billion less than a year ago, posted a $31.9 million quarterly segment loss. Three months ago management projected Foot Locker would earn $110–150 million this year; now they expect a loss of $40–80 million. That is a swing of about $190 million at the midpoint. Full-year adjusted earnings guidance was cut to $11–12 per share from $13.50–14.50. Notably, the core DICK'S business was fine — comparable sales rose 4.9%, helped by World Cup demand — while Foot Locker's comparable sales fell 3.6%.

## Headlines that matter

**Three weak U.S. economic reports, all at once.** The Conference Board's consumer confidence index for August fell to 89.4 from 90.2, its weakest reading since January, with the forward-looking "expectations" component down 7.8% on worsening views of jobs and inflation. July new home sales dropped 10.5% to an annualized 607,000, also the weakest since January, though June was revised up meaningfully. The Richmond Fed's manufacturing index slipped to 4 from 5, versus expectations of 7. *Why it matters:* consumer spending is roughly two-thirds of the U.S. economy, and confidence surveys are an early read on whether people are about to pull back. Weak data is genuinely bad news for the economy, but it lowers the odds of Fed rate hikes — which is why stocks rose on it today. That inverted relationship is one of the more confusing things about markets, and it shows up often.

**Canada retaliated on trade.** Canada announced counter-tariffs on about $20 billion of American goods — more than 800 product categories, at rates of 15%, 25%, and 50%, effective September 8 — after the U.S. imposed 50% tariffs on Canadian goods over the weekend following the collapse of negotiations. The list reaches past industrial goods into everyday items like seafood, cheese, clothing, and cosmetics. *Why it matters:* tariffs are a tax on imported goods, and they push prices up. Higher prices complicate the Fed's inflation fight, and they squeeze the profit margins of any company that sells across that border. Markets largely shrugged today, which itself is worth noticing — investors have seen many rounds of this.

**Jackson Hole is Thursday through Saturday, and Warsh speaks Friday.** The Kansas City Fed's annual symposium runs August 27–29, with Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivering his first keynote in that role Friday morning at 8:00 a.m. Eastern. This year's official theme is financial innovation and payments, but Warsh has said he wants to "frame the big questions" rather than debate quarter-point moves. *Why it matters:* markets are currently pricing roughly a one-in-three chance of a rate *hike* in September — not a cut. A new Fed chair's first major speech is how he establishes what he cares about, and traders will parse every sentence of it for direction.

**Treasury is supporting the bond market.** Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has doubled buyback operations for longer-dated government bonds. *Why it matters:* when the government buys back its own bonds, it adds demand, which supports prices and pushes yields down. This is part of why yields have eased for two straight sessions, and a softer dollar alongside it is part of what lifted bitcoin.

## After the bell

Two significant reports landed right at the close, and both companies beat expectations while their stocks fell. That combination is the subject of today's lesson.

**Intuit (INTU)**, the maker of TurboTax and QuickBooks, reported fiscal fourth-quarter adjusted earnings of $4.03 per share against an expected $3.58 — a beat of about 13% — on revenue of $4.354 billion, up 13.65% from a year ago and ahead of the $4.268 billion consensus. QuickBooks Online Accounting revenue rose 20%, TurboTax Live rose 37%, and Credit Karma rose 20% for the year. The catch is the outlook: for fiscal 2027 management guided revenue growth of just 9–10%, a slowdown, even while guiding earnings-per-share growth of 23–24%. Shares fell in after-hours trading. I saw a report describing an initial drop of nearly 15% that recovered to around 5%, but after-hours prices move fast and I could not independently confirm where the stock stands right now — treat the direction as reliable and the magnitude as unconfirmed.

**Zoom Communications (ZM)** reported second-quarter revenue of about $1.28 billion, ahead of the $1.27 billion expected, with enterprise revenue up 7.8% year over year — its fastest in three years. Management raised full-year guidance to revenue of $5.08–5.09 billion and adjusted earnings of $6.08–6.12 per share, and still has $1.3 billion of buyback authorization unused. Its AI customer-service products are growing quickly, with Zoom Virtual Agent customer count up 256%. Shares were down roughly 4% after hours on one reading, again unconfirmed at this hour.

## Today's lesson: guidance, and why a company can beat and still get crushed

Our recent lessons covered operating margin, dilution, support and resistance, and index construction. Today's is a fundamental one, and the market handed us two clean examples on the same afternoon.

**Guidance** is a company's own forecast of its future results, delivered by management alongside its quarterly report. **Consensus estimate** is the average of what professional analysts expected. Most people assume a stock rises when a company beats the consensus estimate and falls when it misses. Today proved that's the wrong mental model, twice.

Start with DICK'S Sporting Goods. Its core business was healthy — comparable sales up 4.9%, meaning stores open at least a year sold nearly 5% more than a year earlier. That's a good number. The quarter's earnings came in a little light, $3.53 per share against $3.78 expected. A modest miss like that might cost a stock a few percent. The stock fell about 28%. The damage was almost entirely in the guidance: management cut full-year earnings expectations to $11–12 per share from $13.50–14.50, and flipped the Foot Locker unit's forecast from a $110–150 million profit to a $40–80 million loss. The quarter that just ended was a small disappointment. The *next four quarters* got repriced.

Now look at Intuit, where the same mechanism ran in the other direction. Intuit beat earnings estimates by about 13% — a genuinely strong quarter — and the stock still fell after hours. Why? Because management guided fiscal 2027 revenue growth to 9–10%, down from the nearly 14% it just delivered. Investors weren't punishing the results; they were repricing the growth rate going forward.

Here's the basketball version. A player drops 40 points tonight, and it's a great box score. But if the team announces after the game that he'll be on a minutes restriction for the rest of the season, nobody is talking about the 40 anymore. The box score is history. The scouting report for the next 60 games is what changes how you value him. A stock price is not a scorecard for last quarter — it's an estimate of all the profits a company will earn from here forward. That's why guidance so often matters more than the number that made the headline, and why the smart move on any earnings day is to read what management said about *next* year before you react to what they reported about *last* quarter.
