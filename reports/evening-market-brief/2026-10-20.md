# Market Close Brief — Tuesday, October 20, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a split market, and the split tells the whole story. Earnings season arrived in force, and the companies that reported were old-economy industrial and defense names — and almost all of them beat and raised their forecasts. That lifted the Dow Jones Industrial Average more than 500 points, a 1% gain, while the technology-heavy Nasdaq Composite finished essentially flat, a hair below where it started. In other words, the money that moved today moved into machines, jet engines, missiles and credit data, not into artificial intelligence. That is close to an exact mirror image of yesterday, when tech did all the lifting and the Dow finished slightly down. Running underneath it was the second storyline: oil. Attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz pushed Brent crude above $110 a barrel early in the day before prices fell back on hopes of diplomatic progress between the United States and Iran — and that retreat in oil is also what let bond yields tick down and gave stocks room to breathe. The single biggest storyline, though, is the earnings one: this was the first real test of whether corporate profits can hold up with the Federal Reserve raising rates and the 10-year Treasury yield above 5%, and today the industrial half of the economy passed it.

## Headlines that matter

**Housing data confirmed what the builders told us yesterday, and it was ugly.** The Census Bureau's September new residential construction report landed this morning. Housing starts — homes on which construction actually began — fell 5.2% to an annual rate of 1.221 million, a four-month low that came in below *every* estimate in Bloomberg's survey of economists. Single-family starts dropped 4.6% to 859,000, the lowest in a year. Total building permits were roughly flat at about 1.400 million, but single-family permits fell 4.0% to 845,000, the weakest reading since March 2023. Multifamily permits rose 5.9% to 503,000.

Why this matters: yesterday's builder-confidence survey hit a multi-year low, and that measured how builders *feel*. Today's report measures what they are actually doing with money, and it points the same direction. Permits are the most forward-looking piece, because a builder pulls a permit before breaking ground — so weak single-family permits are a statement about next spring, not this month. The cause is not mysterious. The average 30-year mortgage rate is reported near 7.40%, and housing is the single most interest-rate-sensitive part of the economy. One fair caveat: the Census Bureau itself notes the monthly starts decline falls within its margin of error, so one month alone is not statistically conclusive. The direction across two separate reports in two days is what carries weight.

**The Federal Reserve is the reason those mortgage rates are where they are, and it meets a week from tomorrow.** The Fed raised its target rate by a quarter point in September to a range of 3.75% to 4.00% — its first increase since 2023 — on a unanimous 12-to-0 vote, with Chair Kevin Warsh saying inflation had been "too high for too long." The projections released at that meeting showed 16 of 18 officials seeing the possibility of at least one more increase this year. Markets currently do not expect a hike at the October 28 meeting, but they have also stopped expecting cuts anytime soon. The Fed is now in its pre-meeting blackout period, so you will hear nothing from officials until the decision.

Why this matters to everything above: a central bank that is *raising* rates to fight inflation, rather than cutting to support growth, is why the 10-year yield sits above 5%, why mortgages are at 7.40%, why housing is contracting, and why a company like General Motors gets punished for a profit forecast that merely holds steady. When borrowing costs are high, future profits are worth less today, and the market is less willing to wait for them.

**The tanker war in the Strait of Hormuz is now a live oil-supply problem.** Reuters, citing data from the ship-tracking firm Kpler, counted at least 18 tanker attacks in the past two weeks, and reported that daily oil flows out of the Persian Gulf have shrunk dramatically. Brent topped $110 a barrel early today on the latest attacks, then fell back as traders priced in hopes of U.S.–Iran diplomatic progress, with Qatar preparing to announce a regional defense agreement. Iran separately claimed two more tankers were mined and a U.S. drone downed, though no vessels have been identified and those claims are unverified. Analysts at ING described the market as pricing in a significant risk premium.

Why this matters: the Strait of Hormuz is the chokepoint through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes, and oil is an input cost for nearly everything. It is also the one inflation channel the Federal Reserve cannot do anything about — raising interest rates does not reopen a shipping lane. That is why today's oil *decline* was read as good news for bonds and stocks, and why a defense-sector earnings rally and a Middle East supply scare showing up on the same day is not a coincidence.

## Today's lesson: the price-to-earnings ratio, or what "expensive" actually measures

Today handed us two companies that make a perfect pair, so let me teach you the single most-used number in fundamental analysis. *Fundamental analysis* means studying the business itself — what it earns, what it owns, what it owes — as opposed to studying its price chart.

The number is the **price-to-earnings ratio**, usually written P/E and often just called "the multiple." The arithmetic could not be simpler: take the share price and divide it by the company's earnings per share. *Earnings per share*, or EPS, is the company's annual profit divided by the number of shares, so it is each share's slice of the profit. If a stock trades at $100 and earns $5 per share, its P/E is 20. The useful way to say that out loud is: *investors are paying twenty dollars today for every one dollar of annual profit.*

Now today's pair. Eli Lilly closed near a record around $1,296, and the reporting on Goldman Sachs' note said that both Goldman's $1,413 target and Bank of America's $1,397 target work out to about 36 times **forward** earnings, versus roughly 33 times today. *Forward* means the estimate of the next twelve months' earnings rather than the last twelve months' actual results, which is the *trailing* version. You can back out the earnings number from that: $1,296 divided by 33 gives you about $39 of expected earnings per share. Check it the other way — $1,413 divided by 36 is also about $39. That tells you something important and easy to miss. Goldman did not raise its target because it expects Lilly to earn *more* money. It raised it because it thinks investors will pay a *higher price for the same money*. Analysts call that a **re-rating**, and it is a very different claim than "profits are going up."

Set that against General Motors on the very same day. GM's new full-year forecast puts its earnings near $13.25 per share, and the stock traded around $78. Divide and you get a P/E of roughly 6. So two large, profitable, well-known American companies closed today at about 33 times earnings and about 6 times earnings. Lilly is more than five times as expensive per dollar of profit. That is not a mistake and it is not a bargain sitting in plain sight — it is the market making a statement about the *future* of those two dollars of profit. It believes Lilly's dollar will grow and keep growing, and it doubts GM's dollar will, which is exactly what GM's own guidance said today when it capped its forecast and investors sold a stock that had just beaten expectations. A low multiple is usually not the market being generous. It is the market being skeptical.

Here is the basketball version, and it fits cleanly. P/E is like what a team pays per point. You can sign a 22-year-old who averages 20 points a game, and you will pay an enormous price per point — because you are not really buying this year's 20 points, you are buying the 28 points you think he'll average at 26, and the decade after that. Or you can sign a steady 33-year-old who also averages 20, and you will pay a fraction as much per point, because everyone in the building knows those points may be 15 next season and 10 after that. Neither contract is automatically the smart one. The question a P/E ratio asks you is simply: *am I paying a young man's price, and if so, is this a young man's career?* The ratio does not answer that. It only tells you what price you are being asked to pay, and that is why you always check it before you form an opinion.

To be explicit: this is a way of reading valuation, not a recommendation. A high multiple does not mean sell and a low one does not mean buy — plenty of 33-times stocks have been bargains and plenty of 6-times stocks have been traps. The point is that "expensive" and "cheap" are meaningless until you know what you are paying per dollar of profit, and now you can work that out yourself in one division.
