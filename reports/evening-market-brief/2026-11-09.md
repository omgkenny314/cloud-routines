# Market Close Brief — Monday, November 9, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a big, broad rebound — and it happened for a reason worth understanding, because the obvious explanation is wrong. Last week was ugly: the Nasdaq fell into a correction, the S&P 500 had its worst week since April 2025, and Thursday alone knocked more than 1,800 points off the Dow. Today a large part of that came back. The Dow rose 1,104 points, the S&P 500 gained 2.35%, the Nasdaq Composite climbed 2.74%, and small-company stocks did best of all. Ten of the eleven sectors in the S&P 500 finished higher, with energy the only one lower at the close. That is what a genuinely broad rally looks like — not one group carrying the index, but almost everything rising together.

Now the part that makes today strange, and the single biggest storyline. The most important stock in the market went *down*. Nvidia (ticker NVDA), the chipmaker at the center of the whole artificial-intelligence trade, fell about 4.4% while everything around it rose. The reason is not business news at all: Nvidia's board of directors opened an independent investigation into its own chief executive, Jensen Huang, after reporting that he had been the target of a Chinese intelligence effort, and Huang admitted to an affair in an email to staff. So the market spent today buying the AI story while selling the company most associated with it. That split is the most instructive thing that happened today, and it is what tonight's lesson is built on.

Two other forces mattered. Oil fell about another 5% as Qatar's prime minister carried an Iranian proposal to the White House to reopen the Strait of Hormuz — cheaper energy is helpful for inflation and therefore for stocks. And underneath it all was something mechanical rather than emotional: JPMorgan told clients that the forced selling by borrowed-money AI funds that drove last week's collapse has probably passed its peak. Last week was partly people being made to sell. This week, so far, they are not being made to sell.

## Big movers and why

That is the signature of a rebound rally rather than a re-rating, and it is an important distinction: one is about prices recovering, the other is about businesses being worth more.

## Headlines that matter

**Nvidia's board is investigating its own chief executive.** A committee of independent directors, assisted by outside counsel, will oversee an investigation into reporting that Jensen Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The New York Times reported Thursday that U.S. intelligence agencies determined last year that Alice Huang — an executive connected to a company called Megaspeed that was buying Nvidia's advanced chips — had illegally smuggled advanced AI chips into China, and concluded she was a Chinese spy. Huang wrote to staff that he had "fallen short" of his responsibilities, saying "I was unfaithful to my wife. I am deeply sorry." The board stated it has "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets" were compromised, and expressed confidence in Huang and his leadership team. China's Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun called the "so-called Chinese spy narrative" unsubstantiated.

Why this matters more than a typical executive scandal: Nvidia is the largest company in the index, so its stock alone moves the S&P 500. The allegations sit precisely on top of the most politically sensitive issue in the industry — whether advanced American chips are reaching China — at a moment when Congress has just changed hands. And analysts are now openly raising the possibility that Huang could be removed depending on what the investigation finds. He is not an ordinary chief executive; he founded the company and has run it for over thirty years. Hold that thought for the lesson.

**Oil fell again on real diplomacy toward reopening the Strait of Hormuz.** Qatar's prime minister met President Trump at the White House carrying an Iranian proposal for an interim deal: Iran would reopen the Strait and halt attacks on Gulf tankers, and the United States would lift its naval and economic blockade and release roughly $8 billion of Iranian funds held in Qatar. Why it matters: the Strait of Hormuz is the narrow waterway through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes, and cheaper oil feeds directly into lower inflation, which is the single thing most likely to stop the Federal Reserve from raising interest rates further.

But be careful how much you bank on it, because the caveats are substantial. Hours after the meeting, Secretary of State Rubio called Iran's demands "outrageous and ridiculous and really not realistic." The White House released no public readout. The claim that Iran has agreed to the terms comes from two unnamed diplomatic sources speaking to Israeli media — **I could not verify it.** Trump said renewed military action is "highly unlikely" if there is progress, while adding that the U.S. is prepared to strike if necessary. The oil market is pricing a peace that has not been signed.

**A December interest-rate increase is now close to fully expected.** The CME FedWatch tool put the odds of a December hike at about 84%, up from 76% on Friday; other estimates ranged from 77% to 81%. The trigger was Friday's jobs report — 165,000 jobs added against roughly 60,000 expected, with unemployment falling to 4.1%. Why it matters: this is the ceiling on rallies like today's. The Federal Reserve under Chair Kevin Warsh held rates steady in late October, but two officials, Cook and Hammack, dissented in favor of raising them, and several others — Musalem, Logan, Barkin, and Daly — have since argued publicly that rates need to go higher. Logan has said she wants at least two more quarter-point increases. The next decision comes December 9. Higher interest rates lower what investors will pay for a dollar of future profit, which weighs hardest on exactly the fast-growing technology companies that led today.

**The quieter but most mechanically important story: forced selling appears to have peaked.** JPMorgan told clients that the heavy deleveraging phase behind last week's collapse has probably passed its worst, and that funding conditions should gradually improve. Bloomberg had reported that wealthy investors from Taiwan to Hong Kong were facing margin calls on borrowed AI bets. Why this matters, and it is one of the most useful ideas in investing: when investors buy with borrowed money and prices fall, their lenders demand more collateral — a margin call — and they must sell *whatever they own*, immediately, regardless of what they think it is worth. That is why good companies fall alongside bad ones in a rout, and it is why the bounce, when it comes, is often sharpest in whatever was sold hardest. Last week and today are two halves of the same mechanism.

The reason is simple and a little absurd: the agency skipped collecting prices in October 2025 during the government shutdown, so there is no year-ago figure to compare against. So the monthly number carries all the weight. Economists expect headline prices to have risen 0.2% to 0.3% from September, with core — which excludes food and energy because they swing wildly — also at 0.2% to 0.3%. The Cleveland Fed's model points to 0.24% headline and 0.21% core. For context, September came in at a hot 0.6% monthly. Analysts warn that a reading above 0.3% could cause traders to fully price in a December rate increase. One practical warning: some outlets are circulating annual inflation projections for October, such as 3.9% or 4.0%. **Those are unofficial and will not appear in the release.** If you see one presented as the headline inflation rate tomorrow, it is wrong. ## Today's lesson

Tonight's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning we study the business itself rather than the behavior of its stock chart. The concept is the **moat**, and today handed us an unusually sharp version of it: the question of whether a company's moat lives inside the company, or inside one person.

A moat is the durable advantage that stops competitors from taking a company's profits away. The word is a metaphor — a castle with a wide moat is hard to attack. Nvidia has a famously wide one, and it is not mainly about the chips. It is a software system called CUDA, which nearly every AI researcher in the world has already learned and built on. Switching to a competitor's chips would mean rewriting work and retraining people. That is called a *switching cost*, and it is one of the strongest moats there is, because it does not depend on Nvidia staying cleverer than everyone else — it depends on millions of hours of other people's work already being invested in Nvidia's way of doing things.

Here is where today gets interesting. Nothing happened to CUDA today. No customer cancelled. No competitor shipped a better chip. What came into question was a *person* — the founder who has run the company for over thirty years, and whom analysts now openly discuss as possibly being removed. And the market's answer was remarkably precise. It sold Nvidia about 4.4% and simultaneously bought almost every other AI-related company in sight: Coherent up 18%, Cloudflare up 11%, Nebius up 9%, CoreWeave up 6%. Read that as a sentence and it says: *we still believe in the AI buildout; we have a new question about this company's leadership.* That is the cleanest diagnostic you will ever get. **When a stock falls while its entire sector rises, the market is pricing something specific to that company — not to its business conditions.** When a stock falls *with* its sector, as Nvidia did last Thursday, the market is repricing the whole industry. Same stock, same direction, two completely different meanings.

Basketball makes the distinction concrete. Think about the difference between a team built around one superstar and a team built around a system. If your whole advantage is one extraordinary player, you are genuinely great — right up until he sprains an ankle, and then you are ordinary, because the advantage walked off the court. The Spurs under Popovich were the other kind: the advantage was in the system, the development pipeline, the culture. Players came and went and the team stayed good. So the question to ask about any company you are studying is exactly the question you would ask about a team: *if the star left tomorrow, what would still be here?* For Nvidia, the honest answer is "quite a lot" — CUDA does not resign — but also "less than people assumed," because Huang personally drove the strategy, the customer relationships, and much of the company's credibility. Professionals call this **key-person risk**, and the tell-tale signs are worth learning to look for: a founder still running the company decades in, no visible succession plan, and a leader whose public identity is fused with the product.

One last point, which is really a warning about today rather than about Nvidia. Most of today's biggest gainers rose on no news whatsoever. Coherent's excellent quarter was reported five days ago; it is still down about 43% over the past month, and an analyst said plainly that these stocks are now moving on sentiment about AI funding rather than on their results. A moat is a fact about a business and it changes slowly, over years. A stock price is an opinion about that business and it can change 18% in a day. Today was mostly opinions changing. Keeping those two things separate in your mind is most of the work of fundamental analysis.
