# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, November 4, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

Today was one of the biggest single days this market has had in more than a year, and the entire thing was about one piece of news: Democrats swept the midterm elections, winning control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose about 3.19%, roughly 1,652 points — its largest one-day advance in both points and percentage terms since April 9, 2025, and by CNN's reckoning the best post-midterm-election day in 44 years, since 1982. But the shape of the day matters as much as the size. Overnight, as the results came in, markets did the opposite: stock futures and the dollar sold off sharply in a classic risk-off panic. By the opening bell that panic had flipped into a relief rally, and the market opened near its full gain and held it all day. The single biggest storyline underneath the headline number is that this was not a rally where everything went up together — it was a violent reshuffling of who wins and who loses under a different Congress. Health insurers and hospital operators soared, solar and clean-energy names soared, and oil collapsed roughly 8% to 9% as traders decided a Democratic Congress means a real legislative check on the Iran war. Meanwhile the two names most tied to Elon Musk — Tesla and SpaceX — fell, and the companies building AI data centers were among the day's worst performers. So: a huge up day on the index, with a genuine bloodbath going on inside it.

## Big movers and why

An honest caveat on this section: most of these percentages come from afternoon and close-of-trade reports rather than confirmed final prints, so treat them as the right size and direction rather than exact. Where a source looked stale I left the name out entirely rather than guess.

**Winners**

- **Microsoft (ticker MSFT), the software and cloud-computing giant, rose about 6.37% to close at a record high of roughly $554.16.** This is the clearest example of the day's logic. Microsoft generates enormous cash from its own operations, so it can fund its AI build-out without borrowing or waiting on anyone's permission. In a market suddenly worried about regulatory and permitting hurdles for AI infrastructure, the companies that already have the money won.

- **Apple (ticker AAPL), the iPhone maker, rose roughly 6.75% to about $354.11, hit an all-time high, and carried its market value above $5 trillion.** Note the whiplash: Apple fell more than 2% yesterday and was the single biggest drag on the Dow. Today it was one of the biggest lifts. Nothing about Apple's business changed in 24 hours.

- **Amazon (ticker AMZN), the e-commerce and cloud company, rose roughly 6.3% to 7%.** Its gain had a business reason layered on top of the election: Amazon Web Services revenue grew 39% to $46 billion, its fastest cloud growth in nearly five years.

- **Hospital operators and health insurers were the day's best group.** HCA Healthcare (ticker HCA) and Universal Health Services (ticker UHS) each rose about 10%. Centene (ticker CNC) rose about 9% and Molina Healthcare (ticker MOH) about 8%. Elevance Health (ticker ELV) rose about 4% and Cigna (ticker CI) about 3%. The reason is specific and worth understanding: investors now think enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies are more likely to be extended. Those subsidies help people afford insurance premiums, and when more people carry insurance, hospitals get paid for care they would otherwise deliver for free and insurers collect more premiums. A policy change that sounds like it is about patients is, in the market's eyes, about who gets paid.

- **Solar and clean energy jumped on the same policy logic.** Canadian Solar (ticker CSIQ) rose more than 11%, First Solar (ticker FSLR) about 8%, Plug Power (ticker PLUG) about 7.5%, and Sunrun (ticker RUN) about 5%. These companies' economics depend heavily on federal tax credits and subsidies, so the composition of Congress is close to a first-order input to their earnings.

- **Kyndryl (ticker KD), the information-technology services company spun out of IBM, along with LivaNova (ticker LIVN), a medical-device maker, and Clear Secure (ticker YOU), which runs the CLEAR airport identity-verification lanes, were among the S&P's strongest individual gainers** per the close-of-trade report. I could not verify the specific reason behind each of these three, so I will not invent one.

**Losers**

- **CVS Health (ticker CVS), the pharmacy, insurance and pharmacy-benefits company, fell about 12% to roughly $80.13** — and this one is a genuinely interesting puzzle. CVS beat third-quarter estimates *and* raised its full-year outlook, which is normally the combination the market pays for. It fell hard anyway, because of what else it announced: CVS is splitting itself into two separate public companies by the first half of 2028. One company keeps the CVS Health name and holds the insurance and care businesses — Aetna, CVS Caremark, CVS Specialty, Oak Street Health and Signify Health. The other holds the roughly 9,000 CVS Pharmacy retail stores. Shareholders would receive stock in both, and the company expects the split to be tax-free. The bear case that drove the selling: running pharmacy, pharmacy-benefits management and insurance under one roof was supposed to produce cost advantages, and separating them gives those up. Note what happened to the competitors — Elevance and Cigna both rose. The market read the breakup as a competitor retreating.

- **Tesla (ticker TSLA) fell about 2.5%, and SpaceX (ticker SPCX) fell about 4%.** The trigger was not a federal result but a state one: Democrat Gina Hinojosa won the Texas governor's race, and analysts flagged this as a real risk to AI data-center construction in Texas. One analyst warned the Democratic victory creates "major hurdles" for the AI build-out generally.

- **Nvidia (ticker NVDA), the dominant designer of AI chips, fell about 1.8%, and Intel (ticker INTC) fell about 4.9%.** Nvidia and Tesla were the only two of the big technology giants to finish lower. This is the most important detail in the whole section: money rotated *out* of the companies that sell AI equipment and *into* the companies that buy it. If permitting, state politics and regulation slow down data-center construction, the buyers can simply spend more slowly, but the sellers lose the orders.

- **Energy was the worst-performing sector of the day**, which follows directly from an 8% to 9% drop in crude. When the product you sell falls 8% in a session, your future earnings fall with it.

- **Everus Construction (ticker ECG), which does electrical and mechanical construction work including data centers, and Modine Manufacturing (ticker MOD), which makes thermal-management and cooling equipment, each fell more than 25%** per the close-of-trade report. I want to flag the pattern rather than overclaim the cause: both of these sell physical infrastructure into data-center construction, which is precisely the activity the market spent today marking down. I could not confirm whether company-specific earnings news also contributed, so treat the cause as probable rather than established.

## Headlines that matter

- **Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate.** Reports characterized it as a sweep and a surprise relative to what markets had priced. **Why it matters:** markets do not care who wins in a partisan sense — they care about what a government can and cannot do, and to whom money flows. Three concrete channels opened today. First, fiscal: a Congress controlled by the party not holding the White House is unlikely to pass new deficit-financed tax cuts, which means less Treasury borrowing, which is why bond yields fell. Second, sectoral: specific industries whose economics run on federal subsidy decisions — health insurance, hospitals, solar — were repriced immediately. Third, war powers: Congress controls military funding, and traders took roughly 8% to 9% out of the price of oil on the view that the Iran war now faces a legislative brake. Note also what *did not* happen: this was not a referendum the market treated as good or bad in the abstract. It redistributed value rather than simply creating it.

- **The Federal Reserve is still most likely to raise rates in December, and the election barely changed that.** Estimates of the odds ranged from about 77% to 85% depending on the venue and the hour, and multiple sources noted the election results moved them very little — one put it at roughly 78% today versus about 75% on Tuesday. The Fed held its benchmark rate at 3.75% to 4.00% at its October 28 meeting on a 10-2 vote, with both dissenters wanting a *quarter-point increase*. The next meeting is December 8-9. **Why it matters:** this is the fact that should keep today's euphoria in perspective. Higher interest rates make safe bonds pay more, which makes risky stocks relatively less attractive, and they raise borrowing costs for every company funding a build-out with debt. An election does not change the inflation data, and the inflation data is what the Fed is reacting to. Today's rally happened *despite* a central bank that is leaning toward tightening, not because of one that is easing.

- **Friday's jobs report is now the most important scheduled event on the calendar.** Multiple sources framed it as the data point that decides whether the December hike actually happens. **Why it matters:** the Fed is being asked to raise rates into an economy that has not obviously weakened — yesterday's manufacturing reading came in hot at 55.5. A strong payrolls number on Friday removes the Fed's last excuse not to hike; a weak one gives it cover to wait. Either way, Friday is likely to matter more to your portfolio over the next month than today's 1,652-point Dow move did.

- **The U.S.-Iran situation is unresolved, and that is why oil moved so much.** Prices have now fallen for several straight sessions on hopes of a diplomatic settlement, but a senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran and Washington remain significantly apart on ceasefire terms. **Why it matters:** two things are being priced at once — the chance of a deal, and now the chance that Congress constrains the war regardless of whether there is a deal. Oil is the fastest-moving input to inflation, and inflation is what decides the December Fed meeting. So every headline out of those talks is, indirectly, a headline about interest rates.

- **McDonald's (ticker MCD) reported before the open and gave a useful read on the consumer.** Adjusted earnings of $3.57 per share beat the $3.38 expected, but revenue of $7.16 billion came in *below* the roughly $7.29 billion expected. U.S. same-store sales fell 0.2%, though that was better than the 0.5% decline analysts feared. The stock rose. **Why it matters:** this is the shape of a lot of reports this season — profits beating because of cost control, revenue missing because customers are buying less. Same-store sales, meaning sales at restaurants open at least a year, strip out the effect of simply opening more locations, which makes them the honest measure of whether people are actually coming in. A small decline that is less bad than feared is not strength; it is a consumer who is still pulling back, just less sharply.

- **Uber (ticker UBER) also reported before the open**, with revenue of $14.8 billion, up 10% from a year earlier. I could not verify the earnings-per-share figure or the stock's reaction, so I am leaving both out rather than guessing.

## Today's lesson: the gap, or why the most important trading of the day happened while you were asleep

Today's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning we read the behavior of the price itself rather than the company's financial statements. And today handed us the textbook example of a pattern called a **gap**.

Here is the concept. The stock market is open for six and a half hours a day. News does not keep those hours. When something big happens overnight — an election result, a war headline, an earnings release — buyers and sellers cannot trade on it, because the doors are locked. So all of that disagreement about what things are worth piles up, and when the opening bell rings it is resolved instantly, in a single jump. The price does not *travel* from yesterday's close to today's open. It teleports. That jump is a gap: a price level where no shares actually changed hands.

Think of it like leaving a basketball game at the end of the third quarter to take a phone call in the parking lot. You were watching a two-point game. You come back and it is a seventeen-point game. Nothing you can do about the seventeen points — that scoring happened while you were outside, and you could not place a bet during it. What you *can* still read is what happens from here: whether the team that pulled ahead keeps extending, or whether the lead starts bleeding away. The gap is the score you missed. What you do with it is watching what happens after.

Now look at what actually happened today, because it is unusually clean. Overnight, as the election results came in, futures and the dollar sold off *hard* — one analyst described a sharp selloff in equities and the dollar. By the open, that had flipped entirely. At 9:54 a.m. Eastern, roughly twenty minutes into the session, the Dow was already up about 2.9%, at around 53,308. It closed the day around 53,439, up about 3.19%. Read those two numbers together: the market made roughly nine-tenths of its entire daily gain before the first half hour was over, and then spent the next six hours holding onto it and grinding slightly higher.

That is the distinction worth learning, and it is the whole reason this concept earns a lesson. A gap can be **held** or it can be **filled**. A held gap — what we saw today — means the overnight repricing was *accepted*: once real buyers and sellers could actually trade, they looked at the new price and agreed with it, and nobody showed up in size to sell it back down. A filled gap means the opposite. The price drifts back toward where it closed the day before, which tells you the overnight move was a panic or a thin-volume overreaction that daylight disagreed with. Compare today to the lesson from Monday, November 2, when the Nasdaq opened up 0.6%, ran to up 165 points, and then bled all day to close *down* 1.07%. That was a gap the market rejected. Today was a gap the market ratified — and the three-to-one advance-decline count on the New York Stock Exchange is the corroborating evidence, because it says the holding was done by a broad mass of stocks rather than a few giants.

One caution, and it is the practical takeaway. A gap this large is a measure of *surprise*, not of truth. The market gapped up 1,652 Dow points today because the election outcome differed from what was priced in, not because American companies became 3% more valuable overnight. Prices move on the difference between what happens and what was expected — and a gap is simply that difference, made visible all at once, at a price where nobody traded.
