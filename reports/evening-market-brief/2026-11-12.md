## The day in one paragraph

Today was the day the most important company in the market lost its founder, and the market decided it could live with it. Nvidia (NVDA) announced before the open that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately, with Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress stepping in as interim CEO. The stock opened in free fall — down around 14% at its worst — and then spent the rest of the day climbing back, finishing down roughly 5%. That single reversal is the story of the session. Because Nvidia is the heaviest weight in the S&P 500, its morning collapse dragged the whole index down, and its afternoon recovery pulled most of that back: the Dow Jones Industrial Average ended essentially flat, and the S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite closed slightly lower rather than sharply so. Underneath the Nvidia drama ran a second, quieter storyline that may matter more for your money over the next year: the 10-year Treasury yield pushed back above 5%, because traders now expect the Federal Reserve to *raise* interest rates in December, not cut them.

## Headlines that matter

**Nvidia's succession is now the market's single biggest open question.** This matters beyond one stock. Nvidia is the largest company in the S&P 500 by market value, which means a very large share of every index fund Americans own sits in this one name. Alongside the resignation, the board named Harvey C. Jones as the company's first-ever board chair and opened a search for a permanent CEO, considering both internal and external candidates. The Financial Times reportedly floated Nvidia's own Ian Buck and Arm chief executive Rene Haas as possibilities. Until that seat is filled, every Nvidia headline is also an index headline.

**The Federal Reserve is debating a rate hike, and that is the real macro story.** October's Consumer Price Index, released Tuesday, November 10, showed headline inflation up 0.4% for the month and core inflation up 0.3% — the third straight month at that 0.3% pace. "Core" strips out food and energy, whose prices swing for reasons that have nothing to do with the broader economy, so it is the number policymakers actually watch. Fed Governor Christopher Waller had said publicly that a 0.3% core reading would make him weigh a half-point hike in December. He got it. San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly added today that her "bias is that further tightening will be needed." According to the CME FedWatch tool, traders now price roughly a 91% chance of a quarter-point *increase* at the December meeting and about a 9% chance of a half-point increase. The policy rate stood at 3.75% to 4.00% in October. The December meeting is reported as December 9.

Why this matters to you: when the Fed raises rates, safe Treasury bonds pay more. That makes every risky asset compete against a higher risk-free return, and it hits hardest the companies whose value rests on profits expected many years from now — which is to say, exactly the AI and technology names that have led this market. Rising yields are a slow, grinding headwind, and they do not make for dramatic headlines the way a CEO resignation does.

**Oil and Iran.** President Trump's remark that he did not need a deal with Iran pushed crude higher. Oil feeds directly into inflation, which feeds into the Fed's thinking, which feeds into bond yields. Today you could watch that chain operate in a single session: oil up, yields up, long-duration tech under pressure.

## Today's lesson: volume, or how to tell a crowd from a few loud voices

Tonight's lesson is a technical one — *technical analysis* meaning we read the behavior of the price itself rather than the company's financial statements. The concept is **volume**, and today gave us one of the best examples I could ask for.

Volume simply means how many shares changed hands. If a stock trades 300 million shares today against a typical 150 million, volume was double normal. That is all it measures — not direction, not whether the move was justified, just *how many people participated*. On its own it tells you nothing. Paired with a price move, it tells you a great deal, because it answers a question price alone cannot: was this the whole market repricing something, or was it a thin trickle of orders pushing a quiet stock around?

Here is the basketball version. A team goes on an 8-0 run. You look at the scoreboard and see the lead change. But you learn something different depending on whether the arena is on its feet or half-empty and checking phones. The run is the price move. The crowd is the volume. An 8-0 run in a packed, roaring gym in the fourth quarter usually means something real has shifted — the other team is rattled, the momentum is genuine. The same 8-0 run in an empty gym during a preseason scrimmage is just a few possessions. Volume is how you tell which run you are watching.

Now today. Nvidia opened down roughly 14% on what was described as extremely active trading — enormous volume. That heavy volume is the market telling you this was not a few nervous sellers; this was essentially everyone reacting at once to genuinely new information, a founder-CEO gone overnight. Then something interesting happened. Buyers came in, and by the close the stock had recovered most of that loss, ending down about 5%. The buying also came on heavy volume. Both sides of that round trip were crowded.

What should you take from that? When a huge drop on huge volume gets bought back on huge volume in the same session, it generally means the market held a real argument and reached a genuine disagreement — a large group thought the news was worth 14% and a large group thought it was worth 5%, and the second group won the day. That is a far more informative outcome than the same recovery happening on thin volume, which would suggest the sellers simply ran out of orders and nobody showed up to push back. Conviction on both sides tends to mean the question is actually live, and a live question usually keeps producing volatile days until it is settled — which, for Nvidia, means until there is a permanent CEO.

And here is the limit, the same limit as every technical tool: volume told us the crowd was large and engaged. It said absolutely nothing about whether the buyers were right. Heavy volume measures conviction, not correctness. A packed arena can roar for a run that collapses two minutes later. That is why volume is best used as *confirmation* — a way of weighing how seriously to take a price move — and never as a reason, by itself, to do anything at all.
