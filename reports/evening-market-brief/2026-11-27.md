# Market Close Brief — Friday, November 27, 2026

*One scheduling note before anything else: today was a half session. The stock market closed at 12:00 noon Central (1:00 p.m. Eastern) because of the day after Thanksgiving, and the bond market closed an hour later at 1:00 p.m. Central. So the close is already three and a half hours behind us, and "after the bell" is unusually quiet. Thursday was Thanksgiving and markets were shut, which means today's comparison is against Wednesday, not yesterday.*

## The day in one paragraph

Today was a relief rally, and a big one for a three-and-a-half-hour day. Every major index rose, the technology-heavy ones rose most, and the money went straight back into exactly the stocks that had been beaten up worst over the past two weeks — memory chipmakers, AI infrastructure companies, and the newly public AI names. The Dow Jones Industrial Average added about 592 points, the S&P 500 rose 1.60%, and the Nasdaq Composite jumped 2.31%. That capped a remarkable week: all three indexes gained more than 5%, and the Dow logged its biggest weekly advance since October 2022.

Here is the single biggest storyline, and it is worth sitting with, because it is a little strange. Nothing got fixed this week. The Federal Reserve is still expected to *raise* interest rates at its December 8–9 meeting — the odds of that sit somewhere between 95% and 99% depending on which measure you look at. Core inflation is still at its hottest level in nearly three years. The new tariff proposals are still on the table. The credit-market worry about whether AI companies can service their enormous debts has not been answered. And yet this was Wall Street's best week in more than a year. What changed was not the facts but the price: by Wednesday morning the Nasdaq 100 had briefly fallen into bear-market territory, more than 20% below its record, and at some point a price gets low enough that buyers show up for no better reason than that it got low. That is not the same thing as the problem being solved. Both of these are true at once — this was a genuinely powerful week, and the S&P 500 still finished November down about 3.7%, roughly 6.7% below its October 6 record close of 7,818.93.

## Big movers and why

The honest headline here is that almost none of today's big moves were caused by news about the companies themselves. This was a sector-wide bounce in things that had fallen hard. Keep that in mind as you read the list.

- **SanDisk (ticker SNDK), which makes flash memory and storage chips, rose about 15.96%** — the biggest gainer among the large chip names, and more than double the next one. There was no good news from the company. It had fallen roughly 47% over the past month, and that is the whole explanation. Note the sources disagree on the magnitude here: readings ranged from 6% to 16% depending on when the snapshot was taken, and I am reporting the one tied explicitly to the close.

- **Micron Technology (ticker MU), the largest U.S. maker of memory chips, rose about 7.60%**, and the American shares of **SK Hynix**, its South Korean rival, rose about 7.52%. What makes this interesting is that it happened on a day when the research firm TrendForce published a *warning* about memory, which I cover below. The market bought them anyway.

- **Anthropic (ticker ANT), the AI company that went public eight trading days ago, rose about 10.98% to $91.88**, climbing back above its $83 offering price after finishing below it earlier in the week. That is up $8.88, or 10.7%, from the price institutions paid in the IPO. Cathie Wood's funds were reported to be buying.

- **Nvidia (ticker NVDA), which designs the chips nearly all AI systems run on, closed around $150.49, up about 0.80%.** That quote is timestamped 1:15 p.m. Eastern, just after the close, so treat it as very close to final rather than official. The notable thing is how *little* Nvidia moved on a day the rest of the chip complex surged — it had already rallied hard earlier in the week. Wednesday's close was $149.30.

- **SpaceX (ticker SPCX) rose about 3%, Nebius (ticker NBIS), an AI cloud provider, rose about 2%, and the American shares of Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (ticker TSM), which actually manufactures most advanced chips, rose nearly 3%.** Same pattern: AI infrastructure names that had been sold, catching a bid.

- **Retail stocks on Black Friday: I could not verify closing moves** for Walmart, Target, Best Buy, Amazon or Gap. This surprises people every year, so it is worth saying plainly — Black Friday itself is almost never a big day for retail *stocks*, because no retailer reports sales figures that day. The real numbers show up in December and January reports. Today's retail news was about shoppers, not share prices.

## Headlines that matter

**The Fed is still expected to raise rates in under two weeks, and that has not changed all week.** The CME FedWatch tool put the odds of a quarter-point increase at the December 8–9 meeting at roughly 95% to 99%, which would move the federal funds rate from its current 3.75%–4.00% range up to 4.00%–4.25%. One measure put the chance of at least a half-point of total increases by the January 27 meeting at 64%. Fed Governor Christopher Waller has argued for either a half-point move in December or a faster pace through 2027. *Why it matters:* this is the gravity underneath everything. Higher interest rates hurt expensive, fast-growing companies far more than cheap, steady ones, because a growth company's value rests on profits that arrive years from now, and higher rates make future money worth less today. Today's rally happened *despite* this, not because of it — which is exactly why you should not read a good day as a change in the weather.

**Black Friday spending looks strong, and the fine print is the inflation part.** Adobe forecast a record $12.9 billion of U.S. online spending today, up 9.2% from last year, after Thanksgiving Day itself set a record at about $7.1 billion, up 10.4%. The National Retail Federation projects roughly $1.01 trillion for the season. Sensormatic Solutions still counts Black Friday as the busiest day of the year for physical store traffic, and the Associated Press described lines outside Macy's Herald Square before sunrise. *Why it matters:* here is the part most coverage buries. The NRF said about *half* of the 4.1% sales growth it expects comes from higher prices, not more stuff being bought. One analysis put projected holiday spending growth at 5.4% before inflation and 2.8% after. "Record spending" and "weak real demand" can both be true, and separating the two is one of the most useful habits you can build.

**TrendForce lowered its forecast for memory-chip price increases.** The research firm said contract price increases for conventional DRAM — the working memory in computers and phones — should narrow through 2027, flatten in the second half of that year, and potentially decline by early 2028, with a bearish case pulling that decline into late 2027. It pointed to Chinese cloud providers ordering less and weaker consumer demand for older chips. BNP Paribas downgraded Micron to Neutral. Micron, for its part, says more than 75% of its 2027 shipments are already committed, much of it under multiyear agreements with minimum prices. *Why it matters:* this is the first real crack in the memory-shortage story that has powered these stocks all year, and the fact that Micron and SanDisk rose 7.6% and 16% on the day it landed tells you the market was in no mood to listen. It is also tonight's lesson.

**The jobs report is now the next scheduled event that can move the Fed.** November payrolls come Friday, December 4, at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. Economists polled by LSEG expect about 90,000 new jobs and a 4.1% unemployment rate, against 165,000 in October. *Why it matters:* with inflation hot, the labor market is the only argument for the Fed going slower. A strong number removes that argument. In the current regime, good economic news is bad news for stocks — a genuinely strange situation, and one worth understanding rather than memorizing.

**The AI credit question is unresolved, not answered.** Earlier this week the cost to insure the debt of Oracle, Alphabet, SpaceX and CoreWeave against default hit record highs. Nothing I found today reversed that. *Why it matters:* a falling stock price means investors think a company is worth less; a distressed bond price means lenders fear they may not get paid back. The second is more serious, because it changes behavior — a company whose borrowing costs spike has to slow its spending, and in the AI world one company's spending is another company's revenue. Today's rally in these names is a move in their stock prices. It is not news about their debt.

## Today's lesson: cyclical earnings, or why a cheap-looking P/E on a chipmaker is a different animal

Tonight's lesson is a fundamental one — *fundamental analysis* meaning we study the business itself rather than the behavior of its stock chart. The concept is **cyclicality**, and today handed us an unusually sharp example, because the market and a research firm looked at the same industry on the same day and reached opposite conclusions.

Start with the idea. A **cyclical** business is one whose profits swing enormously with its own supply-and-demand cycle, rather than grinding steadily upward. Memory chips are the classic case. Memory is close to a commodity — one company's DRAM chip is largely interchangeable with another's — so there is no brand loyalty to protect the price. When there is a shortage, prices spike, and because these companies have already paid for their factories, nearly every extra dollar of price falls through to profit. Margins go from thin to spectacular. Then everyone builds more factories, supply catches up, prices fall, and the same arithmetic runs backwards just as violently.

Now the trap, and this is the part worth remembering. You compute a **price-to-earnings ratio**, or P/E, by dividing the share price by the annual profit per share. **Forward P/E** uses the profit analysts expect over the next year rather than the profit already earned. Either way, it is a fraction — and a fraction is only as good as its bottom half. When a cyclical company is at the top of its cycle, that bottom half is as large as it will ever get, so the whole fraction looks small. The stock looks cheap. A Motley Fool piece published today argued exactly that about Nvidia, Micron and SanDisk, noting they trade at roughly half the S&P 500's forward multiple and that, in the author's view, a 4-times-forward-earnings valuation prices in a downturn too early. (That is one writer's opinion, not advice, and not mine — I am pointing at it because it is a clean illustration of the argument.) The counter-case is the one TrendForce made today: if memory prices stop rising in 2027 and start falling by 2028, then the earnings in the bottom of that fraction shrink, and a stock that looked like 4 times earnings turns out to have been 15 times the earnings that actually show up. Investors call this **peak earnings** — the stock was never cheap, the denominator was just temporarily enormous.

Basketball makes this concrete. Imagine a shooter who goes 11-for-14 from three over two games. His shooting percentage in that moment is genuinely elite, and if you valued him off those two games he would look like a bargain at any salary. But you would be paying for a number produced by a hot streak, not by the player's durable ability. The question is never "what is the number right now" — it is "what number does this player produce across a full season." Analysts call that normalized or mid-cycle earnings: not the best year, not the worst, but the average through a whole cycle. For a steady business, the current number and the mid-cycle number are close, so a P/E is reasonably trustworthy. For a memory chipmaker, they can differ by a factor of five.

So here is what to actually take from today. SanDisk rose about 16% and Micron about 7.6% on a day when the main piece of news about their industry was a *downgraded* price forecast. Neither company's earnings power changed today. What changed was the crowd's willingness to pay for it — the same lesson as multiple compression from last week, running in the other direction. And the tell for cyclical stocks is this: the arguments are never really about whether the company is good. They are about *where in the cycle you are standing*, which nobody can know for certain at the time. Micron's own answer to that uncertainty is instructive on its own — it says more than 75% of 2027 shipments are already locked in, much of it with minimum prices. That is a cyclical company trying to make its earnings less cyclical, which is a reasonable thing to watch for in any business whose profits swing this hard.
