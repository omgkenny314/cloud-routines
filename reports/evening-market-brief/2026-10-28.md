# Market Close Brief — Wednesday, October 28, 2026

## The day in one paragraph

The Federal Reserve did exactly what everyone expected, and the market went up a little — and then the real news arrived forty minutes after the closing bell. During the day, the Fed left its benchmark interest rate alone, but it did so by a 10-2 vote in which both dissenters wanted rates *raised*, and Chair Kevin Warsh spent his press conference making clear he is not finished fighting inflation. Stocks took that calmly: all three major indexes finished higher for the third straight day, with gains of roughly a quarter of a percent each. The bond market was less relaxed — the 10-year Treasury yield pushed back above 5% — and oil jumped nearly 3% on reports that the U.S. is preparing to resume strikes on Iran after the midterm elections. But the day's biggest storyline is the one that broke after the close, and it is the one I built today's lesson around: Microsoft and Alphabet, two of the largest companies in the world, both reported quarterly results tonight. Microsoft beat on both revenue and earnings and rose. Alphabet reported Google Cloud revenue that roughly *doubled* from a year ago — and the stock fell something like 7% to 9% anyway. Understanding why those two things can both be true is worth more to you than any number in this brief.

## The numbers

- **S&P 500: closed at 7,743.98, up 21.38 points, or about 0.28%.** A third consecutive winning day. The index was up as much as 0.46% during the session, gave all of it back, then recovered into the close — a sign of a market arguing with itself about the Fed's tone rather than its decision.

- **Dow Jones Industrial Average: closed at 52,475.10, up 97.53 points, or about 0.19%.** The smallest gain of the three, which is normal on a day when the action was in technology and healthcare rather than in the Dow's industrial and consumer names.

- **Nasdaq Composite: closed at 26,549.85, up 72.04 points, or about 0.27%.** Chip stocks did the lifting, led by SK Hynix, the South Korean memory-chip maker, whose U.S.-listed shares rose about 13% on a record quarterly profit.

- **10-year Treasury yield: roughly 5.02% to 5.04%, up about 2 to 5 basis points, back above the 5% line.** A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. I could not pin down a single official closing figure for this one — reports from the session put it at 5.025%, 5.03% and 5.04%, against about 4.99% the day before. What is not in doubt is the direction: yields rose because Warsh sounded hawkish, meaning inclined toward higher rates. Bond yields rise when bond prices fall, and bond prices fall when investors expect rates to go up.

- **Crude oil: U.S. West Texas Intermediate settled at $102.78 a barrel, up 2.9%; Brent, the international benchmark, settled at $115.32, up 1.3%.** The move came on reports that Washington is preparing to renew strikes on Iran after the midterm elections, on top of a Strait of Hormuz — the shipping chokepoint for a large share of the world's oil — still running at roughly 60% of normal transit volumes, according to an ANZ Research estimate. Oil above $100 is the single most important reason the Fed is still talking about hikes rather than cuts.

- **Bitcoin: around $80,600 to $81,400.** It climbed above $81,000 right after the Fed announcement, then gave back roughly $1,000 in the hours that followed. Bitcoin trades 24 hours a day, so it has no official close; treat this as a level, not a settlement. High interest rates are generally a headwind for it, which is the pressure analysts pointed to tonight.

## Big movers and why

The indexes barely moved, so the real story was in individual names. Tonight's list is almost entirely earnings-driven.

- **Vertiv Holdings (ticker VRT), which makes the power and cooling equipment that data centers run on, rose about 13.9% — the top gainer in the S&P 500.** It reported net sales growth of 61% and raised its full-year 2026 guidance across all of its key measures. This is the AI trade one layer removed from the chips: whoever builds the data center still has to cool it.

- **F5 (ticker FFIV), a networking and application-security company, fell about 19%.** The quarter itself was not the problem — the forecast was. It guided to adjusted earnings of $18.00 to $18.30 per share for fiscal 2027 against a consensus estimate of $18.03, meaning the midpoint of its own outlook sits essentially *at* what analysts already expected, with the bottom of the range below it. Piper Sandler also cut its rating to neutral.

- **Enphase Energy (ticker ENPH), which makes microinverters for home solar systems, fell roughly 16% to 18%.** Quarterly revenue came in at $293.3 million, down from $410.4 million a year earlier, and fourth-quarter guidance missed estimates too. That is a business shrinking by about 29% year over year.

- **Wingstop (ticker WING), the chicken-wing restaurant chain, rose about 15%** on third-quarter earnings that beat on profit per share even though revenue fell short.

- **Labcorp (ticker LH), the clinical laboratory company, rose about 10%,** and **GE HealthCare (ticker GEHC), the medical imaging and diagnostics maker, rose about 8%.** Both raised full-year guidance. Note the pattern across today's winners: Vertiv, Labcorp and GE HealthCare all went up on *raised guidance*, not on the quarter they just finished.

- **Logitech (ticker LOGI), the computer peripherals maker, fell about 12%,** citing a $70 million to $80 million hit from an incident that shut down a chip supplier's plant. A reminder that supply-chain accidents land on the customer's income statement, not just the supplier's.

- **Robinhood Markets (ticker HOOD), the retail brokerage, fell** after reporting third-quarter earnings of 51 cents per share against the 56 cents analysts expected.

- **Oracle (ticker ORCL), the database and cloud company, rose about 11%** on a long-term free-cash-flow outlook, per Bloomberg's coverage of the session. I found this in one source rather than two, so hold it a little more loosely than the rest of this list.

- **Wendy's (ticker WEN), the burger chain, rose** after confirming it had received a cash proposal of $7.35 per share from a group of investors.

- **Bicara Therapeutics (ticker BCAX), a clinical-stage biotech, fell about 44%** with no company announcement to explain it — the largest decliner among the day's biggest movers. When a stock moves like that with nothing from the company, the honest answer is that the reason is not yet public.

## Headlines that matter

**The Fed held rates at 3.75% to 4.00%, and the dissents went the hawkish way.** The vote was 10-2, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both wanting a quarter-point *increase*. Why this matters: for most of the past three years, when Fed officials disagreed with the majority, they wanted easier money. Dissents arguing for tighter money are a different signal entirely. Remember that this rate range exists because the Fed *raised* rates a quarter point in September — its first increase since 2023.

**The statement upgraded its description of the economy and kept its warning on prices.** It now calls economic activity "expanding at a strong pace," up from "solid" in September, and still says inflation "remains elevated." It also said the Committee wants the "accumulation of more information" before acting again. Why this matters: a central bank that thinks growth is strong has no reason to cut, and one that thinks inflation is elevated has a reason to hike. That combination is what the bond market was reacting to.

**Warsh left no doubt about his priority.** He said, "We will deliver price stability, no ifs, ands or buts." Asked whether political pressure factored into the decision ahead of the midterm elections, he said, "I don't believe it had any role in the decision." He also announced a structural change: from 2027 the FOMC will still hold eight meetings a year, but one will be devoted entirely to broad monetary-policy strategy. Why this matters: the rate decision was already priced in, so the press conference was the actual news, and it moved the odds of a December hike to about 85% from 78% the day before, according to CME FedWatch. (At least one report described a December hike as fully priced; the 85% figure is the one I could corroborate.)

**The Bank of Canada raised its policy rate 25 basis points to 2.5%** — its first increase since July 2023. Governor Tiff Macklem said the hike was meant to reduce the risk that elevated energy prices spill over into broader inflation. Why this matters to you: two central banks on the same day, both pointing at energy costs. Oil above $100 is not just an oil story; it is becoming the thing that sets interest rates in multiple countries, and interest rates are what determine how stocks get valued.

**The earnings day itself was enormous**, with Microsoft and Alphabet after the close — see the next section.

Capital expenditure was about $51.4 billion, roughly $4.4 billion *below* what analysts had modeled. Today's lesson is built on this report, so hold onto it.

## Today's lesson: earnings quality, or how a company can miss by 90 cents without the business getting worse

Here is tonight's puzzle. Alphabet reported earnings of $2.12 per share when Wall Street expected $3.02. That is a miss of 90 cents — a shortfall of nearly a third. In the same report, Google Cloud revenue grew about 103% from a year ago. A business does not double in size and simultaneously collapse in profitability. So what happened?

The answer is a concept called **earnings quality**, and it is one of the most useful ideas in fundamental analysis. Earnings quality asks a single question: *where did this profit number actually come from?* A company's bottom line mixes together two very different things. The first is the operating business — selling ads, renting cloud servers, the stuff the company does every day. The second is everything else: gains and losses on investments it happens to own, legal settlements, tax quirks, restructuring charges. Both land in the same reported earnings-per-share figure, but only the first one tells you anything about how the business is doing.

Alphabet's gap is mostly the second kind. The $8.7 billion loss cited in its report is an **equity loss** — a writedown in the estimated value of ownership stakes Alphabet holds in other companies. Accounting rules require that when the estimated value of those stakes falls, the decline runs through the income statement as though it were a business loss. No cash left the building. Nothing changed about advertising or cloud computing. Do the arithmetic yourself: $8.7 billion spread across Alphabet's roughly 12 billion shares is about 70 cents a share before any tax effects — which accounts for the large majority of that 90-cent "miss." (My own calculation from the reported figures, so treat it as approximate. I should also flag honestly that one source labeled the $2.12 as *adjusted* earnings, which would normally already exclude an item like this; I could not verify which basis it was reported on. When you read the actual release, check whether a number is labeled GAAP — the standardized official accounting — or "adjusted," because they can differ enormously and companies prefer whichever looks better.)

The basketball version: imagine a team loses by nine, and you learn that six of those points were free throws awarded for a technical foul called on a fan in the stands. The six points counted. They are on the scoreboard, they are in the final margin, and the loss is real. But if you are trying to judge how the team *played*, those six points tell you nothing, and a coach who spent film study on them would be wasting the week.

Now the part that makes today a genuinely good lesson rather than a neat trick. The stock fell 7% to 9% anyway. If the miss was mostly accounting noise, why? Because the lines that *do* measure the business also came in soft: Search revenue was $63.35 billion against about $64.85 billion expected, and Search is still Alphabet's engine, far larger than Cloud. The market did roughly what a careful analyst would do — it looked past the equity loss, found a real shortfall in the most important operating line, and priced that. Meanwhile Microsoft beat on revenue *and* earnings, with no asterisk required, and rose. Earnings quality cuts both ways: it protects you from panicking over noise, and it stops you from excusing a real problem as noise. The skill is telling them apart, and tonight you had both in the same hour.

As always, none of this is a recommendation to buy or sell anything. It is a method for reading a number you will see in a headline, so that the headline has less power over you.
