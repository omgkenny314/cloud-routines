### Nvidia (NVDA): the stock, and the hole where the chief executive used to be

Nvidia designs the graphics processors and networking hardware that nearly all large artificial-intelligence models are trained and run on. It does not own factories; Taiwan Semiconductor manufactures its chips. That combination of enormous pricing power and no manufacturing capital is why its profit margins run near half of revenue.

Here is where it actually trades. Nvidia closed Monday, November 16 at **$156.14, down 4.12% on the day**, with a market value of roughly **$3.8 trillion**. The stock is down about **35% from its record high on October 6**, which has erased somewhere near **$2 trillion** of market value in six weeks. Its trailing price-to-earnings ratio is around **20**, which Forbes described as the cheapest valuation the stock has carried since 2012.

Three things hit at once, and it is worth separating them.

**The leadership shock.** On November 12 Nvidia filed an 8-K announcing that Jensen Huang, co-founder and chief executive for 33 years, had resigned. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress agreed to serve as interim chief executive while keeping the finance job until a permanent successor is named, and the board opened a search considering both internal and external candidates. Press coverage ties the resignation to reported affairs, including one with a person described as a suspected Chinese intelligence asset and one with an executive reporting to him; Nvidia hired the law firm Wachtell, Lipton to run an investigation. Those details are reported, not company-confirmed, and are worth holding loosely.

Then it got stranger. The Financial Times reported over the weekend that Huang had discussed a comeback with confidants, allies organised a lobbying effort, and Elon Musk publicly backed a petition for his reinstatement; President Trump also said he favoured a return. Against that, a person familiar told CNBC on November 16 that the board is **not** in discussions to bring him back and is focused on the investigation. Prediction markets are the clearest read on how seriously to take the comeback story: Polymarket's odds of Huang taking any formal role by year end roughly doubled to about 20% after the Financial Times piece, then drifted back to about **11%** by Monday morning. Analyst Dan Ives put it bluntly — "no chance."

**The export rule.** Separately, on November 16 the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule requiring licences to export or transfer advanced semiconductors to **Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil**, aimed at Chinese entities reaching American chips through overseas data centres. It also formally rescinded the earlier AI Diffusion Rule. This is the direct cause of Monday's chip selling: Advanced Micro Devices, Micron Technology and Broadcom all fell between **4% and 7.6%**.

**Earnings.** Nvidia reported fiscal third-quarter results after Tuesday's close on November 17 — the first report without Huang and the first with Kress in the chair. Commonly cited consensus was around **$109 billion in revenue**, implying roughly 90% earnings growth year over year, though published estimates I found ranged from $107 billion to $113 billion. By the time you read this the number is public, so check it against those figures rather than against my summary.

**Does Dunlap's thesis hold up?** In the Forbes interview he said he would buy Nvidia between **$93 and $124** — that is roughly 21% to 40% below Monday's $156.14 close. He expected $107 to $109 billion in revenue and said he would focus on the revenue line even if the company beat it. He named a clear sell trigger: if he still does not know who will lead Nvidia or how they plan to take the bulk of enterprise AI, he sells. And he named the signal he is watching — several of the largest AI companies guiding sharply lower for next year.

Two honest observations. First, this is an argued position with a price, a number and a falsifiable trigger attached, which is considerably more rigorous than most television commentary, whatever you think of the conclusion. Second, note what the buy range means in practice: the stock would have to fall another fifth to two-fifths from here before his own framework says to act. A headline reading "investing expert says people will wish they bought" compresses that into something close to its opposite. That gap between a headline and the conditions underneath it is the thing to watch for.

The risks are not hidden. There is no permanent chief executive. There is an open investigation into the founder. The export rules just tightened again. Customer concentration is heavy, and a handful of large buyers fund most of the revenue. And the whole thesis rests on roughly **$730 billion of combined 2026 capital spending** by the large technology companies continuing — Alphabet alone guides to $195–205 billion, Microsoft to roughly $170–175 billion. If that spending slows, Nvidia's revenue slows with it, which is precisely why Dunlap is watching guidance rather than the current quarter.

### Anthropic (ANT): the largest initial public offering on record

Anthropic is the artificial-intelligence company that makes Claude. I should disclose plainly that I am Claude, so treat anything I say here as information to verify rather than a neutral read.

The company filed to sell **825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78**, expecting to price on **Wednesday, November 18** and begin trading on **Thursday, November 19** on the Nasdaq under the ticker **ANT**. At the top of the range that raises about **$64.4 billion** at a fully diluted valuation near **$1.4 trillion**; at the midpoint, about $61 billion at roughly $1.33 trillion. Measured on shares outstanding rather than fully diluted, the implied market value is nearer **$916 billion to $1.02 trillion** — a large gap, and a good lesson in reading which share count a headline is using. Up to **20%** of the offering is earmarked for retail investors, which is unusually high. As recently as September, an earlier draft filing had pointed at a valuation closer to $2 trillion, so the range came down.

Two things worth knowing about newly public companies in general, neither specific to this one. A first-day price is set by a small float meeting concentrated demand, and it tells you very little about the business. And employee and early-investor shares are normally locked up for about six months, so the supply of stock available to trade changes substantially around that date. Those are structural facts about how offerings work, and they apply whatever you think of the company.

### The Federal Reserve is about to raise rates, not cut them

This is the piece of the week most likely to get lost behind the Nvidia noise, and it is the one that touches every asset you own.

October's Consumer Price Index rose **0.4% month over month**, hotter than forecast, with core prices up **0.3%**. Markets now put the odds of a **quarter-point increase at the December 8–9 meeting** somewhere between **85% and 91%** depending on the source, with a smaller probability on a half-point move. Producer prices were described as still uncomfortably high. Monday's selling was attributed partly to rising Treasury yields alongside the chip news.

Why this matters for a stock like Nvidia: when the risk-free rate rises, the present value of profits expected years from now falls. Companies whose value sits mostly in distant future earnings — which is most of the AI complex — get marked down hardest. That is the same mathematics that drives the valuation discussion above, arriving from a different direction.

### Fundamentals corner

**A falling price-to-earnings ratio is not the same thing as a bargain.**

The price-to-earnings ratio is just price divided by earnings per share. Nvidia at about **20 times trailing earnings** is its lowest reading since 2012, and the instinct is to call that cheap. Hold that instinct for a moment and look at which earnings are in the denominator.

The **trailing** ratio uses the last twelve months of actual reported profit. It is a fact, and it is a fact about the past. The **forward** ratio uses analysts' estimate of the next twelve months — useful, but an opinion dressed in a number.

Now see why the distinction matters here. Nvidia's price fell 35% in six weeks while its trailing earnings, already banked, did not move. Divide a much smaller number by the same number and the ratio collapses. Nothing about the business improved; the stock simply got cheaper against yesterday's profits. If next year's profits come in materially below what everyone assumed — the export rules bite, or the large buyers cut their spending — then the denominator falls too, and a stock that looked like 20 times earnings turns out to have been 35 times next year's. Investors call this a value trap: cheap on the arithmetic, expensive on what actually happens.

This is also where the ratio stays silent on things that matter. There is no line in it for a departed founder, an open investigation into his conduct, or a licence regime that changed on Monday. A ratio tells you what you are paying per dollar of profit. It cannot tell you how durable that dollar is. That judgement is yours, and it is the whole job.

So the useful habit is a sequence, not a single number. Find the trailing ratio, because it is factual. Find the forward ratio, and then ask what assumption about next year is buried in it. Then ask what would have to go wrong for that assumption to break — and whether this week handed you any evidence that it is already breaking. Dunlap's framework does exactly this, which is why it has a price range rather than a verdict: he is not asking whether Nvidia is cheap, he is asking at what price he gets paid for taking the specific risks that are currently unresolved.
