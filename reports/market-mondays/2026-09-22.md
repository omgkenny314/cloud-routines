## What to fix — and this week I have something specific

Last week's report guessed that the launchd jobs were never loaded. That is still possible, but I went through the repository properly this time and found a more likely cause, and it is a two-minute check.

**The reinstall instructions never install the script.**

The README's reinstall recipe has exactly two commands. Both of them copy a `.plist` file into `~/Library/LaunchAgents/` and bootstrap it. Neither one copies the actual script anywhere. And the README says plainly, a few lines further down, that "the copy of the script in this repo is documentation; the deployed copy is the one that runs."

So the script's deployment step exists only in someone's head. If it was ever skipped, launchd has nothing to execute.

**And there is a name mismatch that makes skipping it very easy to do.**

The file in this repository is named with underscores: `fetch_show_transcript.sh`

The plist tells launchd to run a file named with hyphens: `/Users/kennethalford/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh`

If you ever copied the repo file into `~/.local/bin` without renaming it, launchd is looking for a hyphenated file that is not there. The job fails before line one of the code runs.

**This explains the silence perfectly.** Every failure path inside that script alerts you by push. But a script launchd cannot find never runs, so the alerting code never executes. No log line, no error, no alert. That is exactly the symptom: five weeks of nothing, including no complaints.

One command tells you whether this is it:

```
ls -l ~/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh
```

If that says "No such file or directory," you have found your bug. The fix is to copy the script from this repo into place under the hyphenated name, make it executable, and force one run rather than waiting a week to see:

```
launchctl kickstart -p gui/$(id -u)/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-mm
```

Then check that the log actually got written:

```
tail ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/fetch.log
```

So let me score the calls honestly.

**Troy's $1,600 year-end target still requires a lot.** From here that is roughly another 53 percent in about three months. ### Fundamentals corner

**The concept: the cost of waiting for your price.**

This is the lesson the Micron follow-up hands us, and it is one that investing content almost never teaches honestly, because it is unflattering.

When you decide you want to own a stock but not at today's price, you are making a second, separate bet. The first bet is "this company will do well." The second is "I will get a better entry than the one in front of me right now." People treat the second one as free caution. It is not free. It has a cost, and the cost has a name: **opportunity cost** — the return you gave up on the choice you did not make.

Work through what happened here. In mid-August, Micron was around $970. Ian said wait for roughly $820. That is patient, disciplined, and completely reasonable on its face — about a 15 percent discount to the going price. Five weeks later the stock is around $1,043. The $820 never printed. So the person who waited did not save 15 percent. They are now looking at a price 27 percent above the one they were holding out for, and the decision they face today is harder than the one they declined five weeks ago.

**Now the crucial part, because the wrong lesson here is dangerous.** This does not mean waiting is bad, and it absolutely does not mean you should buy whatever is in front of you. Sometimes the pullback comes and the patient buyer is rewarded handsomely. If Micron had gone to $820, Ian would look brilliant and nobody would write this paragraph. **You cannot judge a decision by its outcome** — a good decision can have a bad result, and vice versa. That is one of the hardest habits to build in investing and one of the most valuable.

What you can judge is whether the decision accounted for both costs. And most people only count one.

Here is the asymmetry that makes this worth internalizing. If you buy and the stock drops 15 percent, the loss is visible, it is in your account, it stings, and you will remember it. If you wait and the stock rises 27 percent without you, you lost nothing you ever had — there is no red number anywhere — and most people barely register it. **The pain is lopsided, so the decision-making gets lopsided too.** You end up systematically over-weighting the risk of buying and under-weighting the risk of not buying, because only one of them ever hurts.

**Three practical habits that come out of this:**

First, when you set a target entry price, write down what happens if it never comes. Not vaguely — actually decide. Do you buy anyway higher up? Do you walk away permanently? Do you buy a smaller amount now and more if it dips? Having that answer in advance is the whole game, because you will not think clearly about it in the moment.

Second, notice that "wait for a pullback" is unfalsifiable advice. It is never wrong, because the pullback can always still come. That makes it very comfortable to say on a podcast and very hard to hold anyone accountable for. Whenever you hear advice that cannot be graded, grade it yourself later — which is exactly what this section did.

Third, understand why **dollar-cost averaging** exists. Buying a fixed dollar amount on a fixed schedule regardless of price is not a way to get the best entry. It is a way to stop making entry-timing decisions at all, which sidesteps this entire problem. You give up the chance of a brilliant entry in exchange for never missing a move entirely. For most people learning, that trade is worth making — and it is a strategy, not a stock pick, which is why it belongs in this section rather than the one above.

The bigger skill underneath all of this: separate **what to own** from **when to buy it**. They are different questions with different kinds of evidence behind them, and mixing them up is how good research turns into bad decisions. The August 18 episode did a solid job on the first question. This week shows how much harder the second one is.
