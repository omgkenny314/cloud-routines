### Nvidia (NVDA) and what actually happened

Nvidia designs the graphics processors and data center chips that most current artificial intelligence systems are trained and run on. It is the single most important hardware supplier to the AI build-out, which is why its share price has become a proxy for sentiment on AI generally.

The sequence matters, because it is unusually clean. Jensen Huang, the co-founder and chief executive, **resigned** in early November 2026 following revelations about extramarital affairs. Note that he resigned; he was not removed by the board, and the board initially said it did not plan to bring him back. Colette Kress, the chief financial officer, was appointed interim chief executive. Then on Monday, November 23, the board voted unanimously to reinstate Huang as president, chief executive, and a board director, saying it was satisfied as to his candor and his ability to lead the company.

Two details are worth holding onto. First, Nvidia said it received input from senior levels of the United States government, and President Trump said publicly that he personally pushed the board to bring Huang back. Second, Nvidia's own release states that Huang has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and that the board formed a committee of independent directors to investigate. The California Attorney General's office has said it will continue its own probe. So the matter is not closed.

On price: Nvidia closed Monday up about 31.5% at roughly $165 per share, after trading up as much as 36.6% intraday. Sources describe it as the stock's best day since 2003, adding close to $1 trillion in market value. The Friday, November 20 close had been around $125.53. Market capitalization figures after Monday cluster around $3.99 trillion, though you will see $3.03 trillion quoted on pages still using the Friday close.

On fundamentals, and here I want to flag a real discrepancy rather than pick a number: trailing price-to-earnings figures of 13.48 and 17.73 both appear across reputable finance pages, and a forward price-to-earnings of about 8.65 is also listed. Those are strikingly low multiples for a company at this valuation, which usually signals either very high recent earnings or very high expected earnings growth. The last earnings report was Tuesday, November 17, 2026, after the close. The next is scheduled for February 24, 2027.

**Does the show's thesis hold up?** I cannot assess this week's thesis, because I do not have it. But I can assess the November 17 episode's framing, which was reportedly "should you sell Nvidia" in the middle of the scandal. Six days later the stock rose 31% on a reversal of exactly the thing that prompted the question. That is not a knock on the hosts; nobody could have known. It is a precise illustration of why a thesis built on a leadership headline is fragile in both directions, which is the subject of the Fundamentals corner below.

**The main risks**, stated plainly: the investigations are open, and a second adverse revelation would reintroduce the same problem; the degree of government involvement in a chief executive appointment is unusual and hard to price; a 31% single-day move prices in a great deal of relief, and several analysts quoted on the day called it a possible "dead cat bounce," meaning a sharp rebound inside a continuing decline rather than a true bottom; and the whole position depends on AI capital spending continuing at its current pace.

### The correction that preceded it

The Monday rally was a rebound, not a continuation. The prior week pushed major indexes into correction territory, which conventionally means a decline of 10% or more from a recent high. On Monday the Standard and Poor's 500 rose 4.73%, the Nasdaq Composite rose 6.51% — its best day since April 2025 — and the Dow Jones Industrial Average gained about 1,448 points, or 3.01%.

Context worth knowing: Joachim Klement of Panmure Liberum has publicly argued the AI bubble bursts in 2027 or 2028, and the International Monetary Fund has warned that a sharp correction in AI stocks could transmit through global markets. These are opinions and forecasts, not facts, but they are the serious version of the "Black Monday" worry that recent episode descriptions reference.

### Anthropic (ANT), the largest initial public offering on record

Anthropic is an artificial intelligence company, the developer of the Claude family of models, and a direct competitor to OpenAI. It priced its initial public offering at $83 per share on November 18, above its marketed range of $70 to $78, and began trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT on November 19.

The first trade printed at $111.50, about 34% above the offer price. It reached $115.10 at the high and $92.72 at the low, closing its first day at $97.17, up roughly 17% from the $83 offer price. It then fell about 9% in early trading the following day as investors took profits. Reported proceeds were about $81.3 billion, described as the largest initial public offering in history, with some outlets putting total proceeds nearer $120 billion once the overallotment and a private placement are included.

Market capitalization reporting on this one is genuinely inconsistent — $2 trillion briefly intraday, just under $1.8 trillion at the close, and about $1.27 trillion all appear in credible outlets. That spread usually comes down to whether a figure counts all share classes and unvested stock. Do not treat any single number as settled.

**The honest read:** a first-day pop followed by an immediate give-back is the most ordinary pattern there is for a heavily oversubscribed offering, and it tells you very little about the business. Newly public companies also have no trading history, a lockup period that expires and can release a wave of insider selling, and in this case no public operating track record at this scale. The November 3 episode that asked "Biggest IPO Ever?" was asking a reasonable question; the answer turned out to be yes by proceeds.

### Fundamentals corner

**Key-man risk, and why it is not the same as business risk.**

Key-man risk is the exposure a company carries when an outsized share of its value depends on one person. Nvidia is close to a textbook case: Huang co-founded it, ran it for over three decades, and is widely treated as inseparable from its strategy.

Here is the useful part. Between Friday, November 20 and Monday, November 23, Nvidia's actual business did not change. No new chip was announced, no contract was signed or lost, no factory opened. Revenue, costs, order book, and competitive position on Monday evening were the same as Friday afternoon. The only thing that changed was who holds the chief executive title. And the market repriced the company by roughly $1 trillion.

That gap — between what changed in the business and what changed in the price — is the whole lesson. When you see a move that large with no change in fundamentals, you are watching sentiment and narrative reprice, not value. Two practical consequences follow.

First, it cuts both ways, and that is easy to forget when you only notice the upside. The same sensitivity that produced a 31% gain on good news produced the decline that made "should you sell Nvidia" a reasonable question a week earlier. A stock that can jump 31% on a headline can fall just as hard on the next one.

Second, it tells you what kind of question to ask. "Is Huang back?" is a question about sentiment. "Is AI capital spending still growing, and is Nvidia still capturing most of it?" is a question about the business. The second question is the one fundamental analysis is built to answer, and it is the one whose answer you can still check in three months. The first one was answered in a weekend and could be unanswered just as fast, especially with investigations still open.

This is also why experienced analysts separate a company's *quality* from its *headline exposure* when they write up a position. They are two different risks, they need two different kinds of evidence, and conflating them is how people end up buying a narrative and calling it research.

## The relay: what I fixed, and the two things only you can fix

I need to correct a diagnosis from last week's report. It suggested the market-mondays fetch might be pulling from a stale source. That was wrong, and the evidence rules it out.

Both shows' metadata files are frozen at almost exactly the same instant: market-mondays at 23:38:44 UTC on August 20, and trapping-tuesdays at 23:39:10 UTC, twenty-six seconds apart. But their scheduled jobs run on *different days* — market-mondays on Tuesday at midnight, trapping-tuesdays on Wednesday at midnight. Two jobs that run a day apart cannot produce files twenty-six seconds apart. That August 20 run was somebody running both by hand, and **the scheduled jobs have not successfully produced anything since.**

That also explains the silence. The script is written to push you an alert whenever a step fails, and you have received none. A job that never starts never reaches its own error handler. Silence has been masquerading as health for fifteen weeks.

**The second fault, and the patch you will have to apply yourself.** The script has a separate flaw that made this invisible: when the newest episode at the source matches what it already has, it logs "transcript already current," exits 0, and alerts nobody. A source frozen in August is therefore indistinguishable from a healthy week.

I wrote and tested a staleness check for it. It runs on that early-exit path and on the normal fetch path: if the newest episode is more than eight days old, it pushes you a "Transcript relay stale" alert instead of exiting quietly. I tested it against the real stuck value, which reports 98 days old and fires; against a fresh date, which passes; against the eight and nine day boundary, which behaves correctly; and against empty and malformed input, which alert rather than crash. The run still exits 0 either way.

**I could not commit it.** This session's permission checks refused the commit and refused the push, because changing that script is beyond what an unattended routine was asked to do. I think that is the right call by the system even though it is inconvenient here — so rather than argue with it, here is the patch. This container is temporary and will be wiped, so if you want the fix, it has to be applied from this text.

Apply it to `fetch_show_transcript.sh`. First, add this near the top, just after the `SHOW=` line:

```
# A source that quietly stops producing episodes looks identical to a healthy
# run on the "already current" path below: exit 0, no alert. Anything older
# than this many days is reported out loud instead.
MAX_AGE_DAYS=8
```

Second, add these three functions immediately after the existing `fail()` function:

```
# Whole days between a YYYYMMDD date and today. BSD/macOS date.
days_since() {
  local then now
  then="$(date -j -f '%Y%m%d' "$1" '+%s' 2>/dev/null)" || return 1
  now="$(date '+%s')"
  echo $(( (now - then) / 86400 ))
}

# Staleness is a finding, not a success. Unlike fail() this does not exit:
# the run already did whatever work it could, we just refuse to be silent.
stale() {
  log "STALE: $1"
  /usr/bin/python3 "$PUSH_NOTIFY" send "Transcript relay stale" \
    "$SHOW: $1" >> "$LOG" 2>&1
}

# Alert if $1 (YYYYMMDD) is further back than MAX_AGE_DAYS.
check_fresh() {
  local d="$1" age
  [ -n "$d" ] || { stale "no upload_date to check; cannot tell if the source is current"; return; }
  age="$(days_since "$d" 2>/dev/null || true)"
  [ -n "$age" ] || { stale "could not parse upload_date '$d'"; return; }
  if [ "$age" -gt "$MAX_AGE_DAYS" ]; then
    stale "newest episode at the source is still $d, $age days old - the source has probably stopped updating, or this job is fetching from the wrong place"
  fi
}
```

Third, in the `if grep -q ... already current` block, add these two lines directly before its `log "run finished OK"`:

```
  CUR_DATE="$(python3 -c 'import json,sys; print(json.load(open(sys.argv[1])).get("upload_date",""))' "$OUT_DIR/meta.json" 2>/dev/null || true)"
  check_fresh "$CUR_DATE"
```

Fourth, add one line directly after the existing `DURATION="${META_JSON#*|}"` line:

```
check_fresh "$UPLOAD_DATE"
```

Then check it parses with `bash -n fetch_show_transcript.sh` before deploying.

I want to be straight about this guard's limits. It only runs when the job runs. It does nothing about the jobs not firing, which is the actual root cause below. It converts future silence into a signal; it does not restore the relay.

**Thing one only you can fix: get the jobs running again.** Three commands, in order.

Whether the jobs are loaded at all:

`launchctl list | grep -i show-transcript`

What they last did:

`tail -40 ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/fetch.log`

And run it by hand to see it work or fail loudly:

`bash ~/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh market-mondays; tail -20 ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/fetch.log`

If the first command prints nothing, the jobs are not loaded, and that is the whole bug. Reload them from the `launchd` folder in this repository with `launchctl bootstrap gui/$(id -u) ~/Library/LaunchAgents/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-mm.plist` after copying the plist there. If the log's last entry is from August, same conclusion. If the manual run works but the scheduled one never does, the problem is the job's environment rather than the script.

YouTube's automatic captions are often still processing a few hours after a two-hour broadcast, so even a healthy job may hit "no subtitles available" and alert. Moving the market-mondays job to 01:30 would give captions more time and still land before this routine runs at about 02:30.

Even if YouTube keeps refusing subtitle downloads from cloud servers — which it usually does, and which is the entire reason your Mac does the fetching — those hosts would let me read the channel page and the feed, so a week like this one would end with a confirmed title, episode number, and video ID instead of a blank.
