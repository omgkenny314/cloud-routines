### Nvidia (NVDA) — the numbers behind the headline

**What it does:** Nvidia designs the graphics processors and data-center accelerators that nearly all large-scale AI training and inference runs on, plus the networking and software layers around them. It does not own factories; it designs chips and has TSMC manufacture them.

**The quarter the episode was reacting to.** Nvidia reported fiscal Q2 2027 (the quarter ended July 26, 2026) on August 26. Revenue was **$96.2 billion**, up 18% from the prior quarter and up **106% year over year**. Data center revenue was **$89.0 billion**, up 117% year over year and about **92% of the entire company**. Gross margin was **75.0%**. Guidance for the current quarter is **$108.0 billion**, an $11.8 billion sequential jump. It was the company's fourteenth consecutive quarter beating its own outlook.

One oddity worth knowing about: the release showed GAAP earnings per diluted share of $2.46 against non-GAAP of $2.22. That is backwards from the usual pattern — non-GAAP is normally the higher number, because it strips out costs like stock compensation. When GAAP comes in *above* non-GAAP it usually means a one-time gain flowed through the official figures. I could not verify the cause from the summary sources I have, so treat that specific line as something to check in the actual filing rather than a fact I have confirmed.

**Where it trades.** Roughly **$230.60** as of September 7, with a market cap around **$5.55 trillion**. Trailing P/E about **29**, forward P/E about **19**.

**Does the setup hold up?** On the numbers, yes, and it is not close. Triple-digit revenue growth at 75% gross margins at a $5.5 trillion market cap is a genuinely unusual thing in market history. The forward P/E near 19 is the detail most people find surprising — that is not a bubble multiple on its face; it is roughly a market multiple. But read the next paragraph before you conclude anything from that.

**The risks, honestly.** That 19 forward P/E is only cheap *if the forecast earnings actually arrive*, and those forecasts assume AI capital spending keeps climbing. Nvidia's revenue is extraordinarily concentrated — 92% from one segment, and within that segment, a handful of hyperscale customers who are themselves deciding each year how much to spend. Several of those customers are also designing their own chips to reduce dependence on Nvidia. Guiding to $108 billion means the bar resets higher every single quarter; the fourteen-quarter beat streak is impressive and is also a trap, because the day it ends is priced as a catastrophe rather than a normal miss. Add export restrictions on China sales and the concentration of manufacturing at TSMC in Taiwan. The next earnings report is expected in late November.

**The fundamentals.** These are better than the stock chart suggests, and that gap is the whole argument. Q2 fiscal 2026 revenue grew **12.7%**; full-year fiscal 2026 growth is guided around **11.8%**. Q1 was a record $6.4 billion, up 12%, with total ARR exiting at **$26.1 billion**. The company recently *raised* its fiscal 2026 guidance, citing uptake of its AI products — Firefly, Acrobat AI Assistant, and newer web-based agents. AI-first ARR reportedly tripled year over year.

The reliable summary: it trades somewhere in the **$250–265** range and it is down sharply, on the order of 20–28% this year. Analysts have cut implied fair value from about $332 to roughly $270.

**The catalyst is immediate.** Adobe reports Q3 fiscal 2026 **after the close on Thursday, September 10** — two days from now. If you were going to pay attention to one thing from this episode, this is the one with a clock on it.

**Does the debate framing hold up?** Yes, and this is a genuinely well-chosen name to argue about. The bull case is concrete: double-digit growth, real profitability, $26 billion of recurring revenue, AI products actually selling, guidance going up, and a stock down a fifth to two fifths. When a stock falls 20%+ while earnings rise, the market is not disputing the current numbers — it is disputing how long they last. That is a legitimate disagreement, not a mispricing one side is obviously right about.

### SPY versus SMH — what the viewer was actually asking

**What they are.** SPY holds the S&P 500 — roughly 500 large U.S. companies across every sector. SMH holds the **25 largest** U.S.-listed semiconductor companies and nothing else. SMH is formally classified as **non-diversified**, which is a regulatory term with real meaning, not marketing language.

**The performance gap that prompted the question.** SMH is up about **58%** year to date. The S&P 500 is up about **13.6%** total return through September 4. That is a 44-point gap in eight months, and it is exactly the kind of number that makes people want to switch.

**What is actually inside SMH.** Nvidia is around **20%** of the fund. Taiwan Semiconductor is around **10%**. The top two holdings alone are roughly a third of it, and the top ten run about **60–65%**.

**The honest read.** Here is the thing the performance number hides: if you already own SPY, you already own a meaningful slice of Nvidia, because Nvidia is one of the largest companies in the index. Moving from SPY to SMH is not adding semiconductor exposure to a portfolio that lacks it — it is concentrating an existing bet by a large multiple and dropping every other sector. The 58% return is the reward for that concentration having worked this year. The same structure is what produces the losses when semiconductors turn, and semiconductors have historically been one of the most cyclical sectors in the market. I am not telling you which to hold. I am telling you the question is not "which returned more" — it is "how much of my outcome do I want riding on one industry, and mostly on two companies within it."

### Fundamentals corner

**Concentration risk, and why "diversified" is not a vibe.**

The SPY-versus-SMH question in this episode is the cleanest possible way into one of the most important ideas in portfolio construction, so let me take it slowly.

When you buy a fund, you are buying a basket. The natural assumption is that a basket protects you — spread across many companies, no single failure can hurt you much. That assumption is only true if the basket is actually spread out, and a lot of baskets are not.

The measure to look at is called **concentration**: what percentage of the fund sits in its largest few holdings. For SPY, the top ten holdings are a meaningful chunk but the remaining 490 companies still matter, and they span healthcare, banks, energy, retail, industrials. For SMH, the top ten are about 60–65% of the fund, and the top *two* are about a third. Every one of the 25 holdings is in the same industry, selling to overlapping customers, exposed to the same cycle.

So the real difference between those two funds is not "one is a bit riskier." It is that SMH's outcome is substantially determined by two companies and entirely determined by one industry's cycle, while SPY's is determined by the broad U.S. economy. Those are different kinds of bets, not different intensities of the same bet.

Two practical habits follow from this.

**First, always look up what a fund actually holds before you buy it.** Every fund publishes its holdings, and the top-ten weights are usually on the first page of the fact sheet. This takes two minutes and it is the single highest-value piece of research you can do on an ETF. A fund's name tells you almost nothing about its concentration.

**Second, check for overlap with what you already own.** This is the part people miss. If you hold SPY and then buy SMH "for diversification," you have not diversified — you have doubled down on Nvidia, because Nvidia is already one of the largest positions inside SPY. Adding a fund only diversifies you if it holds things you do not already own. Adding a sector fund on top of a broad index fund almost always does the opposite: it takes a bet you already had and makes it bigger.

There is also a term you will hear that connects directly to this. **Non-diversified** is a formal classification under U.S. fund regulation, not a judgment. A diversified fund faces legal limits on how large any single position can grow; a non-diversified fund does not. When a fund is labeled non-diversified, it is telling you outright that it is permitted to become very concentrated. SMH carries that label. That is not a flaw in the fund — concentration is the entire product, and it is why it returned 58% this year. It just means the label is doing real work, and it is worth reading.

The general principle, which will serve you well beyond these two tickers: **high returns from a concentrated fund are not evidence that the fund is good. They are evidence that the concentration paid off in that period.** The structure that produced a 58% gain is the same structure that produces the large losses when the cycle turns. Judging a concentrated fund by its recent return tells you about the last eight months. It tells you nothing about the risk you are taking on going forward — and the risk is the part you actually control.
