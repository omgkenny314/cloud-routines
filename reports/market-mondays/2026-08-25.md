### The market backdrop on the day they recorded

Monday, August 24 was a split day. The S&P 500 fell 0.28% to 7,652.86 and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.76% to 25,980.19, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 140.15 points, or 0.26%, to 53,417.16.

The weakness was concentrated in semiconductors. Memory chip stocks sold off after a weekend report that Nvidia plans to raise prices on some of its AI servers — the logic being that if Nvidia is absorbing higher costs, memory suppliers may have less pricing leverage than the market assumed. Micron dropped sharply, and Advanced Micro Devices and Broadcom fell more than 3% and 2% respectively.

That is the context for the episode's "is a market correction next?" framing. It was a real question that day, not a rhetorical one.

### Nvidia (NVDA)

Nvidia designs the graphics processors and data-center systems that most large AI models are trained and run on. It is the single largest company in the market by value.

The stock traded around $214.77 on August 24, giving it a market capitalization of roughly $5.21 trillion. It trades at about 32.6 times trailing earnings and roughly 24 times forward earnings, with revenue growth around 70.7% and return on equity around 114.3%.

Pause on those numbers, because they are unusual. A company growing revenue 70% a year while trading at 24 times forward earnings is not obviously expensive by growth-stock standards — CrowdStrike, further down, trades at seven times that multiple. Nvidia's valuation debate is less about the multiple and more about whether the growth rate is durable.

The catalyst was two days out: Nvidia reported fiscal second-quarter 2027 results on Wednesday, August 26, after the close. Consensus expected roughly $91.9 billion in revenue and $2.08 in earnings per share, against company guidance of about $91 billion.

The risk worth understanding: Nvidia has beaten consensus estimates for four straight quarters, and the stock sold off after every one of those beats. That pattern tells you expectations are priced in — beating the number is not enough when the market has already assumed the beat. Nvidia also went into this print down seven consecutive sessions, which cuts both ways: it lowers the bar, but it also signals that large holders were reducing risk ahead of the event.

### Micron Technology (MU)

Micron makes memory chips — DRAM, NAND flash, and high-bandwidth memory, or HBM, which is the specialized memory that sits alongside AI accelerators like Nvidia's.

Micron fell roughly 6% to 7% on August 24, to about $898. Sources differ slightly on the exact drop, reporting between 5.8% and 7.1%, so treat the precise figure as approximate. That came off a record high near $1,089, after a run of more than 700% over the past year. Market capitalization is around $1.1 trillion.

The bull case is concrete rather than speculative. Memory prices across DRAM, NAND, and HBM rose 80% to 90% between the fourth quarter of 2025 and the first quarter of 2026. Micron's entire 2026 HBM supply is already contracted on both price and volume, which means a meaningful share of this year's revenue is locked in rather than exposed to spot pricing. The HBM market was roughly $35 billion in 2025 and is projected to approach $100 billion by 2028. Analyst Pierre Ferragu upgraded the stock to Buy with a $1,250 target against a recent close of $949.83.

The risk is the shape of the business. Memory is historically cyclical — supply catches up with demand, pricing rolls over, and the stock falls much faster than earnings do. A 700% one-year gain means a great deal of good news is already in the price, and Monday's 6% drop on a single secondhand report about Nvidia server pricing shows how fragile sentiment is at these levels.

Worth flagging for continuity: on last week's episode Troy held a $1,600 year-end target on Micron and called it undervalued. That thesis is not broken by one bad day, but the stock is roughly 18% below its record high, and you should know that if you are tracking the call.

### CrowdStrike (CRWD)

CrowdStrike sells cloud-based cybersecurity, primarily endpoint protection — software that monitors laptops, servers, and cloud workloads for intrusions. It sells by subscription, so the metric that matters is annual recurring revenue, or ARR.

The stock is up roughly 91% in 2026 and set an intraday record of $227.21 on August 14, trading around $207 to $213 by August 19. Like Nvidia, it reported on Wednesday, August 26, after the close. Management had guided net new ARR of $284 million to $286 million and ending ARR of about $5.79 billion.

The number to sit with is the valuation: roughly 166 to 175 times forward earnings and about 35 to 38 times forward sales. That is an extraordinary multiple, and it sets a specific trap. At 175 times forward earnings, meeting expectations is not a good outcome — the market needs a beat *and* raised full-year guidance just to justify the current price. The highest analyst price target on the street was $256, and at least one published analysis argued the stock could fall 30% on a disappointing print.

This is the clearest example in the episode of valuation risk being separate from business quality. CrowdStrike can be an excellent company and still be a painful stock from this entry point, because the price already assumes years of flawless execution.

### McDonald's (MCD)

McDonald's came up as an economic indicator rather than a pick, and that framing is sound. Because its customer base skews toward lower-income households, its traffic is a reasonably good read on whether ordinary consumers are under strain.

The signal right now is not encouraging. In the second quarter of 2026, US same-store sales rose just 0.8%, and the composition matters more than the number: the average check went up while traffic went down. That means the growth came from price increases, not from more people walking in. Chief Executive Chris Kempczinski has pointed specifically at elevated gas prices squeezing low-income consumers.

The stock closed at $272.28 on August 13, below both its 50-day moving average of $273.95 and its 200-day average of $295.44, and it has underperformed the S&P 500 by about 32.7% over the past year. The average analyst price target sits near $337.

Read McDonald's here as a data point about the consumer, which is how the show appears to have used it — a weakening consumer is one of the more credible arguments for the correction the episode's title asks about.

### CAVA Group (CAVA) versus Chipotle Mexican Grill (CMG)

This is a genuine growth-versus-value matchup and a good teaching case.

CAVA operates fast-casual Mediterranean restaurants and is in rapid expansion. First-quarter revenue rose 32% to $434.4 million, helped by 20 new restaurants and same-store sales growth of 10% year over year, with full-year guidance of about 5% to 6% same-store growth. It trades around 172 times forward earnings and about 49.6 times enterprise value to EBITDA.

Chipotle is the scaled incumbent. Its same-store sales rose just 0.5% in the first quarter and 2.2% in the second — slow, but recovering. It trades around 29 times forward earnings and about 20.3 times enterprise value to EBITDA, with return on equity near 49.6% and no debt on the balance sheet.

So CAVA is growing roughly five times faster and costs roughly six times more per dollar of earnings. Neither is automatically the better business. The honest framing is that CAVA's price already assumes years of near-flawless expansion with very little room for a stumble, while Chipotle's price assumes the slowdown is permanent. Whichever side of that you find more plausible is where your answer lies.

### Bitcoin (BTC)

Bitcoin earned the episode's headline. It pushed past $79,000 on the morning of August 24, touching an intraday high near $79,406, after gaining roughly 24% over seven days. US spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds took in close to $2 billion the prior week.

Two catalysts are identifiable. The first is Treasury bond market intervention, which markets read as liquidity support. The second is political: President Trump publicly called for passage of the CLARITY Act, a crypto market-structure bill, which the market treated as a regulatory tailwind.

The risk is straightforward. A 24% gain in seven days is a momentum move, and moves like that are driven substantially by flows rather than by any change in what the asset is. Bitcoin stalled at $80,000, which is the kind of round-number level where rallies commonly pause. Nothing in the current setup makes the move durable on its own.

### The bond market and the VIX

This is the most substantive part of the episode's macro block, and the underlying data is real.

The 30-year Treasury yield reached 5.31%, its highest level since June 2007, and as of August 19 had held at or above 5% for 32 consecutive trading days — the longest such streak since the summer of 2007. The concerns driving it are corporate credit stress, interest-rate expectations, and the scale of national debt issuance.

Why this pressures stocks: when a government bond, which carries essentially no default risk, pays over 5% for thirty years, it competes directly with stocks for capital. It also raises the discount rate used to value future corporate profits, which mechanically lowers what high-growth companies are worth today. That is why long-yield spikes tend to hit expensive growth stocks — a CrowdStrike at 175 times earnings — hardest.

The VIX side is the mirror image. The VIX measures expected S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days, and it hit a 2026 low around August 17. A very low VIX is not a prediction of calm; it means options are cheap because few people are hedging. The warning is about positioning, not forecasting: when nobody is protected, an unexpected shock moves the market further than it otherwise would.

Put together, the episode's macro thesis is coherent — high long-term yields creating pressure, and a complacent volatility market creating fragility.

### The midterm election angle

The historical record here is stronger than most seasonal market claims, so it is worth knowing accurately.

From 1945 through the end of 2025, the S&P 500 averaged a gain of just 3.8% in midterm election years, against 10.9% in the other three years of the presidential cycle. Midterm years also carry the deepest drawdowns: about 18% on average versus 12.4% for other years, and in 11 of the last 16 midterm years the index fell 15% or more at some point.

The pattern then inverts. Following midterm elections, the S&P 500 has averaged 5.7% over three months, 10.5% over six months, and 13.6% over twelve months. Since 1950, the index has been higher twelve months after every single midterm election — nineteen out of nineteen.

The caveat you should carry: nineteen observations is a small sample, and "has never failed" over nineteen instances is a much weaker statement than it sounds. Treat it as context for why a rough patch in 2026 would be unremarkable, not as a forecast.

### Fundamentals corner

**What a price-to-earnings multiple actually means.**

This episode surfaced it naturally, because the same list of companies spanned almost the entire range a multiple can take: Nvidia near 24 times forward earnings, Chipotle near 29, CAVA near 172, CrowdStrike near 175.

The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share. If a company earns $5 per share and trades at $100, its P/E is 20. The cleanest way to hear that number is as a payback period: at today's earnings, it takes 20 years of profits to add up to what you paid. Twenty is roughly the long-run average for large US companies.

"Forward" P/E uses next year's *estimated* earnings instead of last year's actual ones. That is more useful for a growing company, and also less reliable, because an estimate can be wrong in a way a historical figure cannot.

The essential insight is this: a high multiple is not the same as an expensive stock, and a low multiple is not the same as a cheap one. The multiple tells you what growth the market has already assumed. Nvidia at 24 times forward earnings while growing 70% is arguably a modest multiple — the market is not assuming that growth persists. CrowdStrike at 175 times is a very demanding one — the market is assuming many years of rapid, uninterrupted expansion, and has priced those years in today.

That is why an expensive stock can fall on good news. If a company at 175 times earnings beats expectations but only matches the growth already assumed, nothing new has been learned and there is no reason for the price to rise — while any hint of a slowdown removes an assumption the price depended on.

So when you hear a multiple, the useful question is never "is this high or low?" It is "what does this price already assume, and do I find that assumption believable?" That single reframing is most of what fundamental valuation is.
