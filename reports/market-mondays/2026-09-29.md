## What to fix — I was wrong last week, and I found the real bug

Last week I told you the likely cause was that the script had never been copied into `~/.local/bin`, possibly under the wrong filename. **That was a guess, and the evidence now says it is wrong.** The script clearly did run — twice — so it exists and it works.

Here is what I actually found. Every transcript commit this repository has ever received:

- Market Mondays, August 2 — landed **Sunday** at 12:45 PM Pacific.
- Trappin Tuesdays, August 2 — landed **Sunday** at 12:50 PM Pacific.
- Market Mondays, August 20 — landed **Thursday** at 4:38 PM Pacific.
- Trappin Tuesdays, August 20 — landed **Thursday** at 4:39 PM Pacific.

Now compare that to the schedule. The Market Mondays job is set for **Tuesday at 00:00**. The Trappin Tuesdays job is set for **Wednesday at 00:00**.

**Not one commit landed on its scheduled day. Not one landed anywhere near midnight.** And look at the pairs: both times, the two shows landed *five minutes apart* — on the same afternoon. A working schedule would put Market Mondays on Tuesday and Trappin Tuesdays on Wednesday, roughly 24 hours apart. Five minutes apart on a Sunday afternoon and a Thursday afternoon is the unmistakable signature of someone running both by hand, back to back.

**Conclusion: the launchd jobs have never fired successfully, not once.** Every transcript this repo has ever received, you fetched by hand. August 20 was the day you relocated the relay out of Documents — you verified it manually, it worked, and it has never run on its own since. The relocation fixed the script. It never fixed the scheduling.

**And this explains the silence completely.** Your script has push alerts on every failure path. But a job that never starts never reaches those alerts. The watchdog cannot bark if it is never let out. That is why you have had six weeks of nothing *and* six weeks of no complaints — which is the most confusing part of this whole thing, and now it makes sense.

**The two commands that confirm it.** Run these and you will know in thirty seconds:

```
launchctl list | grep kennethalford
```

If nothing comes back, the jobs are not loaded at all, and that is your answer.

```
launchctl print gui/$(id -u)/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-mm | grep -iE "last exit|state|path"
```

This shows whether the job is loaded, what it last exited with, and — critically — **which script path it is pointing at.**

**The likeliest specific cause.** Your installed plists in `~/Library/LaunchAgents/` may still be the *old* ones from before the August 20 relocation, still pointing into `~/Documents`. Those would fail with "Operation not permitted" before your script's first line, which means no alert. The plists in this repo point at the correct new `~/.local/bin` path — but copying them into the repo is not the same as installing them. If you never re-ran the bootstrap commands after the relocation, the old broken ones are still what launchd is using.

**The fix**, one job at a time:

```
cp ~/Documents/cloud-routines/launchd/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-mm.plist ~/Library/LaunchAgents/
launchctl bootout gui/$(id -u)/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-mm 2>/dev/null
launchctl bootstrap gui/$(id -u) ~/Library/LaunchAgents/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-mm.plist
```

The `bootout` line is the one the README is missing. Without it, `bootstrap` refuses to replace an already-loaded job, and you will think you reinstalled something you did not. Then do the same for the `-tt` plist.

**Then force a run rather than waiting a week to find out:**

```
launchctl kickstart -p gui/$(id -u)/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-mm
tail ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/fetch.log
```

If that log gets a fresh line, the relay is alive. If the log does not move, the job still is not executing and the plist path is wrong.

**Two smaller things while you are in there.**

The job runs Tuesday at midnight Pacific, which is 2:00 AM your time. The show airs Monday evening, and YouTube's automatic captions on a two-hour livestream are often not ready that fast. Even a perfectly working job can hit the "no subtitles available, may still be processing" path. Since my run starts at 2:30 AM Central, there is not much slack — but moving the job an hour earlier in Pacific terms would help, and it would at least produce an alert instead of silence.

### Fundamentals corner

**The concept: why cyclical stocks look cheapest exactly when they are most dangerous.**

Here is a puzzle that Micron hands us perfectly this week, and it is one of the most useful things you can learn in fundamental analysis.

Micron is up 279 percent this year. It is worth 1.19 trillion dollars. And it trades at a **forward price-to-earnings ratio of roughly 11 to 14 times**, depending on whose estimates and which day you take. For comparison, the S&P 500 as a whole typically trades in the high teens to low twenties.

So a stock that has nearly quadrupled looks *cheaper than the average company in the market.* How?

**Start with what P/E actually is.** The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share. At 1,053 dollars and an expected annual EPS somewhere around 72 to 75 dollars, you get roughly 14 times. Read plainly, it means you are paying about 14 dollars for each dollar of annual profit. Lower usually reads as cheaper.

**Now the trap.** That ratio has two moving parts, and beginners watch only the top one. Price is in the numerator, earnings in the denominator. A P/E can fall because the price dropped — or because earnings exploded.

Micron's earnings have exploded. Memory prices are in a historic upcycle, HBM is sold out through 2027, and profits have gone nearly vertical. The denominator grew faster than the numerator. That is the entire explanation for the low ratio.

**Here is why that is the opposite of comforting.** Micron sells a commodity. Memory chips from different manufacturers are close to interchangeable, so the price is set by supply and demand, not by brand. Commodity businesses are *cyclical*: when supply is tight, prices and margins soar; when competitors finish building new factories, supply floods in, prices collapse, and margins go with them. Those factories are being built right now — that is what a boom causes.

So the earnings in that denominator are **peak-cycle earnings**, and peak-cycle earnings are the least durable kind. If memory prices normalize and EPS falls by half, then at an unchanged share price the P/E instantly doubles from 14 to 28 — and the stock that "looked cheap" is suddenly expensive without having moved at all.

**This is one of the most reliable traps in fundamental analysis, and it has a name.** For cyclical companies, a **low P/E often signals the top of the cycle, and a high P/E often signals the bottom.** It is backwards from the intuition, and it catches people constantly. At the bottom, earnings are near zero, so the ratio looks terrifyingly high right when the stock is cheapest. At the top, earnings are at a record, so the ratio looks like a bargain right when the risk is greatest.

**How to actually use this.** Three habits:

First, when you see a strikingly low P/E, **ask which part moved.** Did the price fall, or did earnings spike? Pull up revenue and earnings over five years, not one. A company whose profits tripled in eighteen months is telling you something very different from one whose price fell by two-thirds.

Second, ask **whether the company sells a commodity or something with pricing power.** If customers choose on price alone, the business is cyclical and today's margins are a loan from tomorrow. If customers would pay more rather than switch, earnings are more durable and a low P/E means more.

Third — and this is where it connects to tomorrow — understand that **the market prices in expectations, not results.** Consensus expects about 31.56 dollars per share. If Micron delivers exactly that, nothing has changed, because that outcome is already in the 1,053 dollar price. The stock moves on the *gap* between what arrives and what was expected, plus what management says about next quarter. This is why a company can report record-breaking numbers and fall 10 percent: it beat last quarter and guided down the next one. That is not the market being irrational. That is the market having already paid for the good news.

**Which brings it back to Troy's best point on August 18** — read the earnings of companies you do not own, because they tell you about demand across the whole chain. He is right, and tomorrow is the cleanest possible test of it. Micron's report will say something about Nvidia, about AMD, about every AI infrastructure name, because they all depend on the same bottleneck. Whatever you think of the stock, the report is worth reading as information about an entire sector.

**And notice what you can do now that you could not in August:** you have a thesis on the record with dates and numbers attached, and a scheduled event that will test it. That is what turns watching a show into actually learning something. Most people never close the loop. You now have one closing tomorrow at 4:30 PM Eastern.
