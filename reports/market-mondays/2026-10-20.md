### The telecom story: what actually happened, and why it is a big deal

This is the most important thing in the episode, and the facts are dramatic.

On October 8, 2026, SpaceX agreed to acquire a nationwide 800 megahertz low-band spectrum portfolio from a firm called Grain Management. Low-band spectrum is the unglamorous, long-range, building-penetrating kind — the foundation of real mobile coverage. SpaceX said the purchase closes a key technical gap in its plan to become a major U.S. mobile carrier. Elon Musk called it "the last critical piece of the spectrum puzzle" for full phone coverage in America.

The market's reaction was violent. On Friday, October 9, Verizon fell roughly 10 percent — its worst single day since 2002. T-Mobile US fell about 13 percent. AT&T fell around 10 percent. Billions in combined market value gone in a session, on an announcement about a company you cannot buy shares in. The stocks recovered somewhat by the following Tuesday.

**Where the three carriers stand now.** AT&T trades around $23.19, with a market capitalization near $159 billion, a price-to-earnings ratio around 7.75, and a dividend yield somewhere between 4.74 and 5.0 percent depending on the source and day. Verizon trades around $44.52, market capitalization near $185 billion, price-to-earnings ratio around 11.60, dividend yield about 6.0 percent. T-Mobile's current quote I could not pin down reliably; as of October 15 it carried a price-to-earnings ratio of 16.4 and a market capitalization near $168 billion.

**The catalysts are immediate.** AT&T reports earnings on October 21 — that is tomorrow. Verizon reports October 26. Both will be asked about Starlink, and what the chief executives say will likely move the stocks more than the quarterly numbers.

**Does the threat hold up?** Honestly, it is contested by serious people on both sides, and that is the most useful thing I can tell you.

For the threat being real: Jeffrey Wlodarczak at Pivotal Research estimated Starlink could capture as much as 13 percent of U.S. wireless market share over ten years. Bank of America expects the carriers to respond by bundling more aggressively, which is itself a margin problem — defending share by cutting effective prices.

Against: AT&T chief executive John Stankey called the low-cost small-cell approach "not a viable strategy." Verizon chief executive Dan Schulman made the more technical argument, that physics limits what satellites can deliver in urban and suburban areas — a satellite beam covers a wide area and everyone under it shares that capacity, which is fine in Montana and useless in Brooklyn at 6 PM.

And two facts that cut against urgency: SpaceX has given no launch date for services using this spectrum, and the deal still needs regulatory approval. The valuations above tell you something too. A price-to-earnings ratio of 7.75 on AT&T is the market saying it already expects very little growth. Disruption risk is partly in the price. What a satellite competitor cannot easily replicate is the enterprise contracts, the fiber, and the spectrum depth in dense cities.

### Micron: the contradiction at the heart of the episode

Micron makes memory chips — DRAM, the working memory in computers and phones, and high-bandwidth memory, the specialized stacked memory that sits beside AI accelerators. High-bandwidth memory is the reason Micron stopped being a boring cyclical and became an AI stock.

**The numbers are genuinely extraordinary.** Fiscal 2026 revenue, for the year ended August 2026, came in at $133.2 billion — up 256.3 percent from fiscal 2025. The most recent quarter, reported September 30, delivered $54.23 billion against estimates near $51.5 billion, which is revenue growth of 379.3 percent year over year. Those are not normal numbers for a company this size.

**Where it trades.** Sources disagree, which tells you how fast it is moving: around $878.84 at the October 16 close, roughly $906 on October 19, about $916 intraday. Market capitalization lands somewhere between $994 billion and $1.04 trillion. The 52-week range is $192.40 to $1,255.00 — so the stock has more than quadrupled off its low, and still sits roughly 27 to 30 percent below its high.

**The valuation is the strange part.** Trailing price-to-earnings sits around 11.7 to 12.4 times. Forward price-to-earnings is *higher*, around 14.7 times. Read that again, because it is the single most informative fact in this report: the market expects Micron to earn **less** next year than it did last year. That is what a forward multiple above a trailing multiple means.

**Next earnings** is listed as December 23, 2026, but that listing looked stale, so confirm it on Micron's investor relations page before relying on it.

**Does the show's framing hold up?** The chapter title "Micron Hype and FOMO" suggests skepticism, and the arithmetic supports the skeptics more than the raw growth numbers do. A price-to-earnings ratio of 12 on a stock that grew revenue 256 percent looks like the bargain of the decade. It is not necessarily, and the next section explains precisely why. The risks are concrete: CXMT's progress in China, the fact that memory pricing is set by industry supply and cycles rather than by Micron, a Taiwan-concentrated supply chain, and a workforce at Micron's Taoyuan site that voted 99 percent to authorize a strike over profit-sharing.

### Fundamentals corner

**Why a cheap-looking price-to-earnings ratio can be the most expensive thing in your portfolio.**

The price-to-earnings ratio is simple: share price divided by earnings per share. If a stock trades at $100 and earned $10 per share, the ratio is 10. The rough intuition is that you are paying ten years of current earnings for the business, so lower generally sounds cheaper.

Now look at Micron. Trailing price-to-earnings around 12. The market overall trades much higher. Revenue grew 256 percent. Every instinct says bargain.

Here is the trap, and it is the most valuable thing this episode can teach you.

That ratio uses the earnings Micron **already made** — a year in which memory prices went vertical because the entire world was buying AI hardware at once. Micron does not set memory prices. The industry's supply and demand balance does. Memory is the textbook **cyclical** business: when prices spike, every producer races to add capacity; capacity takes a couple of years to arrive; then it all arrives at once, prices collapse, and earnings fall off a cliff.

So a cyclical company at the top of its cycle shows you record earnings and therefore a tiny price-to-earnings ratio — at precisely the moment its earnings are most likely to fall. The ratio looks cheapest exactly when the risk is highest. Value investors call this the **peak-earnings trap**, and it has eaten a lot of people who thought they were buying a bargain.

**And you do not have to take my word for it, because the market has already told you.** Remember Micron's forward price-to-earnings of about 14.7 against a trailing figure of about 12.4. The forward number uses *estimated future* earnings. For the forward ratio to be higher than the trailing one, analysts must be forecasting lower earnings ahead. The market is openly pricing a down cycle. The "cheap" multiple is not the market missing the growth — it is the market declining to pay for earnings it does not expect to repeat.

**The practical habit to build:** whenever a price-to-earnings ratio looks surprisingly low, ask two questions before anything else. First, is this a cyclical business — chips, memory, oil, shipping, autos, homebuilders, steel? Second, what is the forward ratio versus the trailing one? If forward is higher than trailing, the market expects earnings to shrink, and your "cheap" stock is priced for a decline that has not shown up in the trailing number yet.

For cyclicals, experienced analysts often compare price to *average* earnings across a full cycle, or to book value, or to free cash flow, rather than to one spectacular year. That is also why the same arithmetic can run the other way: a cyclical at the *bottom* of its cycle shows a frighteningly high price-to-earnings ratio, or none at all, which is sometimes when it is genuinely cheap.

This cuts against the simple version of the lesson — low ratio equals cheap — and that is the point. The ratio is not an answer. It is a question about what the earnings in the denominator are actually worth going forward.
