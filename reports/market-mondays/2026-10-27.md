**Two — the job fires but cannot find the script.** Run this:

`cat ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/launchd-mm.log`

### Fundamentals corner

This episode handed me an unusually clean lesson, so let us do **market capitalization** — and then the thing most people miss about it.

Market capitalization is what the whole company is worth at today's price. The formula is simple:

**market capitalization = share price × number of shares outstanding**

Advanced Micro Devices at roughly $530 a share with about 1.6 billion shares gives you a company worth about $850 billion. That is the number behind the "$1 trillion" headline, and the first useful thing to know is that **market cap tells you size, not value**. A trillion-dollar company is not expensive and a ten-billion-dollar company is not cheap — those are measurements of how big, not how dear. To ask whether a price is reasonable you need to compare it to something the business produces, which is what the price-to-earnings ratio does: price divided by earnings per share. Advanced Micro Devices at a trailing 133 means you are paying $133 for each $1 of last year's profit. That is the number that says "expensive," and the milestone headline says nothing at all.

Now the part most people miss, and the reason I paired these two companies.

Look at the formula again. Market cap has **two** moving parts, and almost everyone only watches the first. The share count can change too — and when a company issues new shares, the pie gets cut into more slices.

That is exactly what Meta Platforms is doing. Raising $97.5 billion partly by selling new stock means new shares come into existence. The company gets the cash, which may well fund something valuable. But each existing share now represents a slightly smaller fraction of the business. That is **dilution**, and it is why you can see a company's market capitalization rise while an individual shareholder's claim gets smaller.

So when you research a company, look up the share count alongside the price, and check whether it is rising over time. A share count that creeps up year after year is a company quietly handing out slices — through stock compensation, or acquisitions, or raises like this one. A share count that falls means buybacks, the opposite. Neither is automatically good or bad: Meta raising money to build something may turn out well or badly, and the share count alone will not tell you which. But a per-share figure — earnings per share, for instance — moves on the denominator as surely as the numerator, and if you only ever watch the price you will miss half of what moved it.

A practical place to see this: on any stock summary page, find "shares outstanding." Compare it to the same figure a year ago. That one comparison tells you whether you are being diluted, and it takes about thirty seconds.
