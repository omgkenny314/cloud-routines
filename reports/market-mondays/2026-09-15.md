### Monday's AI selloff was about safety, not earnings

Over the weekend, Anthropic's chief executive Dario Amodei called for AI developers to slow the pace of frontier model development. OpenAI's Sam Altman and Elon Musk both said they agreed.

Markets took it badly on Monday. The PHLX Semiconductor Index fell almost 6 percent, its worst day since early July. Nvidia (NVDA) fell about 2 percent, Intel (INTC) about 5.7 percent, and Marvell Technology (MRVL) about 6.2 percent. Overseas, SK Hynix closed down more than 6 percent, Samsung Electronics more than 4 percent, and SoftBank — one of OpenAI's largest backers — fell 10 percent in Japan. The big cloud providers, Meta (META), Amazon (AMZN), Alphabet (GOOGL) and Microsoft (MSFT), all drifted lower.

The broad indexes moved far less: the S&P 500 fell 0.48 percent, the Nasdaq 0.56 percent, the Dow 0.29 percent. That gap between a 6 percent move in chips and a half-percent move in the index tells you the selling was concentrated, not general.

One detail worth noticing, because it is the kind of thing that teaches you how markets actually think: **cybersecurity stocks rose** while AI stocks fell. Same news, opposite reactions. If AI safety becomes the story, the companies that sell safety and control get a bigger market, not a smaller one. Money did not leave — it rotated.

Here is the honest analytical point. Not one company's revenue, earnings, or guidance changed over that weekend. What changed was what investors were willing to *pay* for the same expected earnings. That distinction is the whole lesson below.

### Why these two stories are really one story

A rate hike and an AI-safety selloff look unrelated. They hit stocks through the same channel, and the concept below is the channel.

### Fundamentals corner

**The concept: the discount rate, and why "good company" and "good price" are different questions.**

A share of stock is a claim on cash a company will earn in the future. To decide what that claim is worth today, you have to convert future dollars into today's dollars — because a dollar arriving in ten years is worth less than a dollar in your hand now. You could have invested that dollar in the meantime. The rate you use to shrink those future dollars back to today is the **discount rate**, and it moves with interest rates.

The mechanics matter less than one consequence: **the further out a company's profits sit, the more its value depends on the discount rate.**

Think of two companies. One is a utility earning steady money right now. The other is an AI infrastructure company whose big profits are expected in 2032. Raise the discount rate, and the utility's value barely moves, because most of its cash arrives soon. The AI company's value drops hard, because almost all of its value sits in distant years, and those are the years that get shrunk the most.

This is what analysts mean by **duration** in equities. A long-duration stock is one whose value lives far in the future. High-growth, high-multiple technology stocks are the longest-duration assets in the market — which is exactly why they fall hardest when rates rise, and rip hardest when rates fall.

Now look back at this week with that lens. A hiking Fed raises the discount rate, which compresses long-duration valuations. An AI slowdown pushes those distant profits even further out, *and* makes them less certain. Both stories do the same thing: they reduce the present value of far-off earnings. That is why a semiconductor index fell 6 percent in a week when no chip company's earnings changed at all.

**The practical habit to build:** when a stock you follow moves hard, ask which of two things changed — the company's expected earnings, or the price the market will pay for those earnings. Earnings changes come from the business: a product, a contract, a guidance cut. Multiple changes come from outside it: rates, sentiment, risk appetite. Monday was almost purely the second kind.

Learning to tell them apart is most of fundamental analysis. It is also what keeps you from mistaking a repricing for a broken business, or a broken business for a bargain.
