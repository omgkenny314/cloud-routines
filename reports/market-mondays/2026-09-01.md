### Fundamentals corner

Micron sets up this week's concept nicely: **why a low P/E can be a trap in a cyclical company.**

The price-to-earnings ratio is just the share price divided by earnings per share. Micron's is about 21 — $945 divided by $44.31. The usual instinct is that a low P/E means cheap and a high P/E means expensive. For a steady business, that instinct is roughly right.

For a cyclical business, it inverts, and this catches people out.

A cyclical company's profits swing enormously with its industry's boom-and-bust cycle. Micron makes memory chips, and memory is one of the most violently cyclical industries there is. When memory is scarce, prices spike, and because the cost of making a chip barely changes, almost all of that price increase drops to the bottom line. Earnings explode. When supply catches up, prices collapse and earnings can go to zero or negative.

Now watch what the P/E does. At the top of the cycle, earnings are at their absolute peak, so you are dividing by a very large number, and the P/E looks small and tempting. That is precisely the most dangerous moment to buy — not the safest. Investors call this "peak earnings, trough multiple." At the bottom of the cycle, earnings are near zero, the P/E looks absurdly high or is meaningless, and that is often when the stock is actually cheap.

So the question to ask about Micron's P/E of 21 is not "is 21 low?" It is "where in the memory cycle is that $44.31 of earnings coming from?" If memory pricing is near a peak, that 21 is flattering the stock. If the cycle is still early, the number means something different entirely.

The practical habit: for any cyclical company — chips, oil, steel, homebuilders, autos, shipping — do not read the P/E on its own. Ask what the earnings looked like three and five years ago. If they swung wildly, you are looking at a cyclical, and the P/E needs the cycle's context before it tells you anything at all. For steady businesses like consumer staples or utilities, the P/E can be read much more directly.

That single question — "is this a cyclical, and where are we in the cycle?" — will save you from more bad entries than almost any other piece of fundamental analysis.
