## Fixing the relay

I am putting this before the analysis because it is the thing that actually ends this problem, and it is seven weeks overdue.

### I think last week's diagnosis was wrong, and I found something more specific

Last week I concluded the launchd jobs had never fired at all, reasoning from the fact that every transcript commit landed on the wrong day at the wrong hour. That reasoning still looks sound to me, but I have since found something that explains everything just as well and is far more concrete.

**The script in this repository is named `fetch_show_transcript.sh`, with underscores.**

**Both launchd job files call `/Users/kennethalford/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh`, with hyphens.**

Those are different filenames. And the script's own usage line, inside the file, also says hyphens — so hyphens is the intended installed name, and the repository copy is simply named differently.

Here is why that would produce exactly what you have been living with. The job runs `/bin/bash` against that path. If the file at that path does not exist, bash exits immediately with "No such file or directory." It never reaches line one of your script — which means it never reaches the `fail()` function, which means **your push alert never fires.** You built a watchdog that reports every failure path, and this failure happens before the watchdog is even loaded.

That is the signature you have: no transcripts, no alerts, no complaints, for seven weeks. And it explains why August 20 worked — you ran it by hand from the repository path, where the name is correct.

### The one command that settles it

Run this first. It takes ten seconds.

```
ls -l ~/.local/bin/ | grep -i transcript
```

If what comes back has **underscores** — `fetch_show_transcript.sh` — that is the bug, and the fix is below.

If it has hyphens, or nothing comes back at all, read the launchd log instead. **This is the file nobody has looked at yet, and it is decisive:**

```
cat ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/launchd-mm.log
```

- If it is full of repeated "No such file or directory" lines, the jobs **are** firing on schedule and dying on a bad path. That confirms the filename theory and disproves what I told you last week.
- If that file is empty or does not exist, the jobs genuinely are not firing, and last week's `bootout` then `bootstrap` sequence is the right fix after all.

Either way you will know in under a minute, and you will know which of my two diagnoses was right.

### The fix, if it is the filename

```
cp ~/Documents/cloud-routines/fetch_show_transcript.sh ~/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh
chmod +x ~/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh
```

Then force a run instead of waiting a week to find out:

```
launchctl kickstart -p gui/$(id -u)/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-mm
tail -20 ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/fetch.log
```

If `fetch.log` gets fresh lines, the relay is alive. Then do the same `kickstart` for the `-tt` job.

I deliberately did not rename the file in this repository, because this routine only commits the report. But renaming it so the repository and the job files agree is worth doing, so this cannot bite you again the next time you redeploy.

### Fundamentals corner

**Why a low price-to-earnings ratio on a cyclical company can be a warning rather than a bargain.**

Start with the arithmetic, because it is startling and you can check it yourself.

Micron closed October 5 at **1,063.96 dollars**. It earned **75.52 dollars per share** in the fiscal year just reported. Divide the price by the earnings: **1,063.96 divided by 75.52 is about 14.** So Micron trades at roughly **14 times earnings.**

Now do it with the guidance. The company guided to about **38.15 dollars per share next quarter.** If that rate simply held for a year, that is about **152.60 dollars** of annual earnings. **1,063.96 divided by 152.60 is about 7.** Roughly **7 times forward earnings.**

For comparison, the S&P 500 as a whole typically trades somewhere in the low-to-mid twenties. So here is a stock up more than 500 percent in a year, the thirteenth most valuable company on earth, trading at half the market's multiple on trailing earnings and a third of it on forward earnings.

The instinct is to call that absurdly cheap. **Hold that instinct, because this is the exact trap.**

Here is the thing to understand. The price-to-earnings ratio asks: how many years of current profit does it take to pay for the share? That is a reasonable question when profits are reasonably steady — a utility, a grocery chain, a consumer staples business. Those earnings next year will look much like this year's, so dividing by them means something.

**Memory chips are not that kind of business. They are one of the most violently cyclical industries that exists.** Memory is close to a commodity: one manufacturer's DRAM is substitutable for another's. When demand outruns supply, prices spike, and because the factories are already built and largely paid for, almost every extra dollar of revenue falls to the bottom line. That is what an 87 percent gross margin is telling you — these are not normal times. Then the whole industry, seeing those profits, builds more capacity. Supply catches up. Prices fall. And the same factories that printed money now have to be paid for out of much smaller revenue. Margins do not drift down; they collapse. The industry has done this repeatedly, for decades.

So when a deeply cyclical company shows a very low P/E, there are two possible explanations, and you cannot tell them apart from the ratio alone:

1. The market is wrong and the stock is cheap.
2. **The market thinks these earnings are the peak**, and is refusing to pay a normal multiple for profits it expects to shrink.

Professional investors have a name for the second one: **peak earnings.** And the counterintuitive rule that follows is one of the most useful things in all of fundamental analysis — **for a cyclical business, a low P/E often marks the top of the cycle and a high P/E often marks the bottom.** At the bottom, earnings have collapsed toward zero, so dividing by them produces an enormous or meaningless ratio right when the stock is actually cheap. At the top, earnings are inflated, so the ratio looks tiny right when the risk is highest.

**This is also the cleanest explanation for why Micron barely moved on a blowout quarter.** The company told the market its profits are enormous and getting more enormous. The market said, in effect, we believe you about this quarter, and we still are not paying twenty times for it, because we do not believe that number is durable. A stock price is not a verdict on how a company is doing. It is a verdict on how a company is doing *relative to what was already expected* — and more importantly, on what comes after.

**What to do with this, practically.** Whenever you see a P/E that looks too good to be true, ask one question first: **is this business cyclical?** If it is — memory chips, oil, shipping, steel, homebuilding, autos, airlines — then the P/E alone is nearly useless and can be actively misleading. Look instead at where you are in the cycle. Are margins unusually high or unusually low versus the company's own history? Is the industry adding capacity? Is anyone warning about oversupply? Those questions tell you far more than the ratio does.

And notice how much this reframes Ian's "where you buy, not what you buy." For a cyclical, that is not merely good discipline — it is the whole game. Buy a great cyclical business at the wrong point in its cycle and you can be right about the company for years and still lose money. Which, incidentally, is exactly why he was waiting for 820 dollars.
