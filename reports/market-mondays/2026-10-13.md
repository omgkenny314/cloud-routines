### Micron Technology (MU) — the thesis held up on fundamentals, and the stock still fell

**What it does:** Micron makes memory chips — DRAM, the working memory in computers and servers, and NAND flash storage. The piece that matters now is high-bandwidth memory, or HBM: specialised memory stacked physically beside artificial-intelligence accelerators. An Nvidia training system needs enormous quantities of it, and only a few companies can make it.

**Where it trades:** $989.04 at the October 12 close, down about 3.9% that day. Market capitalisation falls somewhere between $1.12 trillion and $1.17 trillion depending on source. The 52-week high is $1,255.00 — so the stock sits roughly **21% below its high**. For context, Micron first crossed a $1 trillion market value on May 26, 2026, when it rose 19% in a single session.

**What the fundamentals actually say.** Micron reported fiscal fourth-quarter results on September 30 and they are, by any normal standard, remarkable:

Quarterly revenue of **$54.23 billion** against roughly $50.5 billion expected. Adjusted earnings of **$33.42 per share** against roughly $31.16 expected.

Full-year revenue of **$133.2 billion, up 256%** year over year. Gross margin of **81.1%**. Full-year earnings per share of **$75.52, up 811%**.

DRAM revenue of a record **$39.8 billion**, which is 73% of the whole company. The data-centre business alone did about **$18 billion** in the quarter.

Guidance for the next quarter of roughly **$61.5 billion** in revenue and **$38.15** in earnings per share — one transcript gives the revenue range as $60 billion to $63 billion. Management also said HBM shipments should outgrow conventional DRAM, and flagged significant price increases for 2027 along with what the chief executive called the industry's first custom HBM implementation, done with Nvidia.

**So why is the stock 21% off its high?** Two reasons I can verify. Shares slipped after the report as investors weighed heavy capital-spending plans against the strong outlook — building memory capacity is enormously expensive, and that spending lands before the revenue does. And gross-margin guidance of about 86.25% was read by at least one analyst as the single soft spot in an otherwise flawless quarter, which tells you how extraordinary expectations have become when an 86% gross margin counts as disappointing.

**Does the show's thesis hold up?** On fundamentals, emphatically yes — anyone bullish on Micron for HBM reasons has been vindicated by the numbers. But I want to be precise rather than flattering, because the gap between the two is the whole lesson. **A thesis can be right about the business and still not pay you on your timeline.** The business delivered 256% revenue growth; the stock is a fifth below its high. I could not find a verified August 18 closing price this week, so **I am not going to quote you a percentage return on Ian's August call** — I would rather leave a hole than fill it with a number I cannot stand behind.

**Main risks:** memory is the most violently cyclical business in semiconductors. Prices are set by the balance between industry capacity and demand, and capacity is being added right now across the industry — including by Micron itself. The heavy capital spending that funds the boom is also what has historically ended it. Next earnings are listed as December 23, 2026 by one source, and as unavailable by another, so treat that date as unconfirmed.

**What the "Broadcom Custom" chapter is really about, and why it is the most useful distinction in the episode.** Nvidia sells a general-purpose product: a graphics processing unit that any customer can buy and program. Broadcom does something structurally different — it co-designs *custom* chips for individual giant customers, built for one specific workload. The industry term is an application-specific integrated circuit, or ASIC.

The investment consequence is concrete. Nvidia's risk is that its biggest customers are also its most motivated competitors: every large cloud company would rather design its own chip than keep paying Nvidia's margins. Broadcom's business *is* helping them do that. So the two are not simply faster and slower horses in the same race — to a meaningful degree, Broadcom wins business precisely when Nvidia's customers defect. Notice also that Nvidia is roughly three times Broadcom's size yet trades at a *lower* trailing multiple, which is the opposite of what people usually assume.

### Fundamentals corner — the price-to-earnings ratio, and why its bottom half decides everything

Last week I taught you why great earnings do not always move a cyclical stock. This week Micron hands us the mechanical explanation, and it starts with the most quoted number in investing.

**The price-to-earnings ratio is just division.** Share price divided by earnings per share. Micron closed at $989.04 and earned $75.52 per share over its fiscal year, so $989.04 divided by $75.52 is **about 13.1**. You would say Micron trades at roughly 13 times earnings. The intuitive reading is "I pay $13 for each $1 of annual profit," and roughly speaking, that is right.

**Now here is the thing that will confuse you with VoiceOver, so I want you to meet it on purpose.** Two reputable sites gave Micron two different ratios this week: one said about 13.8 times, another said 23.34 times. Neither is wrong. **They are dividing by different earnings.**

The price is unambiguous — it is whatever the last trade was. The *earnings* are a choice. A site can use the last four reported quarters, or the last full fiscal year, or an estimate of the next twelve months. For a company whose profits grew 811% in a year, those choices give wildly different answers, because a four-quarter window that still includes older, weaker quarters produces a much smaller denominator than one made purely of recent blowout quarters. Small denominator, big ratio.

**So the habit to build is this:** whenever you see a price-to-earnings ratio, ask *which earnings*. "Trailing" means already reported and in the past. "Forward" means analysts' estimates of the future. You saw this twice already in this report without me pointing at it — Broadcom at 45 trailing but 21 forward, and Eaton quoted at both 43.72 and 34.76 by two sources using different earnings measures. Same companies, same day, different denominators.

**And now the part that actually matters for Micron.** Management guided to about $38.15 in earnings per share for the *next quarter alone*. If the company earned anything near four times that over a year, a $989 share price would be something like six times forward earnings. Against Nvidia's roughly 28.6 times trailing, Micron looks absurdly cheap.

**It almost certainly is not.** A very low forward multiple on a cyclical company is not the market saying "bargain." It is the market saying **"we do not believe these earnings last."** Memory chip prices are set by supply against demand, the whole industry is adding supply right now, and when that supply arrives prices fall and those enormous margins compress. The market is pricing peak earnings and quietly forecasting the decline on the other side.

This is why a low price-to-earnings ratio is not automatically cheap and a high one not automatically expensive. **The ratio is a sentence with two halves, and the bottom half — which earnings, and are they sustainable — carries nearly all the meaning.** A 13 times multiple on earnings about to halve is more expensive than a 28 times multiple on earnings about to double. Learning to interrogate the denominator is most of what separates reading a stock screener from understanding one.

## Fixing the relay — week eight

I checked the specific bug I identified last week, and **the filename mismatch is real.** I verified it directly in the repository this morning:

The script in the repository is named `fetch_show_transcript.sh`, with **underscores**. Both launchd job files invoke `/Users/kennethalford/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh`, with **hyphens**. The script's own internal usage line also says hyphens, so hyphens is the intended installed name.

That remains the best explanation for eight silent weeks: bash exits immediately on a path that does not exist, before your script's failure-alert function is ever loaded. A watchdog that never runs cannot report that it did not run.

**The one command that settles it, which takes ten seconds:**

```
ls -l ~/.local/bin/ | grep -i transcript
```

If the result shows **underscores**, that is the bug. If it shows hyphens or nothing at all, read the log instead — this file is still the decisive piece of evidence nobody has looked at:

```
cat ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/launchd-mm.log
```

Repeated "No such file or directory" lines mean the jobs are firing and dying on a bad path. An empty or missing file means they are not firing at all, and the `bootout` then `bootstrap` sequence is the right fix.

**The fix, if it is the filename:**

```
cp ~/Documents/cloud-routines/fetch_show_transcript.sh ~/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh
chmod +x ~/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh
launchctl kickstart -p gui/$(id -u)/com.kennethalford.show-transcript-mm
tail -20 ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/fetch.log
```

Fresh lines in `fetch.log` mean the relay is alive. Then do the same `kickstart` for the `-tt` job.

I confirmed it directly: the proxy returns 403 for all of them, and `yt-dlp` installs fine but cannot connect. Web *search* still works, which is the only reason this report exists.
