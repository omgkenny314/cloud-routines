### Micron Technology (MU) — the memory story

**What the company does.** Micron makes memory chips: DRAM, which is the working memory a computer uses while it is running, and NAND flash, which is storage. The part that matters for AI is high bandwidth memory, or HBM — specialised stacked DRAM that sits next to an AI processor and feeds it data. An AI chip is only as fast as the memory keeping it fed, which is why a memory company ended up at the centre of the AI trade.

**Where it trades and what just happened.** Snapshots taken during the November 9 session put Micron around 731 to 737 dollars a share, up roughly 7 percent on the day, with a market capitalisation of about 825 to 833 billion dollars. I want to flag that these are intraday snapshots from different sources captured at different moments, not a confirmed closing price, so treat the exact figure as approximate. Four days earlier the stock had fallen about 17 percent in one session to 656.39, part of a selloff that knocked memory names down as much as 20 percent. The 52-week range is 192.59 to 1,255.00 dollars. Read that range again, because it tells you more about this stock than any single price does: it has traded at six times its low and it currently sits well below its high.

**The fundamentals I could verify.** Trailing earnings per share of roughly 74 dollars, giving a trailing price-to-earnings ratio of about 9.8 to 9.9. DRAM industry revenue reportedly grew 369 percent as memory prices rose. The research firm TrendForce estimated conventional DRAM contract prices rising 58 to 63 percent quarter over quarter. Micron's HBM4 capacity was reported sold out through 2026, and Morgan Stanley has characterised the shortage as structural rather than a passing squeeze.

**Does the bull case hold up?** The demand side is real and verifiable: prices are up enormously, capacity is sold out, and the earnings have actually arrived rather than being promised. That is a genuinely strong position, and it is not hype. The risk is equally real and is the oldest risk in this industry. Memory is a commodity. When prices spike, every producer builds more capacity, and capacity arriving all at once is what ends memory cycles — violently. Analysts covering the name warn directly that if memory markets normalise, earnings could fall a long way from here. The November 5 drop was the market rehearsing that fear. A second risk surfaced in the same week and is specific to this moment: if AI data centre construction slows, the demand for HBM slows with it.

**One caution on sources.** Several pages I found making dramatic claims about Micron — one asserting the stock rose over 700 percent in 2026, another projecting a trillion-dollar-plus valuation — are promotional in tone, and one contained two different share prices on the same page. I did not use their numbers and neither should you. The figures above come from quote providers and named research firms.

### Nvidia (NVDA) — a fall that was not about the business

**What happened.** Nvidia closed November 9 at 178.67 dollars, down 4.27 percent from a prior close of 186.64, on volume of roughly 455 million shares — about 3.7 times its three-month average. It fell on a day the Nasdaq rose nearly 3 percent, which is unusual and worth understanding.

The cause was not earnings, guidance, or chip demand. Nvidia's board of directors opened an independent investigation into reports that chief executive Jensen Huang had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The board stated it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised.

**Why this is worth separating out.** This is a governance and key-person event, not a business event. Nothing about it changes how many chips Nvidia sold last quarter. But Nvidia is unusually tied to one person's judgement and relationships, and markets price uncertainty about a chief executive separately from uncertainty about a product. The honest read is that nobody yet knows whether this is a headline that fades or something with consequences, and a one-day 4 percent move on 3.7 times normal volume is the market saying exactly that: high disagreement, no resolution.

**The catalyst ahead.** Nvidia reports fiscal third-quarter 2027 results on **Tuesday, November 17, 2026, after the close** — described as confirmed by one earnings calendar and still listed as estimated by another, so worth verifying before you rely on the date. The company has guided to roughly 108 billion dollars of revenue for the quarter, and analyst consensus sits near 2.49 dollars of earnings per share. That report lands eight days after the episode, and with the AI trade this jumpy it is the most consequential single event on the near calendar.

### The neocloud group — IREN, TeraWulf, Nebius and CoreWeave

The show has previously paired these off against each other, IREN against TeraWulf and Nebius against CoreWeave, so here is where they genuinely stand.

**What they do.** These are "neoclouds" — companies that build and rent out data centres full of AI chips to anyone who needs computing power. Several, including IREN and TeraWulf, started as bitcoin miners and converted, because they already owned the two scarce things: buildings and electrical power.

**What happened to them.** This group has been in a sustained decline since October, and it accelerated after the midterms. Over roughly the month to October 22, CoreWeave fell 35.25 percent, Nebius Group fell 29.46 percent and IREN fell 45.92 percent. On November 5, CoreWeave was down 10.60 percent at 43.29 dollars, Nebius down 13.77 percent at 122.04, and IREN down 15.04 percent at 18.45. These are November 5 levels and will have moved since.

**The three reasons, which matter more than the numbers.** First, debt — these companies borrow heavily to build, which magnifies gains and losses alike. Second, a customer concentration problem made visible: Microsoft's capital spending came in below expectations in late October, and a report said delays at IREN's Childress site led Microsoft to push planned spending into a later quarter. When one customer's budget decides your revenue, that customer's hesitation is your problem. Third, and most interesting, politics became a direct input. A report that Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa had pledged a data centre moratorium hit the whole group, with one analysis estimating that a Texas policy shift could threaten 27 percent of planned United States data centre power. TeraWulf fell about 13 percent on November 4 on that.

**Catalysts immediately ahead.** CoreWeave reports November 10 and Nebius reports November 12 — both within days of the episode. Analysts are split on whether the selloff overshot, with one argument holding that Nebius has the better recovery case while CoreWeave's depends on margin improvement showing up on schedule. I found little current data on TeraWulf beyond its November 4 drop and cannot tell you where it stands now.

**The honest read.** The thing to understand about this group is that it is a leveraged bet on other companies' spending plans. That is a legitimate way to invest, but it is a different and more fragile proposition than owning the chipmaker or the hyperscaler, and the last month demonstrated the difference in the hardest available way. Note also that the big hyperscalers held up far better than the neoclouds through the same selloff. Same theme, very different durability.

### Fundamentals corner

**Why a low price-to-earnings ratio is not the same thing as cheap.**

The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, is the simplest valuation tool there is. Take the share price, divide by earnings per share, and you get the number of years of current profits you are paying for one share. A P/E of 20 means paying twenty dollars for each dollar of annual profit. Lower looks cheaper. That is the instinct, and this week offers a textbook example of why the instinct can be exactly backwards.

Micron trades around 733 dollars with trailing earnings per share of about 74, so its P/E is about 9.9. The S&P 500 as a whole typically trades somewhere in the high teens to low twenties. So on this measure Micron looks like it is trading at half the market's valuation while sitting at the centre of the biggest technology boom in a generation. That sounds like a glaring bargain. Here is why experienced investors look at that number and get nervous instead.

**The word "trailing" is doing the heavy lifting.** Trailing means the last twelve months of profits that have already happened. Micron just lived through a period when memory prices rose 58 to 63 percent in a single quarter. Those extraordinary prices produced extraordinary profits, and that 74 dollars of earnings per share reflects the best conditions the memory industry has seen. The P/E is low not because the price is low — the stock is up enormously — but because the earnings are at a peak.

**This is the cyclical trap, and it has a shape.** Commodity businesses like memory move in cycles: shortage, high prices, enormous profits, everyone builds capacity, glut, collapsing prices, losses. At the top of that cycle, the P/E looks its cheapest precisely because earnings are at their maximum — right at the moment the earnings are most likely to fall. For a cyclical company, a single-digit P/E at the top of the cycle is often a warning rather than an invitation. At the bottom of the cycle the opposite happens: earnings go to nearly zero, and the P/E becomes enormous or meaningless, which is often when the stock is genuinely cheap. The ratio is most misleading at exactly the moments you most want guidance.

**A detail worth noticing.** One quote provider lists Micron's forward P/E — the same calculation using next year's estimated earnings — at about 4.19. A forward P/E lower than the trailing P/E means analysts expect earnings to keep climbing, in this case to roughly double. That may well happen. But notice what you are now relying on: not a fact about profits already earned, but a forecast. The low number no longer describes the company's history, it describes someone's prediction. And that particular figure is low enough that the quote provider's own page flagged it as worth double-checking, which is good practice to copy.

**What to do with this.** Three habits are worth building. First, always ask whether a P/E is trailing or forward, because they answer different questions. Second, ask where the company is in its cycle before treating a low P/E as a discount — for a steady business like a utility or a consumer staple a low P/E often is a discount, but for memory chips, shipping, oil, or homebuilders, it frequently signals the opposite. Third, only ever compare a P/E to companies in the same industry at the same point in the cycle. Comparing Micron's 9.9 to the broad market's twenty-ish tells you nothing useful, because one is a commodity cyclical at peak earnings and the other is five hundred businesses averaged together.

The 52-week range makes the point better than any ratio can. A company whose shares traded between 192 and 1,255 dollars within one year is not a stable, predictable business that the market has mistakenly marked down. It is a business whose profits swing violently — and a single number summarising one year of its profits cannot tell you which part of the swing you are standing in.
