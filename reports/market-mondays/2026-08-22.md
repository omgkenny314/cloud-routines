## The deeper dive

Everything below is context and verification, not a recommendation. Nothing here tells you to buy or sell anything.

### Micron — the thesis mostly holds, and it is the most crowded trade in the report

Micron makes DRAM and NAND memory. The part that matters for the AI story is high-bandwidth memory, or HBM, the stacked memory that sits next to an AI accelerator and feeds it data.

The prices the hosts quoted check out. Micron closed at $974.33 on Thursday, August 20, up 3.97 percent on the day, and traded around $964 on August 21 with a market capitalization above $1 trillion. The all-time closing high was $1,213.37 on June 25, 2026 — Troy said $1,255, which is close to the intraday June peak but slightly above the closing high. The stock sat near $892 on August 5, so the "recovery" Troy was describing was real and recent.

The fundamentals genuinely are extraordinary. Revenue went from $6.8 billion in fiscal Q3 2024 to $23.9 billion in fiscal Q2 2026, roughly 196 percent year-over-year growth, with a record gross margin near 74.9 percent. Analyst consensus for the coming quarter is around $19.72 in earnings per share on roughly $34.5 billion of revenue. Troy's claim that Micron is undervalued on a P/E basis is defensible in the narrow sense: at roughly $970 against high-teens quarterly earnings, the trailing multiple is not stretched by mega-cap tech standards. But that is exactly the trap in a cyclical business — more on that below.

Troy's HBM supply claim also checks out: Micron's HBM capacity is sold out through calendar 2026 with price and volume already agreed, including HBM4, and demand is estimated to exceed supply by 50 to 67 percent.

The Lutnick catalyst is real. On August 15, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick told Apple the administration opposes buying memory from Chinese suppliers CXMT and YMTC on national security grounds. Micron had been lobbying for exactly that outcome. One correction to the show's framing: reporting notes there is no actual rule barring Apple from buying those chips — this is pressure, not regulation — and the Senate set an August 21 deadline for Apple to disclose its position. So the catalyst is more contingent than the episode made it sound.

The risks the episode underweighted. First, this is a cyclical commodity business having a historic upcycle; the multiple looks cheap precisely because the market is pricing in that earnings will not stay at this level forever. That is the classic memory-stock trap. Second, Michael Burry is not only short Nebius — he is also short Micron and Oracle, and his stated thesis is that AI infrastructure has pulled years of demand into a compressed window and excess compute capacity could arrive by 2028. Ian dismissed Burry by pointing at the Nebius short, which is genuinely underwater after Nebius posted 454 percent revenue growth — but that does not address the Micron argument, and presenting one broken short as a reason to ignore the whole bear case is weaker reasoning than it sounded.

Third, and most importantly for timing: on August 18, the day after this episode aired, a Wall Street Journal report on roughly $3 trillion of off-balance-sheet AI commitments sent memory stocks down hard — SanDisk about 9 percent, Micron about 7 percent, Western Digital about 5 percent. That is the same chart Ian showed on this episode as a reason for concern. He identified the risk correctly and then the sector traded on it the next day.

Upcoming catalyst: Nvidia reports Q2 fiscal 2027 on Wednesday, August 26, 2026, after the close, with guidance around $91 billion in revenue. Troy is right that it is a sector-wide event.

### SanDisk — the numbers are real and so is the volatility

SanDisk was spun out of Western Digital and makes NAND flash and solid-state storage. It closed at $1,600.62 on August 20 against a previous close of $1,568.87, with a 52-week range of $45.63 to $2,354.39 — that range alone tells you what kind of ride this has been. The all-time closing high was $2,335.00 on June 25, 2026, the same day Micron peaked. So Troy's "1,700, probably opens at 1,800 tomorrow" was in the right neighborhood on Monday night, and the stock has since come down.

The business case is strong: roughly $20.2 billion of fiscal 2026 revenue with profit margins around 77 percent, driven by the memory shortage. Analyst consensus is a Buy with an average 12-month target near $2,126, but the spread runs from $1,000 to $3,600, which is an honest signal that nobody is confident.

Where the show was hyping rather than arguing: the SanDisk-versus-Seagate segment was explicitly a preference contest — Troy said outright "we made too much history together, I can't turn my back, I'm just loyal," and Ian said he was "splitting hairs." That is fandom, not a thesis, and Troy's own point that Seagate has had the better year on the NAND side actually cuts against his own pick. Treat that segment as entertainment.

Also worth knowing: SanDisk had a secondary share offering that stalled the stock, and it entered August up roughly 653 percent year-to-date. A stock that has done that can fall a long way on nothing.

### LYTE — get the ticker right, and know what is actually inside it

The Roundhill Photonics and Optics ETF began trading August 6, 2026 at $25.73. **The ticker is LYTE, not LYT.** If you search LYT you will not find this fund. Ian's stated entry level of $21.39 is roughly 17 percent below the opening price, so he is waiting for a meaningful pullback, not calling it a buy at current levels.

### Fundamentals corner

**Off-balance-sheet obligations — the thing Ian's chart was actually showing.**

When you look up a company's fundamentals, you see a balance sheet: assets on one side, liabilities on the other. Debt shows up there. Lease liabilities show up there. It feels like the complete picture of what a company owes.

It is not. There is a category called purchase commitments — binding agreements to buy something in the future that has not been delivered yet. If Microsoft signs a contract today promising to buy $228 billion of data-center capacity over the next several years, that is a real obligation, but because nothing has changed hands yet, it does not appear as a liability on the balance sheet. It appears in the footnotes to the financial statements, in a section usually titled "Commitments and Contingencies."

That is the entire point of the chart Ian pulled up. Microsoft at $228 billion, Meta at $349 billion, Alphabet at $811 billion, Amazon at $130 billion — those numbers are large enough to change how you read every one of those companies, and none of them are in the number a stock screener shows you as "total debt."

Why this matters practically. If you look at Alphabet's balance sheet and conclude the company has modest leverage, you may be right about the balance sheet and wrong about the company. And if those commitments were made at 2026 prices to meet 2026 demand — which is Michael Burry's whole argument — then a slowdown does not just reduce future revenue. It leaves a company legally obligated to keep paying for capacity it no longer needs.

How to find them yourself. Pull the company's most recent 10-K or 10-Q from the SEC's EDGAR database at sec.gov, and search the document for "Commitments and Contingencies" or "purchase obligations." There is usually a table showing amounts due within one year, one to three years, three to five years, and beyond. Compare that total to the debt figure on the balance sheet. For the AI hyperscalers right now, the footnote number can be larger than the balance-sheet number.

The general lesson, which outlives this particular AI cycle: the balance sheet tells you what a company owes today, and the footnotes tell you what it has promised to owe tomorrow. Analysts who only read the first number get surprised by the second one. That is precisely what happened on August 18, the day after this episode, when a Wall Street Journal story about $3 trillion of these commitments knocked 5 to 9 percent off the memory stocks the show had just spent an hour recommending.

Ian showed you the risk and then the market traded on it twenty-four hours later. That is a good week to remember the footnotes exist.
