## The deeper dive

**This section is my own research, not the show's.** Every number here I verified against current reporting this morning, and I flag where sources disagree or where I could not confirm something. This is education and context so you can do your own work on these names. It is not advice to buy or sell anything, and I am not telling you what to do with any of it.

### First, the honest answer to the episode's own question

The episode asks whether the bubble is bursting. The verifiable facts say something more specific and more useful than yes or no: **the air came out of one part of the market, hard, while the rest barely moved.**

In October 2026, the S&P 500 — the broad index of large American companies — fell just under one percent. Over the same month the Nasdaq 100, which is far more concentrated in big technology, fell more than six and a half percent. And the semiconductor index, the narrowest and most AI-exposed slice of all, fell more than fifteen percent. Meanwhile defensive sectors, meaning consumer staples and health care, actually rose.

Read those four numbers together and they tell you this was not a market-wide crash. It was a rotation out of one theme. The further a sector sat from artificial intelligence, the better it did. That distinction is the whole thing, and it is the part a bubble-or-not framing tends to lose.

For where things stand now: the S&P 500 was around 7,621 at midday on November 2, up roughly eleven percent for the year to date, and its intraday record high of 7,844.52 was set on October 6. So the broad market is a few percent off a record high it set a month ago. That is not what the inside of a bursting bubble looks like. The AI-linked names are a different story, below.

I will also pass on the dissent, because it is reasonable. Historians of past bubbles are not convinced this qualifies yet. William Quinn, who studies bubble history, has argued that a fall in AI-linked stocks is not the same thing as a bubble bursting, on the grounds that those stocks have largely tracked real earnings rather than floating free of them. That is a serious argument and it cuts against the alarmed framing.

### Why the telecom comparison is the sharp one

The episode's framing — telecom bubble rather than dot-com bubble — is a genuinely better analogy, and it is worth understanding why.

The dot-com bust of 2000 is remembered for companies with no revenue and no business. That is the easy, comforting comparison, because today's AI leaders plainly have enormous revenue. If that is the test, there is no bubble.

But alongside the dot-com bust there was a second, quieter collapse: the telecom buildout. Those companies were not fake. They had real revenue, real customers, and real assets — they laid immense amounts of fiber optic cable in anticipation of internet demand. The demand did eventually arrive. The problem was that it arrived years later than the debt payments did. The cable was real, the need for it was real, and the companies that built it still went bankrupt, because they borrowed against a timeline that slipped.

That is a much more uncomfortable comparison for today, and a much more precise one. The question it asks is not "is this real" but "is this financed on a timeline that can survive the demand arriving late." Hold that thought, because it is exactly what the credit market numbers below are saying.

### The neocloud companies: IREN, TeraWulf, Nebius, CoreWeave

These are the companies that build and rent out AI computing capacity — the "neoclouds." They are the most leveraged expression of the AI trade, and they were the epicenter of October's damage.

For the month of October: CoreWeave fell about thirty-six percent, IREN about thirty-two percent, and Nebius about twenty-seven percent. As of November 2, IREN had dropped a further twenty-three percent over the prior week. For rough reference points, on October 22 CoreWeave traded around fifty-six dollars, Nebius around one hundred sixty dollars, and IREN around twenty-five dollars. Those are snapshots from a fast-moving tape, not current quotes — check live prices before relying on any of them. I could not find reliable figures for TeraWulf at all, so I am not going to give you any.

Now the part that actually matters, and it is the clearest example of fundamental analysis in this whole report. **The selloff started in the credit market, not the stock market.** CoreWeave's unsecured bonds have been yielding close to sixteen percent, and its credit default swaps — contracts that pay out if the company fails to pay its debts — have traded above one thousand basis points. Both of those are distress levels. In plain terms: the people lending CoreWeave money began demanding to be paid like they might not get it back.

That is a signal worth learning to watch, because bond investors and stock investors are looking at the same company and asking different questions. Stock investors ask how big it could get. Bond investors ask whether it can make its payments. When the bond market panics first, it is usually asking about the timeline — and the timeline is precisely what killed the telecom companies.

Two more pieces of context. Microsoft's capital spending came in below expectations in late October, which the market read as its infrastructure partners taking longer to deliver capacity — bad news for companies whose whole business is delivering that capacity. And the research firm Gartner has forecast that up to eighty percent of neoclouds could cease operating independently by 2030. Treat that as one firm's projection about an industry structure, not a prophecy about any single name.

Not everyone reads the drop as fundamental. Tal Liani at Bank of America called the selloff sentiment-driven. Analyst opinion is genuinely split, which is a fair reason to be humble about it.

**The main risk here, stated plainly:** these are capital-hungry businesses financed substantially with debt, selling capacity to a small number of very large customers. If those customers slow down, or if borrowing gets more expensive, the debt payments do not slow down with them. That is the structural risk, and it is the same shape as the telecom story.

### Micron Technology (MU) and the memory story

Micron makes memory chips — DRAM, the working memory in computers, and high-bandwidth memory, the specialized kind that AI accelerators need. The show returns to Micron constantly, so it is worth knowing where it stands.

Recent price readings cluster in the eight hundred thirty to eight hundred eighty-five dollar range: one source has it closing at $884.74, a November 2 snapshot shows about $833.59, down nearly six percent. Verify live before relying on either.

The business case is genuinely strong and specific. Micron guided fourth-quarter DRAM contract prices up about ten to fifteen percent from the prior quarter. Its chief executive said supply would be "much tighter" in fiscal 2027 and 2028 than in 2026. Its entire 2026 high-bandwidth memory supply is reported sold out with pricing already agreed, its HBM4 product is shipping in high volume, and the next generation is targeted for volume production in calendar 2027. On market share it runs roughly third in high-bandwidth memory at about twenty-four percent of projected 2026 supply, behind SK Hynix near forty-three percent and Samsung near thirty-three percent.

**The risk that is easy to miss:** Intel warned that memory costs are hurting personal computer sales. About a quarter of Micron's revenue comes from mobile and client devices — phones and PCs. So the same high memory prices that make the AI side of Micron's business lucrative are actively suppressing demand on the consumer side. High prices are not purely good news for Micron, and that nuance is usually absent from the bull case.

On analyst price targets, the spread is wide — one model shows about one thousand twenty-six dollars over twelve months, a Wedbush note cited one thousand three hundred. Both sit above the recent range. Price targets are opinions with a number attached; the reasoning matters and the number does not.

### On the midterms, since the show tied its picks to them

The historical pattern is real and it is encouraging. Since 1950 the S&P 500 has averaged a gain of about sixteen point six percent in the year following a midterm election, according to BNY. Fidelity notes the index has posted gains in the twelve months after midterms about ninety-five percent of the time since 1938.

Two honest cautions about leaning on that. First, those are averages across a small number of events — roughly nineteen midterms since 1950 — and a strong average built from few cases is a weaker guide than it sounds. Second, the consensus among strategists is that the election matters much less for the overall market than headlines suggest. Citi has called it a "non-event" for S&P 500 fundamentals, with effects showing up in specific sectors rather than in the index. That fits the data above: the index barely moved in October while individual sectors moved enormously.

### Fundamentals corner

**Free cash flow, and why Meta's going negative was the real news**

This episode surfaces free cash flow more naturally than any concept I could pick, so let us do it properly.

Start with profit. Profit — net income — is an accounting measure. It includes judgments: how fast to spread the cost of a building over its useful life, what a convertible note is worth now versus last quarter. Those judgments are legitimate, but they mean profit can be large while the bank balance shrinks, or deeply negative while cash piles up. Anthropic is the textbook illustration: an eighty billion dollar loss that is mostly a non-cash revaluation. Almost no money moved.

Free cash flow asks a blunter question: **after paying to run the business and paying for the equipment and buildings it needs, how much actual cash is left over?**

Roughly: take the cash the operations generated, subtract what was spent on physical assets — the capital expenditure. What remains is free cash flow. It is the money genuinely available to pay down debt, buy back shares, pay dividends, or simply hold.

Why it matters so much right now: free cash flow is the number that is hard to dress up. You can take a non-cash charge against profit and shrug, correctly, that no money left the building. You cannot do that with free cash flow. If it is negative, cash really did leave.

So when Meta reported free cash flow of about negative two point one two billion dollars, that was not an accounting quirk. Here is one of the most profitable businesses in the world, with enormous operating cash flow, spending so heavily on AI infrastructure — one hundred thirty-five to one hundred forty-five billion dollars guided for the year — that the spending consumed everything the operations produced, and then some. The stock fell seven percent on that, and it fell for a reason.

Watch what followed, because this is the lesson inside the lesson. Negative free cash flow means the money has to come from somewhere else: borrowing, or selling shares. Meta did both — a twenty to twenty-five billion dollar bond sale in early November. And the credit market responded: S&P cut its outlook to negative, and its credit default swaps hit record highs. Same sequence as the neoclouds, just at a company strong enough to absorb it.

That is the chain to carry with you: **heavy capital spending turns free cash flow negative, negative free cash flow forces outside financing, outside financing raises the question of whether lenders will stay comfortable.** When you understand that chain, the credit-market signals stop being obscure trivia and start being the earliest warning available. The bond market was nervous about the neoclouds well before the stocks broke.

And it connects straight back to the telecom comparison. The telecom companies did not fail because the fiber was worthless. They failed because the obligations came due on a fixed schedule and the revenue showed up late. Free cash flow is how you watch that gap open in real time.

**How to use this on any company you research.** Find operating cash flow and capital expenditure — both sit in the cash flow statement. Subtract the second from the first. Then ask three questions. Is it positive or negative? If negative, is it negative because the business is weak or because it is deliberately investing hard, as with Meta? And if it is investing hard, who is funding the gap, and on what terms? That last question is the one most people skip, and it is the one that mattered most in October 2026.

One caution so you are not caught out: definitions of free cash flow vary between sources, mainly over how they treat share-based compensation and leases. When you compare two companies, make sure the number came from the same place and was calculated the same way.

To check whether the job is loaded at all:

`launchctl list | grep show-transcript`

If that returns nothing, the launchd jobs are not loaded, and the fix is the two `launchctl bootstrap` commands in this repository's README.

To check whether the job runs but cannot find the script:

`cat ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/launchd-mm.log`

This remains my best guess, and it is worth checking first. The launchd job calls `/Users/kennethalford/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh`, spelled with **hyphens**. The copy in this repository is named `fetch_show_transcript.sh`, with **underscores**. If it was ever deployed with a plain copy, the deployed filename has underscores, launchd is calling a name that does not exist, bash exits immediately, and no error handler ever runs — which would explain twelve weeks of silence exactly. Confirm with:

`ls -l ~/.local/bin/fetch-show-transcript.sh`

If that says "No such file," the entire fix is one rename.

To check whether the script runs, fails, and the alert itself is broken:

`tail -40 ~/.local/state/cloud-routines-relay-logs/fetch.log`

If that log has recent entries, the script is running and the problem is in the push notification helper instead.
